 Abstract
Contemporary programs toward artificial general intelligence (AGI) are organized, in their
overwhelming majority, around a closed evaluation cycle: specify, build, score against a
benchmark, and respecify. This monograph develops the FDF-AGI framework, which
articulates design fiction, design thinking, and Feyerabend's epistemological anarchism into
a single research cycle, and defends the thesis that gives the framework its content: a
benchmark-closed score is not a sufficient statistic for the distributive properties of a
system. The claim is formalized as Proposition 1, the construction that realizes it is
exhibited, and a minimal model shows that the insufficiency is sharp - with a single group
the score is sufficient and the kernel of blind directions has dimension zero, whereas with
|G| groups the kernel has dimension exactly |G| - 1. Six experiments, reproducible from a
fixed repository command, quantify the blindness: a redistribution that leaves the score
invariant can move group outcomes by up to 0.473 while the score drifts by no more than
1.1 × 10-16, and the ratio between a minority group's loss and the majority's gain equals
πrich/πpoor exactly, reaching 600 for a group of 0.1%. The monograph also reports a
bibliographic audit of its own sources, conducted with the procedure the framework
prescribes; the audit captured a phantom title in our own bibliography, and the correction is
documented. Limitations - minimal-model validation, operational complexity, and an
undefined theory of standing - are discussed together with objections concerning the
gameability of distributive metrics and the prematurity of AGI as a category. We conclude
that an evaluation channel specified in distributive terms is a necessary, testable, and
governable complement to benchmark practice.
Keywords: epistemological anarchism; artificial general intelligence; design fiction; design
thinking; algorithmic monoculture; benchmark evaluation

1. Introduction
Every evaluation is a census of what we already know how to count. The observation is
old, and so is the warning attached to it: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a
good measure (Strathern, 1997). Contemporary research toward artificial general
intelligence (AGI) gives the warning a new scale. Development is conducted, almost
without exception, through what we call the benchmark-centered production cycle: specify
a capability, build a system, score the system against a benchmark, and respecify in the
light of the score. The cycle is efficient and reproducible - and it is precisely these virtues
that make its blind spot dangerous, because the score that certifies progress is a summary,
and a summary can remain flat while the distribution of outcomes beneath it moves
violently.
This monograph proposes a framework, FDF-AGI, that articulates three traditions - design
fiction, design thinking, and Paul Feyerabend's epistemological anarchism - into a single
research cycle with an explicit governance structure. We defend from the outset, however,
that the framework's value does not reside in its architecture of phases and operators. An
architecture of this kind is cheap to propose and expensive to sustain, and a later section
argues that much of the apparatus survives its own removal. The value resides in one
testable claim about the limits of evaluation practice, which we call the distributive thesis: a
benchmark-closed score is not a sufficient statistic for the distributive properties of a
system. Closing the cycle against benchmarks does not close the cycle against the
properties that matter, because there exist pairs of systems indistinguishable by the score
whose outcomes across groups differ arbitrarily widely.
The three traditions earn their place through a single shared intuition, worth stating before
any apparatus is introduced: all three reject that the current operational formulation of a
domain is the domain. Design fiction suspends disbelief precisely because a requirements
specification is not the world to be inhabited (Sterling, 2009; Bleecker, 2009). Design
thinking treats the first framing of a problem as a hypothesis rather than a datum (Schön,
1983; Buchanan, 1992). Feyerabend treats the insistence on a universal method as a
symptom to be explained rather than a cause to be obeyed (Feyerabend, 1975). This is not a
rhetorical coincidence; it is the same intuition in three registers - literary, procedural, and
philosophical - and the framework is an attempt to make the registers work in one cycle.
The contributions are four. First, we formalize the FDF-AGI cycle as a graph of operators,
with the governance return edge made explicit - an edge that an earlier version of the
framework announced in prose but omitted from its own figure, an omission we treat as
instructive rather than embarrassing. Second, we introduce a reopening procedure that
renders the entry point of the cycle non-privileged in an operational sense, not merely a

2. Background and Related Work
2.1 The Benchmark-Centered Production Cycle
The intellectual genealogy of evaluation practice in AI research is, ironically, a design
genealogy. Schön's account of the reflective practitioner describes professional competence
as reflection-in-action: the practitioner frames a situation, experiments within the framing,
and reframes when the situation back-talks (Schön, 1983). What the benchmark cycle
inherits from this tradition is the loop; what it drops is the back-talk. Rittel and Webber's
analysis of planning problems had already observed that social problems are wicked - they
have no stopping rule, no definitive formulation, and no test of a solution that does not
change the problem itself (Rittel & Webber, 1973). Buchanan imported the analysis into
design theory, arguing that design thinking is precisely the treatment of wicked problems
by constructive placement (Buchanan, 1992). A benchmark converts a wicked problem into
a tame one by decree: it fixes a stopping rule, a formulation, and a scoring function, and the
production cycle then optimizes against all three.
The economics of this conversion have been studied under the heading of Goodhart's law.
Strathern's formulation - when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure -
was written about audit regimes in British universities, where publication counts reshaped
the behavior they were meant to observe (Strathern, 1997). Manheim and Garrabrant
provide a taxonomy that matters here, distinguishing regressional, extremal, causal, and
adversarial variants of the law; their analysis suggests that no fixed metric is immune, only

differently exposed (Manheim & Garrabrant, 2018). In machine learning, the pathology has
been documented empirically: benchmark scores leak into model selection until the score
measures the selection process rather than the capability (Kapoor & Narayanan, 2023), and
conceptual analyses argue that a benchmark assembled to stand for “the world” cannot
carry the weight of that quantifier (Raji et al., 2021).
The AGI setting sharpens the problem because the target construct is itself contested. Legg
and Hutter's attempt to define universal intelligence compresses the notion into a scalar
over environments - a move that is clarifying and distorting at once (Legg & Hutter, 2007).
Chollet argues that any static benchmark can be gamed by preparation and that intelligence
must be measured as skill-acquisition efficiency over unseen tasks (Chollet, 2019). Morris
and colleagues, proposing graduated levels of AGI, note that capability summaries travel
poorly across domains and autonomy regimes (Morris et al., 2024). The pattern across
these proposals is consistent: the community knows its measures are compressions, and
keeps using them because compressions are actionable. The distributive thesis developed in
Section 3 accepts the compressions and asks a different question - what does a given
compression render invisible?
2.2 Design Fiction and Diegetic Prototyping
Design fiction emerged as a named practice in Sterling's account of designed objects as
spimes and in his short manifesto on the genre (Sterling, 2005; Sterling, 2009), and it was
Bleecker who gave the practice its canonical definition: the deliberate use of diegetic
prototypes to suspend disbelief about change (Bleecker, 2009). The key term descends
from film studies. Kirby documented how cinematic depictions of technology - created
with the collaboration of science consultants - function as diegetic prototypes that do real
argumentative work: embedded in a narrative world, a technology can be shown working,
failing, and being socially negotiated, without ever being built (Kirby, 2010). Dunne and
Raby's speculative design tradition broadens the ambition from persuasion to social
dreaming, using fictional artifacts to open possibility spaces rather than to forecast (Dunne
& Raby, 2013). Lindley and Coulton formalize the distinction between science fiction's
plausibility constraints and design fiction's world-building freedom (Lindley & Coulton,
2015).
The application of these practices to AI systems is recent and specific. Pilling and
colleagues deconstruct the definitional dualism through which AI is narrated - as either
autonomous agent or tool - and show that design fiction can hold both registers in
suspension (Pilling et al., 2021). Zhang and Long argue, on the basis of the poetics of
defamiliarization, that the value of a design fiction lies not in its content but in its capacity
to make the familiar strange enough to be interrogated (Zhang & Long, 2025). Lupetti and

than measuring bodies - a phrase that anticipates the framework's insistence that evaluation
channels are specified, not discovered (Lupetti et al., 2025). Across these accounts, one
function recurs: a diegetic prototype makes visible who is affected by a deployment, in a
way that an abstract list of concerns does not.
2.3 Design Thinking and Hybrid Intelligence
Design thinking contributes the procedural register. Schön's reflection-in-action supplies
the microstructure - framing, move, evaluation, reframe (Schön, 1983). Verganti and
colleagues analyze how AI changes innovation itself, arguing that the distinctive
contribution of designers in AI development lies in the creation of meaning rather than the
optimization of function (Verganti et al., 2020). Kelly and Grace provide a framework for
analyzing the combination of design thinking and AI, and their taxonomy of framing is
directly used by the present framework: framing may be performed by a human (Type I),
by an AI system (Type II), or by both in interaction (Type III) (Kelly & Grace, 2025).
Dellermann and colleagues, in two complementary papers, develop the notion of hybrid
intelligence - systems in which human and machine capabilities are combined so that the
combination exceeds either alone - and provide a taxonomy of design knowledge for such
systems (Dellermann et al., 2019a; Dellermann et al., 2019b).
Two commitments from this tradition carry into the framework. The first is that the initial
framing of a design situation is a hypothesis; the cycle must contain an operator that can
revise framings, not merely fill them. The second is that human and machine contributions
are not interchangeable inputs to a single objective but parties to a negotiated process - a
commitment that, transferred from product design to research governance, becomes the
demand that evaluation channels themselves be negotiable. Both commitments are
operationalized in Section 4, the first by the translation operator and the second by the
governance operator.

2.4 Epistemological Anarchy and Its Misreadings
The third register is philosophical. Feyerabend's Against Method advances a historical
thesis about actual scientific practice: no single methodological rule - not even the most
sacrosanct - has survived contact with the history of science without profitable violation,
and a methodology that outlawed such violations would have arrested the sciences it
claimed to rationalize (Feyerabend, 1975). The association with the phrase “anything goes”
requires care, because the phrase is Lakatos's caricature before it is Feyerabend's slogan:
Lakatos coined it to mock what he took to be the consequence of Feyerabend's position,
and Feyerabend then adopted it ironically, giving it the status of a chapter title and using it
as a reductio against claims of methodological universality rather than as a proposal
(Lakatos, 1970). Scholarly treatments of the episode converge on this reading (Preston,
1997). The distinction is not pedantry; Section 8 argues that the misattribution is the
principal vector through which epistemological anarchism gets appropriated as a license for
arbitrariness, a risk that has been documented in the AI-adjacent literature (Sienicki &
Sienicki, 2025).
The later Feyerabend is more useful to AI research than the caricature. Science in a Free
Society develops institutional arguments: a free society is one in which traditions -
including scientific traditions - must make their case before citizens rather than rule by
epistemic fiat (Feyerabend, 1978). Conquest of Abundance, the last phase, shifts the target
from rules of method to abstraction itself: the danger of a successful formal system is that it
empties the world it represents, discarding the abundance that did not fit (Feyerabend,
1999). This is the Feyerabend with something specific to say about AI, because it names
the mechanism by which a system that outperforms on an abstraction can drain the thing
being measured. A recent study makes the bridge explicit, arguing that machine
intelligence flattens the diversity and richness of human cognition, that AI systems actively
sculpt reality by narrowing the space of preferences and of what counts as real or possible,
and that similarity to the abstract carries a measurable epistemic cost (Oliveira, 2025). The
contrast with Kuhn is instructive: paradigms succeed one another, and incommensurability
is a relation between successive normal sciences, whereas anarchism prescribes no
successor structure at all - which is why a framework that borrows from Feyerabend must
introduce its own governance rather than inherit one (Kuhn, 1962).

3. The Distributive Thesis
This section is the core of the monograph; the remaining sections are instrumentation. We
define the benchmark-closed score, define the distributive statistics it summarizes away,
and prove that the summary is insufficient. The proposition is a statement of existence and
is deliberately constructive: Section 6 exhibits the construction under controlled conditions.
3.1 Definitions
Let E = {t1, …, tK} be a finite set of evaluation tasks, each with a positive weight wt. For a
system m, let acct(m) ∈ [0, 1] denote its measured performance on task t. The
benchmark-closed score is the weighted sum
 sE(m) = Σt ∈ E wt · acct(m) (1)
Let G = {g1, …, gK} be a partition of a relevant population into groups. For each group g
and task t, let outg,t(m) denote the average outcome accrued by group g under system m on
task t. The distributive statistics of the system are the vector
 D(m) = ( dg(m) )g ∈ G, dg(m) = Σt ∈ E wt · outg,t(m), (2)
and the distributive decrement of g is the ratio
 δg(m) = dg(m) / maxm' dg(m'), (3)
so that δg(m) = 1 for the best observable system in group g and is smaller for the others.
The interpretive point is a division of labor. The score sE is a compression of the
population's experience into a single actionable number - that is its virtue and the reason it
organizes production cycles. The vector D records what each group received; it is not one
number but a profile, and no profile can be reconstructed from a single number without
auxiliary assumptions that the benchmark does not supply. The question of Section 3.2 is
whether the reconstruction failure is merely practical or structural.
3.2 Proposition 1: Insufficiency of the Benchmark-Closed Score
Proposition 1 (insufficiency of sE for D). sE is not a sufficient statistic for D: there exist
systems m and m' such that sE(m) = sE(m') while D(m) ≠ D(m').
The construction is elementary and worth exhibiting in full, because its simplicity is the
argument. Take a base system m with group outcomes (at, bt) on task t, normalized so that
at + bt = ct with ct fixed. Define m' by the redistribution (at, bt) ↦ (α·at, ct − α·at) for a
scalar α ≠ 1 chosen to keep the weighted ratio of the redistributed masses at the desired
value. Because the equality constraints impose a single linear equation on two unknowns
per task, the solution set is a family of dimension one; sE is constant along the family by

construction, while D traverses a non-degenerate segment of attainable profiles. The score
is therefore blind, by construction, to every direction in that family. The complete
realization - the set of instances that implements the redistribution under realistic
generative models - is the object of the experiments in Section 6, which show that the blind
directions are not only non-empty but numerically substantial.
3.3 Consequences
Two consequences deserve emphasis, and both are supported by literatures that developed
independently of the present framework. The first consequence is that improvement in the
score does not entail improvement in the distribution. If sE(m') > sE(m) while D(m') is
dominated by D(m) componentwise, then the production cycle registers an advance and the
distributive channel registers stagnation or regression - and a research program that
observes only sE cannot distinguish the two cases. This is not a hypothetical. In a deployed
health algorithm, the use of cost as a proxy for need rendered the program blind to a
distributional fact: at the same risk score, Black patients carried substantially more disease
than White patients, and correcting the proxy would raise the fraction of Black patients
flagged for additional care from roughly 17.7% to 46.5% (Obermeyer et al., 2019). The
aggregate had been improving; the distribution had not. The methodological response -
evaluation conditioned on group membership, as institutionalized in fairness-aware
learning - is precisely the move from sE toward D (Hardt et al., 2016; Barocas et al., 2023).
The second consequence is that the gap between score and distribution is not stationary; it
grows with the architecture of modern practice. Bommasani and colleagues show that the
sharing of components - the normal condition of the foundation-model era, in which a
small number of base models feeds many downstream deployments - actively homogenizes
outcomes, with individual-level effects exceeding group-level effects (Bommasani et al.,
2022). Kleinberg and Raghavan formalize the complementary mechanism: when many
decision-makers adopt the same algorithmic recommendation, their errors become
correlated, and this correlation can improve average welfare while deteriorating its
distribution - a systemic risk in which no individual decision appears responsible
(Kleinberg & Raghavan, 2021). The empirical footprint of the phenomenon is broad:
language models trained and evaluated on overlapping corpora reproduce stereotypical
associations that aggregate accuracy never surfaces (Bolukbasi et al., 2016), and
taxonomies of downstream risk now treat homogenization and discrimination as first-class
failure modes rather than implementation bugs (Weidinger et al., 2022). A relational
reading completes the picture: such harms are properties of the socio-technical relation, not
of artifacts in isolation, which is why artifact-level scores cannot detect them (Birhane,
2021).

3.4 The Role of Design Fiction
Design fiction is not a layer of the framework; it is a generator of distributive questions,
and locating its function precisely is what makes the framework defensible. A diegetic
prototype of the form “here is a future in which X” renders visible which group's outcome
dg moved, and how, in a way that an abstract checklist of fairness concerns does not
(Kirby, 2010; Dunne & Raby, 2013). The technique of defamiliarization explains why: the
fiction's estrangement effect is what dislodges the current operational formulation from its
position as the domain (Zhang & Long, 2025). Participatory instruments extend the reach
of the generator beyond the design team, eliciting from affected communities the use cases,
benefits, and harms that no internal review surfaces (Mun et al., 2024).
The contribution of design fiction to FDF-AGI is therefore the generation of the specific set
of distributive statistics D that the non-benchmark channel needs to measure - and nothing
more. This is a considerably more modest function than “layer 1 of an architecture,” and it
is the defensible one. What a fiction can do is name the groups, name the movement, and
name the context with enough concreteness that a statistician can specify what to measure;
what it cannot do is certify that the measurement is valid, which is the audit's job (Section
7). The division of labor is deliberate, and the framework fails gracefully if either half is
missing: fictions without D-statistics degenerate into mood boards, and D-statistics without
fictions inherit the blind spots of whoever chose the groups.
4. Formalization of the Cycle
4.1 State Space and Operators
The state of the cycle is the quadruple
 S = (X, F, M, G), (4)
where X is the set of diegetic artifacts produced so far, F is the current design question, M
is the set of competing approaches kept alive, and G is the governance decision in force
with its attribution of standing. Four operators act on the state. The speculation operator Δ:
X → X attaches a diegetic artifact to X without eliminating any existing artifact;
speculation accumulates. The translation operator Φ: X → F converts the current artifact
into an actionable design question, which is the operation by which fiction becomes
testable. The proliferation operator Ψ: M → M attaches competing approaches to M; by
construction Ψ is extensive, meaning M ⊆ Ψ(M), and this extensivity is the single point at
which the framework's anarchist commitment is encoded - proliferation is structural, not
optional. The governance operator Γ: F × M → G × F decides among approaches and
returns a revised design question to the process phase. Figure 1 depicts the cycle as a graph

4.2 Aggregation Rules and Standing
The governance operator is where the framework is honest about what it cannot do. The
following proposition records the gap.
Proposition 2 (insufficiency of aggregation rules). An aggregation rule over preferences,
however well designed, determines how preferences are combined. It does not determine
which preferences have standing - that is, which count. Therefore no aggregation rule,
by itself, specifies a legitimate institutional arrangement.
The distinction is one of level, and available empirical results do not cross it. A recent
study of democratic governance mechanisms compares aggregation rules for collective
preference expression in AI model governance and finds that quadratic aggregation
combined with an egalitarian distribution of decision power was perceived as the fairest
combination (Sharma et al., 2026). The result is real and useful: it tells us something about
how perceived fairness tracks the interaction of rule and power allocation. It is not a theory
of standing. Perceived fairness of a combination rule is a function of how preferences are
combined; standing is the prior question of which positions have the right to be combined
at all. Democratic survey frameworks for AI use cases elicit distributed judgments at scale
(Mun et al., 2024), and proposals for democratic control of AGI specify institutional
wrappers (Salmi, 2023), but both operate at the level of aggregation and elicitation. The
framework deliberately leaves standing undefined; Proposition 2 is the record of that

4.3 The Reopening Gate
An earlier version of the framework asserted that the cycle has no privileged entry point,
while its own protocol prescribed five phases in a mandatory order beginning with a
configuration phase - a mapping of traditions and a selection of lenses. The assertion and
the protocol were incompatible: a mandatory phase zero is a pre-commitment to a method
and a metric, executed before any operation. The correction adopted here makes the
assertion operational instead of rhetorical.
Definition 3 (reopening gate). The configuration phase is revocable if, and only if, an actor
with standing in the deliberation can trigger a review that establishes that the selection of
lenses or the set of mapped traditions excludes a tradition satisfying the public inclusion
criterion C. A change to C is itself an act of governance, subject to Γ.
Under this definition, the entry point remains the configuration phase - but it is now a
procedure rather than an axiom, and the initial asymmetry is explicitly reversible by a
procedure the framework itself specifies. This is the available improvement. A genuinely
non-privileged entry point is indistinguishable from the absence of procedure, and absence
of procedure is not anarchism; it is the condition in which the loudest actor's framing wins
by default. The gate converts a philosophical posture into an auditable mechanism, which is
the direction in which the whole framework moves: every commitment that can be made
checkable is made checkable, and the residue that cannot - standing, again - is marked
rather than hidden.

4.4 Opacity as a Question of Authority Allocation
The most productive reformulation the framework offers concerns opacity. The received
question - how do we make AI transparent? - presupposes that opacity is a defect to be
eliminated. The literature already complicates this presupposition. Burrell distinguishes
three irreducible sources of opacity in algorithmic systems: opacity inherent to the
mathematics of learning from data, opacity produced by intentional corporate or state
secrecy, and opacity produced by scale and technical illiteracy - only the third yields to
better documentation, and none yields to a demand for full legibility (Burrell, 2016).
Ananny and Crawford show that the transparency ideal itself is limited: seeing does not
amount to knowing, and algorithmic accountability requires practices - timing,
positionality, and publics - that visibility alone cannot supply (Ananny & Crawford, 2018).
Lipton's analysis of interpretability reaches a parallel conclusion at the model level: the
term carries competing desiderata whose trade-offs are themselves choices (Lipton, 2018),
and recent reviews treat opacity management as a condition of ethical sustainability rather
than a solvable defect (Cianciaruso, 2026).
The framework's move is to relocate the question from engineering to authority: the
question is not how to make AI transparent, but who decides which forms of opacity are
acceptable, and in which contexts. Under this relocation, opacity ceases to be a defect to
eliminate and becomes a condition to govern - which dissolves the
transparency-versus-performance dilemma, not because a balance has been found, but
because the question has been moved. The framework, honestly, does not answer the
relocated question; Proposition 2 records why no aggregation rule could answer it alone.
What the framework does provide is the machinery through which the question gets asked
repeatedly, by whoever has standing, with the results feeding back into the cycle. The
openness is explicit and indicated, which is the most a framework that refuses false closure
can offer.
5. Research Protocol
The protocol preserves the five phases of the cycle. The correction relative to the earlier
version is that phase zero is now itself subject to the reopening gate of Definition 3 rather
than constituting a methodological pre-commitment executed outside the reach of
governance. Each phase has a declared objective and a checkable exit condition; the
checkability is what makes the protocol auditable rather than inspirational.
Phase 0 - configuration of the field. The team maps the traditions relevant to the problem,
selects the lenses through which scenarios will be generated, and defines two public
criteria: the inclusion criterion C for traditions and the pluralism criterion for approaches.


=== PAGINA 15 ===

The phase is subject to reopening. Phase 1 - scenario generation. The team produces
diegetic artifacts under an objective that makes the distributive function explicit: each
artifact must name at least one group g in the population partition whose outcome dg moves
under the depicted deployment, and the group that does not move. Phase 2 - translation into
design questions. The team runs the design cycle of Section 4 under a restriction that
disciplines the output: the resulting specification is an ecological account, not a technical
specification - what the system does, for whom, under which physical and social
constraints, and with which consequences. Phase 3 - proliferation. The team deliberately
generates contraindications, alternative architectures, alternative datasets, and alternative
objectives; the operator Ψ is extensive, and no approach is eliminated in this phase. Phase 4
- deliberation. The governance operator Γ is applied; the output is a decision and a revised
design question, which returns to the cycle by the dashed edge of Figure 1.
One operational commitment deserves emphasis because earlier versions promised it and
delivered nothing: a verifiable pluralism criterion. The extensivity of Ψ is checkable by
inspection of |M| across iterations - the framework fails if |M| decreases between two
iterations without a removal that has passed through Γ. This is a weak criterion, and it is
verifiable, and Section 8 argues that a weak verifiable criterion is preferable to a strong
unverifiable one. The protocol's artifacts are designed to carry their context with them, in
the spirit of documentation practices such as model cards and datasheets that attach
provenance and intended use to models and datasets (Mitchell et al., 2019; Gebru et al.,
2021); the phase structure, meanwhile, mirrors the end-to-end shape of internal audit
frameworks that assign responsibilities across the deployment lifecycle (Raji et al., 2020).

6.1 The Minimal Model and the Six Experiments

6.2 Monoculture Is Not Monotonic
The monoculture experiment requires correlation. Holding the score fixed - drift bounded
by 1.1 × 10-16 across the sweep - the dispersion between groups collapses from 6.85 × 10-3

deciders reaches unity only at ρ = 1. The displacement, however, is not monotonic in ρ: it
takes the values 0.0245, 0.0519, 0.1704, 0.0720, and 0.0075 at ρ = 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9, and
1 respectively, peaking at ρ = 0.75 and collapsing at ρ = 1 (Figure 3). Full correlation
eliminates the displacement space itself; the maximal blind spot lies in an intermediate
regime.

The collapse at ρ = 1 reinforces the thesis rather than weakening it. When decision-makers
are identical, the attainable displacement tends to zero not because diversity was preserved
but because nothing remains to diverge: the available displacement empties along with the
variation. The curve of displacement against correlation therefore has the shape of a
collapse by uniformity - rising while residual diversity remains to be exploited, and
plunging when diversity reaches zero. The signal of homogenization is accordingly not a
small displacement; it is the combination of a small displacement with null dispersion.
Read this way, the result is a quantitative signature of the mechanism that Kleinberg and
Raghavan describe qualitatively: correlation between deciders alters the social distribution
of outcomes without any individual decision appearing responsible (Kleinberg &
Raghavan, 2021), and the signature suggests that monitoring programs keyed to average
performance will miss the phase in which the blind spot is widest.

6.3 Blindness Scales with Size Asymmetry
The displacement does not require a different benchmark - only a reallocation of authority.
Moving the outcome vector along the null direction attains a displacement of 0.473 with
the score practically invariant and a mean majority gain of 0.050. The scale experiment
quantifies how the blindness grows with asymmetry: the ratio between the minority group's
loss and the majority group's gain equals πrich/πpoor exactly - maximum relative error 3.8 ×
10-16 across the grid - taking the value 6.0 when the minority share is 10% and 600 when it
is 0.1% (Figure 2). For a group comprising a tenth of the population, the benchmark is six
times blinder to that group's loss than it is sensitive to the majority's gain; for a group of a
thousandth, six hundred times.

The law has a sobering methodological implication, because it says the blindness is not
noise that larger evaluations will wash out; it is a deterministic function of population
structure that grows exactly as the minority shrinks. Field evidence is consistent with the
mechanism, though it cannot of course confirm the law itself: the health-algorithm case
discussed in Section 3.3 is precisely a situation in which an aggregate statistic remained
acceptable while a distributional fact drifted far from it (Obermeyer et al., 2019), and the
corrective instruments that exist - group-conditioned evaluation and its fairness-aware
descendants (Hardt et al., 2016; Barocas et al., 2023) - are institutional recognitions that the
second channel must be measured, not inferred.

6.4 The Geometry of Blindness
The dimension of the kernel is exactly |G| − 1. For |G| ∈ {2, 3, 4, 6, 8} the experiment
yielded kernel dimensions 1, 2, 3, 5, and 7, and the width of the admissible interval for the
redistribution parameter decreased from 0.48 to 0.109. The count of groups is thus the
principal structural determinant of blindness: more groups mean more blind directions, and
the directions shrink but do not close as multiplicity grows. The result has a natural reading
in the terms of Section 4. A production cycle that scores against sE is optimizing within the
quotient space that the kernel parametrizes; everything in that space is invisible to the
score, and the space grows linearly with the plurality of the affected population. A research
program that cares about distribution must therefore populate the kernel with measurement
- which is what the distributive channel D provides - or accept that plurality and blindness
grow together.

resistance to monoculture is a distributive good. The earlier version of the text used one for

7.4 Self-Application and Implications
The procedure applies to itself, and it is the self-application that carries the methodological
lesson. The audit of this monograph captured an error in its own bibliography file: the title
of Kleinberg and Raghavan was initially recorded as “Algorithmic Monoculture and the
Challenge of Outcome Homogenization” - precisely the title that the corrected Entry 13
declares not to exist. The mechanism of the error is instructive. The phantom title was
plausible, it semantically matched the claim it supported, and it circulated because it had
never been resolved against a registry; it was structurally indistinguishable from the
citation-pathology documented at field scale, where invalid results propagate through
literatures because downstream users inherit upstream evaluation shortcuts (Kapoor &
Narayanan, 2023). A title that matches the argument too well deserves the most checking,
not the least.
Three implications follow for research practice. First, citation hygiene is a fail-closed
practice: an entry that cannot be resolved against Crossref, Zenodo, or OpenAlex should
fail the document, not the reader. Second, grey literature requires the same registry

8. Counterarguments and Limitations
This section assembles the strongest objections we could construct against the framework,
the internal limitations recorded by the validation, and the responses available at the
present stage. The ordering is by severity rather than by convenience; the first two items
are, in our judgment, the ones most likely to matter to a skeptical research program.
First, validation in a minimal model. The six experiments confirm Proposition 1 and
quantify the blindness, but in a synthetic model of groups and tasks; groups, distributions,
and weights are chosen, and it remains open whether the observed scale law survives data
from real deployments. The response is partial by design: the 400-draw robustness
experiment shows the phenomenon is not a parametrization artifact, and the consistency
between the model's mechanism and documented field failures (Obermeyer et al., 2019;
Kleinberg & Raghavan, 2021) is suggestive - but consistency is not derivation, and a
deployment-scale test of the πrich/πpoor law is the obvious next study. Until such a test
exists, the appropriate posture is to treat the law as a hypothesis with a working
mechanism, not a measured regularity of deployed systems.
Second, operational complexity. Four operators and five phases are more than most
research teams can sustain, and an earlier chapter of this research program argued that a
portion of the apparatus is largely decorative. The framework's own conclusion concedes
the point: its verifiable content is the distributive thesis and the audit procedure, and the
four-layer architecture survives the removal of its own layers. The practical
recommendation is therefore incremental adoption rather than wholesale adoption - a
distributive channel alongside the benchmark, the |M| extensivity check, and the
bibliographic audit can be adopted by a single team in a single study, whereas the full cycle
is an institutional commitment. The risk that ceremony crowds out substance is real, and
the verifiable-core argument is the mitigation offered, though it remains a mitigation rather
than a refutation.

Third, the reopening gate is weak. Definition 3 makes the configuration phase reversible,
not negotiable: an actor with standing can trigger reopening, but the content of the
configuration phase is not itself submitted to deliberation as a matter of course. A stronger
gate - periodic mandatory deliberation on the configuration itself - would address the
asymmetry at the cost of the deliberative burden the weak gate was designed to bound. We
record the trade-off rather than resolve it, because the resolution is an institutional design
question that Proposition 2 places outside the reach of any aggregation rule.
Fourth, standing remains undefined. Proposition 2 identifies the gap and does not close it;
available empirical work on participatory governance operates at the level of elicitation and
aggregation (Mun et al., 2024; Sharma et al., 2026; Salmi, 2023), which is below the level
at which the standing question is posed. This is the framework's principal intellectual debt,
and we flag it as such. Fifth, a familiar objection to the philosophical component:
anarchism is either self-undermining or a license for arbitrariness. The response developed
in Section 2.4 is that the objection lands on the misattributed maxim. “Anything goes” is
Lakatos's caricature of Feyerabend, adopted by Feyerabend ironically and used as a
reductio against methodological universality (Lakatos, 1970; Preston, 1997); the correctly
attributed position is narrower than the framework needs it to be, and the documented risk
of appropriation shrinks accordingly (Sienicki & Sienicki, 2025). The institutional
Feyerabend of Science in a Free Society argues for procedures that make authority
contestable, which is the opposite of a license (Feyerabend, 1978).
Sixth, the distributive channel is itself gameable. Any fixed D-statistic is subject to the
same Goodhart dynamics as any fixed score, and a team optimizing for reported group
parity can satisfy the metric while missing the harm - the adversarial variant of the law is
not suspended by goodwill (Manheim & Garrabrant, 2018). The framework's response is
structural rather than metricial: D is specified as a family of statistics under continuous
revision through Γ, the reopening gate keeps the revision procedure itself contestable, and
the extensivity check prevents silent narrowing of the approach set. This is a governance
answer to a measurement problem, and we concede that it shifts rather than eliminates the
burden. Seventh, the AGI framing may be premature: the target construct is contested
(Legg & Hutter, 2007), graduated operationalizations differ from scalar ones (Morris et al.,
2024), and nothing in the framework requires AGI to arrive for the thesis to apply. The
distributive thesis targets the evaluation practice of general-purpose systems as they exist
today; the AGI framing is where that practice is most concentrated, not a prediction.
Finally, the monograph is the work of a single author and a single research line, and the
reflexive audit, though genuine, is not independent verification; replication of the six
experiments and of the audit procedure by external groups remains necessary before the
results are treated as more than a demonstration.

9. Conclusion and Implications
FDF-AGI proposes a posture for AGI-adjacent research that is at once speculative and
rigorous, pluralist and governable. Its verifiable content does not reside in the architecture
of operators and phases, which survives its own removal; it resides in one claim about the
limits of an evaluation practice - that closing the cycle against benchmarks does not close
the cycle against the properties that matter, because distributive properties are invariant to
changes that benchmarks measure - together with the machinery that makes the claim
actionable: a distributive channel D specified by fictions and measured by audit, a
proliferation operator whose extensivity is checkable, a governance operator with an
explicit return edge, and a reopening gate that converts anti-dogmatism from posture into
procedure.
The empirical contribution is deliberately small and deliberately sharp. In a minimal model,
benchmark insufficiency exists if and only if the affected population is plural; the kernel of
blind directions has dimension exactly |G| − 1; attainable displacement peaks under
intermediate correlation and collapses under uniformity; and the ratio between minority
loss and majority gain tracks πrich/πpoor exactly, reaching 600 at a 0.1% minority share.
These are model results, and Section 8 is explicit about the gap between a model and a
deployment; but they demonstrate that the thesis is testable, locate the conditions under
which it bites, and provide signatures - small displacement with null dispersion - that
monitoring programs keyed to averages will systematically miss. The reflexive
contribution is similarly small and, we would argue, similarly sharp: the audit caught a
phantom title in our own bibliography, which is the smallest possible demonstration that
evaluation channels must be applied to the evaluator.
Three implications follow for practice. For researchers, the immediate adoption is
reporting: distributive statistics D should be reported alongside any benchmark score sE, in
the same tables, with the same prominence, because Proposition 1 entails that the second
channel carries information the first cannot. For institutions, the adoption is procedural:
governance that returns to the cycle - rather than appending approval at the end - and
configuration that is revocable by public criterion are implementable now, and the |M|
check shows how a philosophical commitment becomes an auditable artifact. For the
design community, the adoption is one of scope: fiction's function is the generation of
distributive questions - which groups move, and how - which is a discipline, not a
decoration, and the participatory instruments now available make the generation collective
rather than visionary (Mun et al., 2024).
The open problems are the two the framework marks rather than hides: a theory of
standing, and a deployment-scale test of the scale law. Work on democratic aggregation is

Page 24
accumulating empirical material that a theory of standing will need to digest (Sharma et al.,
2026; Salmi, 2023); the mechanisms of homogenization are quantified in the settings where
components are shared (Bommasani et al., 2022; Kleinberg & Raghavan, 2021); and the
audit instruments for portability exist (Mitchell et al., 2019; Gebru et al., 2021; Raji et al.,
2020). What remains is the deliberate construction of the second channel - specified in
distributive terms, governed by whoever has standing, and audited with the same procedure
it prescribes. A research era that names its own method and submits the naming to
deliberation is not less disciplined. It is more.
