Abstract
The dominant research programs pursuing artificial general intelligence (AGI) treat safety, auditabil-
ity, and governance as properties to be layered onto capable systems after the fact. This monograph
presentsArkhe, a research program and engineering architecture that inverts that ordering: it takes
verifiable trust infrastructureas the load-bearing foundation upon which increasingly general AI
capabilities can be responsibly built. Arkhe is organized around a single constitutional principle—
that a claim about system behavior counts only when it is backed by mechanical evidence: compiler
output, passing tests, cryptographic hashes, and independently checkable proofs. We make four con-
tributions. First, we specify a canonical trust-infrastructure architecture of seven Rust crates plus
an event bus, organized into platform, domain, and security layers, with a compiler-enforced depen-
dency discipline and an explicit separation betweentechnical invariants(properties the code must
guarantee in every reachable state) andgovernance policies(rules humans decide and may change
without touching code). Second, we present a threat model that takes the internal adversary—the
malicious engineer, the compromised dependency, the colluding key holders—as seriously as the
external one. Third, we describe an economic layer in which autonomous agents transact under
the x402 payment protocol and payment is modeled as a capability constraint inside the policy
engine, with every settlement leaving immutable evidence. Fourth, we situate a prescriptive 2026–
2045 cognitive-architecture roadmap—spanning causal world models, continual learning, and a
multi-layer Safety Kernel—on top of this infrastructure, using quantitative milestones and explicit
maturity labels. Throughout, we are deliberate about epistemic status: we distinguish what is im-
plemented and tested, what is specified but unbuilt, and what remains open research. Arkhe does
not claim that AGI has been achieved or is imminent; it claims thatifgeneral systems are built, the
order of construction matters, and that trust must be a precondition rather than a patch.
Keywords:artificial general intelligence; AI safety; trust infrastructure; verifiable computing; Rust;
decentralized identifiers; verifiable credentials; threshold cryptography; threat modeling; x402;
agentic commerce; AI governance; continual learning; world models.

1 Introduction
1.1 The inversion problem
The contemporary trajectory of AI development follows a recognizable pattern: capabilities are
scaled first, and the apparatus of trust—evaluation, interpretability, oversight, incident response, and
regulation—races to catch up. Frontier language models are deployed to hundreds of millions of users
while the tools for understanding their internal computation remain nascent; autonomous agents are
granted tool access, budgets, and long-horizon goals while the mechanisms for constraining, auditing,
and reversing their actions remain ad hoc [28, 26]. The gap between what systems candoand what
their operators canverify about what they didis widening, not narrowing [4, 5].
This monograph presents Arkhe, a research program built on the opposite premise. Arkhe holds that
for systems approaching general capability, trust infrastructure is not a feature but aprerequisite: the
substrate of identity, attestation, immutable evidence, governed policy, and hardware-anchored control
must exist, be tested, and be adversarially hardenedbeforethe capabilities that will stress it are attached.
The name is drawn from the Greekarkh ¯e(αρ χη)—origin, foundation, first principle—and the choice
is programmatic: the foundation comes first.
1.2 The constitutional principle: mechanical verification
Arkhe’s development is governed by a rule the project treats as constitutional rather than merely method-
ological:a claim counts only when it is mechanically verifiable.Concretely, an assertion about the
system must be backed by at least one of: (a)compiler output—the code type-checks and builds under
a compiler-enforced dependency graph; (b)passing tests—behavior is demonstrated by executable, re-
producible test suites; (c)cryptographic evidence—hashes, signatures, and Merkle proofs that any third
party can recheck without trusting the claimant.
This principle has a corollary that shapes everything in this monograph, including its own prose:
unverified statements must be labeled, not presumed.Where a component is specified but unim-
plemented, we say so. Where a projection depends on research that has not yet succeeded, we say so.
Where a number originates in the project’s internal planning documents rather than in independently
reproducible measurement, we attribute it as such. Section 10 consolidates these epistemic-status dis-
tinctions into a single maturity table.

The principle also has a stringent negative consequence that Arkhe accepts explicitly: some things
cannotbe proven, and pretending otherwise is harmful. In particular, Arkhe’s attestation layer proves
thatdeterministic executionfaithfully followed a committed plan; it does not, and cannot, prove that a
large language model’s natural-languagereasoningwas semantically correct. Cryptographic proof of
“correct reasoning” for LLMs is not a solved problem and may be mathematically ill-defined [1]; Arkhe
therefore scopes its proofs to deterministic execution and treats semantic risk as a governance problem,
handled by policy, risk classification, and human oversight rather than by a false promise of proof.
1.3 What Arkhe is, and what it is not
Arkhe, as presented here, comprises three artifacts of differing maturity:
1.The Trust Infrastructure— a canonical architecture of seven Rust crates plus an event bus
(identity, attestation, storage, governance, security, consensus, observability, event bus), with
standardizedasync_traitinterfaces, runtime-verifiable invariants, and a v2 threat model cover-
ing internal adversaries and supply-chain compromise. This is the most mature layer: portions are
implemented and tested in a multi-crate Rust workspace; the remainder is specified at interface
level [1].
2.The Economic Layer— a proposed integration (ADR-0002) of the x402 payment protocol, un-
der which autonomous agents pay for APIs, inference, and digital resources programmatically
over HTTP, with payment modeled as a policy constraint and every settlement recorded as im-
mutable evidence. This is at the architecture-decision-record stage: designed, threat-modeled,
not yet in production [3].
3.The Cognitive Roadmap— a prescriptive 2026–2045 plan (v2.0) for the capability layers an
AGI would require: multimodal perception, a causal world model (C-JEPA), a hybrid continual-
learning architecture (CL-4L), and a four-layer Safety Kernel interposed between cognition and
action. This is a roadmap with quantitative milestones, not a description of an implemented
system [2].
Equally important is what Arkhe isnot. Arkhe is not a claim that AGI exists, that it will arrive
on a particular date, or that the roadmap targets will be met. It is not a proof system for machine
consciousness, semantic truth, or benevolence—categories that the project explicitly declines to treat
as provable. And it is not a single monolithic system: it is a layered discipline in which each layer’s
guarantees are stated precisely, verified where possible, and honestly bounded where not.

2 Background and Related Work
2.1 The capability–verification gap
Frontier models as of mid-2026 demonstrate strong performance on knowledge-intensive and reason-
ing benchmarks, and test-time compute scaling has driven rapid cost reductions for hard reasoning
tasks. Yet the project’s internal state-of-the-art assessment [2]—consistent with the broader literature
on generalization benchmarks such as the ARC-AGI family [4]—identifies a persistent qualitative gap:
current systems areskill systems, notskill-acquisition systems. A human learns a novel abstraction
4

=== PAGINA 5 ===
task from tens of examples; frontier models require orders of magnitude more supervision or search.
Sample efficiency, compositional generalization, causal (rather than correlational) reasoning [16, 17],
and continual learning without catastrophic forgetting [9] remain open, and each of these gaps is also a
verificationgap: a system whose learning is opaque and whose competence boundary is unknown is a
system whose behavior cannot be bounded.
Mechanistic interpretability has made genuine progress—notably sparse autoencoders (SAEs)
that decompose model activations into more monosemantic features [18, 19] and automated circuit-
discovery methods [20]—and Arkhe’s Safety Kernel roadmap builds on that line of work (Section 7).
But feature coverage is partial, and interpretability tooling today monitors and flags rather than proves.
Arkhe’s position is that interpretability is a necessary layer of defense-in-depth, not a substitute for
architectural constraint.
2.2 Alignment approaches and their runtime gap
Constitutional AI [6] and RLHF-family methods [7] shape model dispositions at training time; process
supervision rewards verified reasoning steps [8]. What these approaches under-supply is aruntime
enforcement substrate: a place where an explicit, human-legible constitution is evaluated on every
proposed action, where high-risk actions fail closed, and where every decision leaves tamper-evident
evidence. Work on concrete safety problems, control, and capability-threshold frameworks [22, 23,
24, 21] motivates such a substrate; Arkhe attempts to engineer it, drawing on the verified-microkernel
tradition—seL4’s machine-checked proof of functional correctness demonstrated that small, verified
enforcement cores are feasible [25]—for the intuition that the enforcement layer should be minimal,
decidable, and independently auditable.
2.3 Agent infrastructure, identity, and delegation
A rapidly consolidating literature argues that agentic AI requiresexternaltechnical infrastructure—
identifiers, credentials, delegation, and incident attribution—rather than model-internal safety alone.
Chan et al. propose “infrastructure for AI agents” as a research agenda spanning agent IDs, certifica-
tion, and accountability channels [26]; South et al. formalizeauthenticated delegation, in which third
parties can verify that an agent acts on behalf of a specific principal within cryptographically bounded
permissions [27]; Shavit et al. enumerate governance practices for agentic systems, including action
logging and human-in-the-loop controls [28]; and privilege-control frameworks such as Progent bring
least-authority enforcement to agent tool use [29]. Arkhe is best read as an opinionated, end-to-end en-
gineering instantiation of this agenda: its identity layer adopts the W3C Decentralized Identifier (DID)
and Verifiable Credential (VC) standards [30, 31]—identities resolve to verifiable public keys; capabil-
ities are granted as signed, expiring, revocable credentials checked cryptographicallyat the moment of
use—and its bound-credential mechanism (Section 6) implements scope attenuation of exactly the kind
the delegation literature calls for. For distributed control of privileged operations, Arkhe standardizes
on threshold Schnorr signatures per FROST (RFC 9591) [32].
2.4 Programmatic payments: the x402 protocol
A general agent that cannot pay for resources is dependent on human-provisioned accounts and API
keys—a dependency that is both an operational bottleneck and an accountability blur. The x402 proto-
col, originated by Coinbase in May 2025 and subsequently transferred to a vendor-neutral x402 Founda-
tion under the Linux Foundation, revives the HTTP 402 “Payment Required” status code as a machine-
navigable payment handshake: a server answers an unpaid request with 402 and machine-readable
payment requirements; the client signs a stablecoin transfer authorization and retries with the payment
attached in a header; a facilitator verifies and settles on-chain; the resource is delivered with a settlement
receipt [33, 34, 35]. The protocol is chain-agnostic via CAIP-2 network identifiers, is designed around
small-value machine-to-machine payments, and by mid-2026 had attracted broad industry governance

of millions of transactions at sub-dollar average values [36]. Arkhe’s ADR-0002 (Section 8) adopts

the policy engine, and every payment stage emits auditable evidence.
2.5 The regulatory environment
The architecture described here is being developed as comprehensive AI regulation crystallizes. The
EU AI Act entered into force in August 2024 with staggered application: prohibitions from Febru-
ary 2025, general-purpose-model obligations from August 2025, and—following the 2025–2026 “Dig-
ital Omnibus” simplification package—a deferral of most high-risk-system obligations into late 2027
and 2028 [37, 38]. In Brazil, PL 2338/2023 (Marco Legal da IA) passed the Senate in December 2024
and, as of mid-2026, awaits a Chamber of Deputies vote; it establishes a risk-tiered framework with
rights to information, explanation, contestation, and human review, an enforcement architecture coordi-
nated by the ANPD, and sanctions up to R$50 million per violation [39, 40]. Both regimes converge
on demands—documented risk management, human oversight, traceability, incident reporting, tamper-
resistant logs—that arearchitecturalin nature. Section 9 argues that Arkhe’s invariants map onto these
obligations by construction rather than by retrofit.
3 The Arkhe Thesis and Design Principles
3.1 Thesis
Thesis.Ifsystems of increasingly general capability are to be built,thenthey must be built on top
of an infrastructure in which identity is verifiable, execution is attestable, evidence is immutable,
policy is decidable and human-governed, and shutdown is a distributed, hardware-anchored capa-
bility that no single party—including the system itself—controls.
The thesis is conditional by design. Arkhe takes no position on whether or when AGI will be achieved;
it takes a strong position on theorder of constructionif it is attempted.
3.2 Principle 1 — Invariants are code; policies are configuration
Arkhe draws a hard line between two kinds of rules [1]:
•Technical invariantsare properties the code must guarantee ineveryreachable state. They are
identified (T-A1,T-S2,T-K1, . . . ), verified at runtime, and their violation triggers panic, degraded
mode, or rejection—never silent continuation. Changing an invariant requires a code change and
re-audit.
•Governance policiesare rules humans decide: quorum sizes, timelock durations, attestation
intervals, latency SLAs. They are identified (P-G1,P-S2, . . . ), live in configuration or in a Datalog
constitution, and can be changed through a governed amendment process without recompilation.
The separation is itself an anti-corruption device: it prevents policy debates from being smuggled
into code (where they escape governance) and prevents engineering constraints from being weakened
by configuration (where they escape audit).
3.3 Principle 2 — Fail closed
Every enforcement point in Arkhe defaults to denial. A credential that cannot be verified blocks the
action. A rule the policy engine cannot decide is rejected at parse time—the constitution is expressed
in decidable logic (Datalog) precisely so that “the engine could not evaluate this” is impossible by con-
struction (invariantT-G3). A trusted-execution environment whose remote attestation fails drops the
6

=== PAGINA 7 ===
system into degraded mode (T-K2). Availability is sacrificed to integrity at every such point, deliber-
ately.
3.4 Principle 3 — Prove execution, not reasoning
Attestation in Arkhe is scoped to deterministic computation: the system can prove that a committed
DecisionGraph—a hashed, step-wise plan with expected input/output hashes—was executed faithfully
against a known state root (T-A2). It cannot prove that the plan waswise. This scoping is a direct
application of the constitutional principle: promising cryptographic proof of LLM reasoning would be
a false promise, so the architecture refuses to make it, and routes semantic risk to the governance and
oversight layers instead [1, 2].
3.5 Principle 4 — No single point of trust, including insiders
No privileged operation may depend on a single key, machine, person, or organization. The kill switch
requires a threshold of distinct signature shards (T-K1) held across a mandated geographic and jurisdic-
tional spread (policyP-S2), implemented over FROST threshold Schnorr signatures [32]. Long-term
keys never reside whole in unprotected memory (T-K3) and, notably, never inside the TEE that hosts
the safety logic—so that even a TEE side-channel compromise yields no signing capability (Section 6,
T-04). Constitutional changes require quorum plus a timelock of at least seven days (T-G1), making
silent rule changes structurally impossible. And the threat model (Section 6) treats the project’s own
engineers, dependencies, and key holders as adversaries to be constrained, not colleagues to be trusted.
3.6 Principle 5 — Everything observable, everything evidenced
Every public API emits structured telemetry with trace identifiers (T-O1); every event carries causa-
tion and correlation metadata (T-E2); every consequential decision, proof, and payment is appended
to a content-addressed, append-only log whose Merkle roots make tampering detectable and history
replayable (T-S1–T-S3). Auditability is not a report generated after the fact; it is a byproduct of the only
way the system is able to operate.
4 The Trust Infrastructure: Canonical Architecture
4.1 Seven crates and an event bus
The canonical architecture [1] fixes seven Rust crates plus one event bus, arranged in three layers
(Table 1). Each crate carries a one-sentence invariant that summarizes its contract. The earlier four-crate
proposal (attestation, storage, governance, security) was rejected because it hid coupling—attestation
calling storage directly to persist proofs—and left identity and observability as second-class, scattered
concerns.
4.2 Communication discipline
Four rules govern inter-crate structure, and the first is enforced by the compiler:
1.Domain crates never import each other.All domain-to-domain communication flows through
arkhe-event-busas typed publish/subscribe events. A direct import fails compilation and
blocks CI (invariantT-E1).
2. Infrastructure crates are consumed by domain crates, never the reverse.
3. Security crates are invoked by domain logic (e.g., to trigger the kill switch) but contain no domain
knowledge.
4. Observability is transversal: injected into every crate via tracing.

4.5 Implementation status and dependency discipline
The Arkhe workspace is developed under a compiler-enforced layered dependency graph and, as of
July 2026, comprises on the order of eighteen verified crates spanning the canonical seven plus sup-
porting components (content-addressed storage with convergent encoding and Merkle-tree tamper de-
tection; interfaces-only binary provenance that refuses to fabricate provenance data; interpretability
tooling; a web gateway over TLS 1.3 in progress). The phased implementation roadmap [1] is delib-
erately conservative: Phase 1 (2026–2027) delivers storage, identity, event bus, and observability with
local IPFS and in-memory pub/sub; Phase 2 (2027–2028) adds FROST 3-of-5 threshold signatures [32]
and hash/signature attestation while explicitly labeling zero-knowledge proof generation asresearch;
TEEs reach production no earlier than Phase 4 (2030–2032), reflecting their vulnerability history; and
BFT consensus stays off the hot path until and unless latency permits. Where a milestone depends on
immature technology, the roadmap says “pilot” or “research,” not “delivered.”

5.1 Interface design rules
All cross-crate contracts are expressed as Rust traits under five rules [1]: all I/O is asynchronous

5.3 Verification methodology
Arkhe’s verification stack is layered to match its claims. At the bottom, thecompilerenforces the depen-
dency architecture (a domain-to-domain import cannot build). Above it,tests—including integration
tests that check observability instrumentation and property tests over storage (deduplication, tamper
detection, replay determinism)—gate CI. Above that,runtime checksimplement the T-invariants: CID
recomputation on reads, Merkle-root comparison on replay, signature verification on every credential
use, threshold counting on every kill-switch attempt. At the top,cryptographic evidence—hashes, signa-
tures, and eventually attestation proofs—allows parties outside the system to re-verify without trusting
it. Each layer catches what the layer below cannot express, and no claim in the architecture is supposed
to rest on a layer weaker than the claim requires.
6 Threat Model v2: Taking the Insider Seriously
6.1 Methodology
The threat model [1] follows STRIDE extended with explicit internal-adversary analysis. Each of
thirteen threats is characterized by vector, impact, mitigation, adversary category (external, internal-
privileged, collusion, supply chain, hardware, governance), owning crate, and the technical invariant
that anchors the mitigation. Two properties distinguish it from typical models in this space: it refuses
to assume the project’s own staff, dependencies, and governance participants are honest, and it records
unmitigated threats as explicit roadmap debt (“requires. . . ”) rather than omitting them.
6.2 The thirteen threats
External and environmental. T-01, LLM hallucination attack: a model emits aDecisionGraph
that is computationally valid but semantically malicious (data destruction framed as storage optimiza-
tion); mitigation is the explicit scoping of proofs (a proof attests execution, never semantics; Section 3.4)
combined with risk-tiered policy that blocks high-risk actions regardless of proof.T-02, trusted-setup
compromiseof zk-SNARKs; mitigation is migration toward transparent (STARK-class) or universal-
setup systems plus multi-verifier checking.T-05, storage censorshipby providers refusing to pin
audit logs; mitigation is mandatory multi-provider replication with periodic integrity verification and
anchoring of final hashes on an immutable layer.T-06, credential leak and reuse; mitigation isbound
credentials cryptographically tied to the hash of the specific action proposal, so a stolen VC fails verifi-
cation against any other action—an instance of the scope-attenuated delegation the agent-infrastructure
literature calls for [27, 29].T-11, Sybil attackson BFT (DID-gated validator identity).T-12, man-in-
the-middle between crates(mTLS with 24-hour certificates plus per-event Ed25519 signatures verified
by the bus).T-13, replay against the event log(monotonic sequence numbers, Merkle roots, signed
events, checkpoint fencing).
Internal-privileged. T-07, malicious engineer with commit accessinserting a Safety-Kernel back-
door. Mitigations are both technical—reproducible builds with publicly auditable binary hashes, manda-
tory multi-party review (3+ independent reviewers) on safety-critical paths, SBOM per release, periodic
external audit—and organizational: separation of duties between authorship and deployment, and a two-
person rule for Safety-Kernel deploys.
Collusion. T-03, malicious or negligent key holder(refusal to sign a necessary kill switch, or
key loss); mitigation includes proactive secret sharing (shard refresh without changing the pub-
lic key) and social-recovery escrow.T-09, collusion among shard holders(threshold reached
by coordinated bad actors, or blocked by coordinated refusal); mitigations combine mandated geo-
graphic/jurisdictional/institutional diversity, logging and alerting on same-entity shard assembly, holder
rotation, and good-faith legal protection for holders who activate honestly.

7 The Safety Kernel and the Cognitive-Architecture Roadmap
This section describes the prescriptive capability roadmap [2]. Unless stated otherwise, everything in it
is a specification with quantitative targets, not a report of built systems. Quantitative claims about the
July 2026 state of the art originate in the plan’s internal assessment and should be read as the authors’
estimates.
7.1 Four pillars
The plan’s central thesis is that AGI requires theintegrationof four pillars, none sufficient alone:
(1) multimodal perception, whose foundations it treats as consolidated in the 2024–2026 generation
of models; (2) reasoning and planning, in active development (2026–2032), where test-time compute
scaling must generalize beyond mathematics and drop in cost by more than an order of magnitude;
(3) continual learning (2026–2038), the critical unsolved problem of acquiring new skills without catas-
trophic forgetting [9]; and (4) alignment and safety, treated not as a pillar among equals but as aprecon-
ditionfor the other three.
7.2 The cognitive message protocol
Everything in the cognitive architecture communicates through a typed, versionedMessage<T>struc-
ture carrying: a content union (embedding, symbol, or probability distribution) with a Blake3 content
hash;epistemic metadata—a calibrated certainty value, confidence intervals, an explicitSourceenum
(perception; inference with a Merkle-rooted reasoning trace; memory with retrieval confidence; or ex-
ternal input with a DID signature), and a validity scope; a dependency DAG of presupposed beliefs
enabling provenance-tracing of any conclusion back to its origins; and an execution-control block with
anActionBudget(maximum FLOPs, maximum wall-clock, and a rollback checkpoint) plus a safety-
clearance level. Four capabilities fall out of this design: full provenance audit, principled conflict
detection when modules disagree, uncertainty propagation along dependency chains (decisions require

certainty above a domain-configurable threshold), and hard sandboxing of actions against unbounded
execution.
7.3 Layered architecture with latency contracts
The architecture is specified in six layers, each with explicit latency SLAs:Layer 0, multimodal percep-
tion with cross-modal fusion (<50 ms streaming);Layer 1, the world model—acausaljoint-embedding
predictive architecture (“C-JEPA”) extending the JEPA line [12, 13, 14] with approximate causal dis-
covery in latent space (PC-algorithm plus differentiable NOTEARS refinement), counterfactual simu-
lation via do-calculus [16, 17], and multi-scale temporal prediction [15], alongside intuitive-physics
and theory-of-mind models;Layer 2, a reasoning engine combining neural pattern-matching, symbolic
search and planning, and probabilistic inference through a neuro-symbolic integration layer;Layer 3,
episodic, semantic, and procedural memory stores;Layer 4, the learning core, including the continual-
learning architecture below; andLayer 5, action and tool use (APIs, sandboxed code execution, browser
and robotics control), with tool creation, composition, and discovery as learned capabilities.
7.4 CL-4L: the continual-learning proposal
The plan’s answer to the plasticity–stability–scalability trilemma is a four-layer hybrid (“CL-4L”): a
frozen base modelfor stability;task-specific adapters(LoRA-family [10]) with a learned router for
plasticity—including an explicit “novel task” signal when routing confidence collapses;episodic mem-
ory with experience replay[11] for rare or critical experiences; and periodicsleep-phase consolidation,
in which the base model is re-distilled from itself-plus-adapters on a mixture of old and new tasks,
accepted only if retention on old tasks stays within a 5% degradation budget, with rejection-and-retry
under stronger regularization otherwise. Consolidation is scheduled (per∼1,000 tasks or monthly), not
online.
7.5 The Safety Kernel
The Safety Kernel is the component where the cognitive roadmap and the trust infrastructure meet: a
mandatory interposition layer, inspired by verified microkernels [25], through whicheveryproposed
action must pass. It stacks four defenses:
1.Policy Engine— the explicit constitution, compiled from decidable Datalog, evaluated in under
10 ms, fail-closed: what no rule permits is blocked. This is the same governance machinery
specified in Section 4 (T-G1–T-G3), now sitting on the action path of a cognitive system.
2.Risk Classifier— neural-plus-symbolic classification of each action into five risk levels; Critical
always requires a human in the loop.
3.Impact Simulator— counterfactual rollout of the proposed action inside the world model before
real execution, predicting second- and third-order effects; predicted harm above threshold blocks
the action (<500 ms for simple simulations).
4.Human-in-the-Loop— an approval interface for critical actions with timeout and escalation
(junior→senior→ethics board).
Around the stack sit three further mechanisms.Circuit monitoring: SAE-based detectors [18, 19]
watch for activation of risk-associated features (deception, power-seeking, manipulation), and sustained
activation places the system in quarantine, where every action requires human approval; feature ac-
tivations are logged immutably for forensics.Kill switch and reversion: a hardware-plus-software
stop signal targeting sub-100 ms interruption, not deactivatable by the system itself, backed by the
threshold-shard scheme of Section 4; all mutable state is Merkle-versioned, enabling rollback to any
checkpoint, after which the system runs read-only pending audit.Immutable decision logging: every
permit/block/HITL outcome is appended to the evidence log with its rollback checkpoint reference.

7.6 Roadmap and metrics, 2026–2045
The roadmap defines five phases, each with falsifiable target metrics—a deliberate design choice, since
a roadmap without failure conditions is unfalsifiable marketing. In compressed form:Phase 2 (2026–
2028)targets generalized test-time reasoning at greatly reduced cost, a v1 causal world model, ma-
ture neuro-symbolic program synthesis, and human-level performance on the second-generation ARC
benchmark [4], alongside a>95% red-team block rate.Phase 3 (2028–2032)targets persistent-memory
agents (90% retention across 1,000 sequential tasks), continual learning meeting the 95%-retention
bar, self-directed learning in unknown environments, autonomous tool discovery,>99% adversarial
robustness, and kill-switch latency under 10 ms.Phase 4 (2032–2038)targets robust compositional
generalization, order-of-magnitude few-shot efficiency gains, causal world models capable of gen-
erating testable scientific hypotheses, and formal-proof coverage of critical safety properties above
50%.Phase 5 (2038–2045)targets human-comparable skill-acquisition efficiency, autonomous scien-
tific discovery at publishable quality, recursive self-improvement under a hard rate cap (<5%/month)
with verifiable bounds and reversibility, and formally verified or rigorously bounded alignment (target
P(harm)<10 −6), with the human-oversight ratio falling only as verification coverage rises.
The plan pairs each major technical risk with an engineering mitigation and a fallback: catastrophic
forgetting (CL-4L, with learning paused if retention drops below 90%); hallucinated reasoning chains
(process supervision [8], symbolic verification of critical steps, SAE confabulation detection, HITL fall-
back); tool-use harm (the full Safety Kernel plus sandboxed execution); uncontrolled self-improvement
(hard rate caps, hardware kill switch, 24-hour checkpointing, independent monthly audit). It likewise
addresses alignment-specific risks—deceptive alignment [21], goal misgeneralization, power-seeking,
value lock-in—and socioeconomic risks (labor displacement, power concentration, military use, indus-
trialized disinformation) with named mitigations, including provenance standards (C2PA) and crypto-
graphic watermarking for generated content, and an explicit stance that no single entity should control
a dominant share of AGI-relevant compute.
7.7 Relationship between roadmap and infrastructure
The dependency between Sections 4–6 and this section is directional and deliberate. The Safety Kernel
presupposes the trust infrastructure: its constitution is the governed Datalog ofarkhe-governance; its
decision log is the append-only Merkle store ofarkhe-storage; its kill switch is the threshold scheme
ofarkhe-security; its identities and tool credentials arearkhe-identityVCs; its telemetry is
arkhe-observability. The reverse is not true—the infrastructure stands alone and is useful today
for governing narrow agents. That asymmetry is the Arkhe thesis in engineering form:the foundation
must not depend on the thing it is meant to constrain.
8 The Economic Layer: Programmatic Commerce under x402
Status: proposed (ADR-0002, July 2026). Designed and threat-modeled; not yet in production[3].
8.1 Motivation
Autonomous agents that consume paid resources today do so through human-provisioned accounts and
API keys—an arrangement that blurs accountability (whose key? whose budget? whose liability?) and
does not scale to machine-speed, machine-count commerce. The x402 protocol (Section 2.4) removes
the account layer: agents pay per request, in stablecoins, over plain HTTP [33, 34]. ADR-0002 adopts
x402 as Arkhe’s native payment layer with one decisive twist:payment is a governance constraint,
not a bolt-on gate.The transformation the ADR names is from trust as a compliance property to trust
as aneconomicone—actions are not merely authorized; they are priced, paid, verified, and evidenced.

8.2 Design principles
Five principles govern the integration:facilitator-agnosticism(the newarkhe-x402crate works with
any facilitator, hosted or local, so no single settlement provider becomes a trusted party);nonce isola-
tion(replay protection is Arkhe’s own responsibility, inarkhe-x402::noncewith a persistent store,

its); andevent-auditability(each stage—requested, verified, settled, failed—publishes onarkhe-bus).
8.3 The governed payment flow
An agent request arrives carrying three things: an mTLS client certificate, an Arkhe VC header, and an
x402PAYMENT-SIGNATUREheader containing the signed payment payload (scheme, CAIP-2 network
identifier, token, recipient, amount, nonce, signature). The gateway then executes a strict sequence:
1.Identity— resolve the DID, verify the VC, bind the certificate CN to the credential.
2.Payment parse and nonce check— reject any reused nonce.
3.Governance—evaluate_actionover the VCandthe payment proof, enforcing constraints
such as maximum amount and permitted network.
4.Verification— via facilitator/verifyor local cryptographic verification.
5.Execution of the work.
6.Settlement—/settle, yielding a transaction hash, deliberatelyafterthe work (pay-after-
delivery, so settlement without delivery is structurally excluded).
7.Event publication(PaymentSettled).
8.Evidence append— VC, payment signature, and settlement receipt chained under Blake3 in the
append-only log.
9. Response with thePAYMENT-RESPONSEreceipt header.
Wallet addresses are mapped to DIDs, so economic activity and identity share one audit trail.
8.4 Threat model of the economic layer
The ADR’s threat table extends Section 6 into the payment domain: nonce replay (persistent TTL store);
forged signatures (facilitator or localk256verification plus VC signature checks); expired credentials
(policy rejection onvalid_until); amount mismatch (credential-embeddedmax_amountceilings);
facilitator compromise (local verification fallback and multi-facilitator support); settlement-without-
delivery (excluded by flow ordering); and evidence tampering (the Blake3 hash chain). Scope exclu-
sions are recorded with the same honesty as elsewhere: credit-based billing, on-chain escrow, fiat rails,
and multi-signature agent wallets are explicitly out of scope for v1. The acknowledged costs are depen-
dence on blockchain infrastructure (mitigated by L2 settlement) and two to three additional round-trips
per paid request.
8.5 Why an economic layer belongs in a trust infrastructure
Three reasons. First,accountability: a payment is a costly, signed, timestamped commitment—a natural
anti-Sybil and anti-abuse primitive that complements credentials. Second,auditability: when every
resource consumption event is a settled transaction bound to a DID and an action hash, the question
“what did this agent do, for whom, at what cost?” has a cryptographic answer. Third,alignment of

9 Governance and Regulatory Alignment
9.1 Governance architecture
Arkhe’s governance stack is constitutional in the literal sense: a machine-readable constitution in de-
cidable logic, amendable only through proposal, quorum, and timelock (T-G1,P-G1–P-G3); an ethics
board holding both amendment votes and kill-switch shards under mandated diversity requirements
(P-S2); and a strict channel separation wherebyvalueslive in the constitution (governed, versioned,
hash-addressed) andmechanismslive in code (audited, invariant-checked). The timelock deserves em-
phasis: it converts governance capture from an instantaneous attack into one that must survive at least
seven days of public visibility, alerting, and counter-action.
9.2 Mapping to the EU AI Act
The EU AI Act’s obligations for high-risk systems—risk management systems, logging and traceability,
human oversight, robustness, and post-market monitoring—apply on a staggered timeline now extend-
ing into December 2027 and August 2028 following the Digital Omnibus agreement of May 2026
[37, 38]. Arkhe’s relevant property is that these obligations are dischargedby construction: record-
keeping is the append-only evidence log (T-S1–T-S3) rather than a bolt-on logger; human oversight
is the HITL layer with risk-tiered mandatory escalation; traceability is the causation-carrying event
bus (T-E2); and the transparency/marking obligations for synthetic content align with the roadmap’s
provenance-and-watermarking commitments (Section 7.6). An architecture whose only mode of opera-
tion produces the compliance artifacts is easier to audit than one that produces them on request.
9.3 Mapping to Brazil’s PL 2338 and the ANPD
Brazil’s PL 2338/2023, Senate-approved in December 2024 and pending in the Chamber as of mid-
2026, establishes a risk-tiered regime with rights to information, explanation, contestation, and human
review; algorithmic-impact assessments for high-risk systems; an enforcement system coordinated by
the ANPD; regulatory sandboxes; and sanctions up to R$50 million per violation [39, 40]. Arkhe’s
evidence log directly supports the explanation and contestation rights (every consequential decision
is reconstructible with its inputs, credentials, verdict, and rationale); the risk-tier machinery of the
PolicyEnginemirrors the bill’s classification structure; and the sandbox provisions align with Arkhe’s
phased, pilot-first deployment posture. We note one acknowledged gap on the deployment side: a
specified-but-unimplemented anonymization/PII-scrubbing component, treated as a blocker for data-
protection-sensitive public-sector deployments—recorded here, in keeping with the project’s practice,
as debt rather than omitted.
9.4 Institutional posture
The plan’s governance chapter [2] situates Arkhe within the broader ecosystem—academic safety re-
search, standards bodies (IEEE, ISO/IEC, W3C for DID/VC [30, 31]), international coordination frame-
works, and deliberative public engagement—and takes explicit positions against capability concentra-
tion (no entity controlling a dominant share of AGI compute; safety-kernel and constitution designs
as open standards) and for external auditability (independent audits, reproducible builds, public binary
hashes). These are policy positions of the authors, stated as such.

10.2 Known limitations
Semantic risk is not proven away.The central honest limitation, stated in Section 3.4 and threat
T-01: Arkhe proves execution fidelity, never plan wisdom. A perfectly attested execution of a malicious
plan is caught—if it is caught—by policy, risk classification, simulation, and humans, all of which are
fallible.
Hardware trust is bounded, not eliminated.TEEs have a documented side-channel history; Arkhe’s
response (no keys in the enclave, continuous attestation, degraded-mode failure) reduces the blast radius
of compromise but does not restore the enclave’s confidentiality guarantee.
Governance is only as diverse as its participants.Threshold cryptography converts “trust one party”
into “trust thatkofndo not collude”; the diversity policies (P-S2, rotation, escrow) raise the cost of
collusion but cannot make it impossible.
External dependencies remain.The economic layer depends on facilitators, RPC infrastructure, and
L2 liveness; storage persistence depends on external pinning networks; the supply-chain mitigations for
the project’s own dependencies (T-08) are partially open items.
Roadmap numbers are targets, not measurements.All Phase 2–5 metrics in Section 7.6, and the
state-of-the-art estimates in the underlying plan, are the authors’ planning estimates. Several of the
roadmap’s load-bearing research bets—latent-space causal discovery at scale, consolidation without
forgetting at the stated retention bar, SAE coverage sufficient for reliable risk-feature monitoring, for-
mal verification of nontrivial safety properties—may fail, and the roadmap’s fallback clauses (pause
learning, tighten oversight, reduce scope) exist precisely because they may.

11 Conclusion
The argument of this monograph can be compressed into one sentence:in the construction of general
artificial intelligence, the order of operations is a safety property.Arkhe’s contribution is not a new
learning algorithm or a capability result; it is an insistence, backed by working code where it exists and
by honest labels where it does not, that identity, attestation, immutable evidence, decidable governed
policy, distributed shutdown, and priced accountability constitute the layer that must existfirst—and a
demonstration, at the level of compilable interfaces, runtime invariants, and an adversarial threat model
that includes the builders themselves, of what that layer concretely looks like.
The project’s deepest commitment is epistemic rather than architectural. A trust infrastructure built
by a team that tolerates unverified claims about its own system is a contradiction; Arkhe therefore
binds itself to the same standard it imposes on the machines—claims count when the compiler, the
tests, and the hashes say so, and everything else is labeled a hypothesis. Whether the 2045 horizon
of the roadmap is reached, and whether the systems built along the way deserve the word “general,”
are questions this monograph deliberately leaves open. What it does not leave open is the standard by
which any such claim should eventually be judged: mechanically, independently, and against evidence
that no one—including the system, and including its authors—can quietly rewrite.
