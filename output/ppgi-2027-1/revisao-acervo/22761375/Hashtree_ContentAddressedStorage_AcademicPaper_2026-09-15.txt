 Abstract
Centralized content delivery networks and cloud object stores concentrate publication, authentication, and
distribution in a small number of operators, and the global outages of 2021–2025 demonstrated how quickly that
concentration propagates failure to the application layer. This paper presents an architectural and comparative
analysis of Hashtree, an MIT-licensed storage system that couples content-addressed Merkle trees with
content-hash-key (CHK) encryption, Nostr-anchored mutable references of the form npub/tree/path, and
peer-to-peer distribution over the Federated Interoperable Peering System (FIPS), WebRTC data channels, and
Blossom blob servers. Using documentary analysis of specifications, package registries, and the reference
implementation, the study reconstructs the write path, the per-block read-path cascade, and the authorization
model, and situates Hashtree within the lineage of Freenet/Hyphanet, Venti, Git, IPFS, and BitTorrent v2. The
analysis suggests that the design delivers meaningful properties — end-to-end verification,
deduplication-compatible encryption, and self-certifying addressing that requires no DNS, certificate authorities,
or accounts — while inheriting known limitations of convergent-style encryption, depending on unremunerated
seeding for availability, and deferring content governance to server operators and social-graph heuristics. The
demand-scaled distribution claims appear plausible for popular content but not for the long tail. The paper
contributes a structured comparative matrix, a threat-and-availability assessment, and a research agenda spanning
measurement, formal transport verification, and privacy-preserving deduplication.
Keywords: content-addressed storage; Merkle trees; convergent encryption; content hash keys (CHK); Nostr;
peer-to-peer networks; decentralized content delivery

1. Introduction
 On three occasions in the last fifty-three months, the operational fragility of concentrated
web infrastructure became visible to non-specialists. On 8 June 2021, a single software defect
propagated through the configuration service of the content delivery network (CDN) Fastly and
removed approximately 85% of its traffic, including major news sites, e-commerce platforms, and
public-sector portals, for much of an hour (Fastly, 2021; ThousandEyes, 2021). On 19 October
2025, an empty DNS record for the DynamoDB API endpoint in the AWS US-EAST-1 region
cascaded across dependent services and temporarily disabled large parts of the consumer internet
(Amazon Web Services, 2025; Pragmatic Engineer, 2025). On 18 November 2025, Cloudflare
disclosed a global outage that again disrupted dashboards, gateways, and downstream applications
(Cloudflare, 2025; ThousandEyes, 2025). These incidents did not involve data loss, and in each
case recovery was measured in minutes to hours; what they exposed instead is a structural property:
when publication, authentication, and delivery are leased from one vendor, that vendor's worst hour
becomes everyone's worst hour.
 The academic and free-software communities have proposed structural alternatives for
decades: Merkle trees for verifiable content addressing (Merkle, 1987), archival write-once stores
such as Venti (Quinlan & Dorward, 2002), anonymous storage networks such as Freenet, now
maintained as Hyphanet (Clarke et al., 2001; Clarke et al., 2002), distributed hash tables such as
Kademlia (Maymounkov & Mazières, 2002), and generalized content-addressed hypermedia
systems such as IPFS (Benet, 2014). Nevertheless, the deployment center of gravity for code
hosting, media distribution, and web publication remains centralized. The reasons are well
understood and are not purely technical: incumbent platforms bundle naming, discovery, payments,
moderation, and reliable availability into one contract, and decentralized substitutes have
historically asked users to sacrifice one or more of those functions.
 Hashtree is a recent open-source attempt to reassemble that bundle without a central
operator. The project, published under the MIT license and under active development (1,880
commits and 72 tags at the time of access, with release 0.2.148 dated 10 September 2026),
describes itself as content-addressed storage, git transport, and an application runtime on Nostr, in
which data is chunked, CHK-encrypted by default, and fetchable from Blossom-compatible storage,
FIPS peers, or a local daemon (Hashtree, 2026b). Merkle roots can be published to Nostr relays to
obtain mutable npub/tree/path addresses, and a git remote helper allows push and clone over the
same fabric (Hashtree, 2026a, 2026e). Production usage is reported for video streaming at
video.iris.to, which the project documents as serving content over Hashtree rather than a
conventional CDN (Hashtree, 2026a).
 This paper asks three questions. First, does the architecture cohere: do the merkle data
model, the CHK encryption layer, the Nostr resolution layer, and the FIPS/Blossom/WebRTC
transport layer compose into a system whose guarantees are stronger than its parts? Second, what
does the design cost: which security, privacy, availability, and governance trade-offs are intrinsic to
the approach, and which are incidental? Third, how does Hashtree compare with the systems from
which it borrows — and with the centralized fabric it aims to replace? The central thesis is that

2. Background and Related Systems
2.1 Content-Addressed Storage and Merkle Trees
 A content-addressed store identifies data by the cryptographic hash of its contents rather
than by a mutable name, so that any reader can verify integrity by recomputing the identifier and
any two identical objects collapse to the same address. Merkle introduced the tree-shaped
generalization in the context of digital signatures: leaves carry hashes of data blocks, interior nodes
carry hashes of their children, and a single root commits to the entire dataset, enabling membership
proofs of logarithmic size (Merkle, 1987). Content-addressed storage (CAS) matured in archival
systems: Venti used the hash of block contents as its write-once identifier across a network storage
service, demonstrating that deduplication and immutable provenance follow naturally (Quinlan &
Dorward, 2002). The same discipline underlies modern version control: Git names every object —
blob, tree, commit — by its cryptographic hash, which makes repository history a Merkle DAG and
enables safe peer-to-peer exchange of objects (Chacon & Straub, 2014).
 Two refinements matter for what follows. Content-defined chunking, introduced by LBFS,
cuts files at content-dependent offsets so that insertions and deletions only reshuffle chunks locally
rather than invalidating the remainder of a file; Muthitacharoen, Chen, and Mazières (2001) showed
that Rabin-fingerprint breakpoints reduce transferred bandwidth by an order of magnitude on
typical edits. Second, BitTorrent v2 replaced flat piece hashes with per-file Merkle trees over
SHA-256 so that a torrent's integrity metadata scales gracefully with file size and hybrid swarms
remain verifiable (BEP 52; Bittorrent.org, 2021). IPFS generalized both ideas into a
transport-independent Merkle DAG with content identifiers (CIDs), positioning CAS as a substrate
for the web itself (Benet, 2014). Hashtree's data model — chunking, hashing, and tree construction
— sits squarely in this lineage, and Section 3.1 makes its specific choices explicit.

2.2 Encrypting for Deduplication: Convergent Encryption and CHK
 Encryption and deduplication pull in opposite directions: encryption randomizes plaintexts,
while deduplication requires identical ciphertexts for identical contents. Convergent encryption
resolves the tension by deriving the data-encryption key from the plaintext itself, typically as its
hash, so identical plaintexts yield identical ciphertexts that a server can deduplicate without reading
them (Douceur, Adya, Bolosky, Simon, & Theimer, 2002). The Freenet storage network
independently operationalized the same idea as the content hash key (CHK): the key under which a
chunk is stored is derived from hashing the content, so fetches verify themselves and no naming
authority is needed (Clarke et al., 2001; Clarke et al., 2002; Hyphanet contributors, 2026). Storer,
Greenan, Long, and Miller (2008) later systematized secure-deduplication designs for storage
systems, including the hash-as-key variant.
 The cost of convergent-style schemes is a well-mapped attack surface. Because the key is a
deterministic function of content, an adversary who guesses a file's contents can test the guess
offline; a malicious storage server can additionally run confirmation-of-file attacks, learning
whether a client owns a target file by observing the deduplication response (Harnik, Pinkas, &
Shulman-Peleg, 2010). Halevi, Harnik, Pinkas, and Shulman-Peleg (2011) proposed proofs of
ownership to blunt the client-side-deduplication variant of the attack, and Bellare, Keelveedhi, and
Ristenpart (2013) formalized message-locked encryption and its limits, including interactive
constructions that require an online server. These results define the honest threat model for any
system whose encryption key is the hash of the plaintext, and Section 4.2 applies them to Hashtree's
CHK layer.
2.3 Nostr, NIP-34, and Blossom
 Nostr is a public-key event protocol in which each user holds a keypair, publishes signed
events of typed kinds, and delegates relay servers for distribution; relays forward but cannot forge,
since signatures bind events to their authors (NIP-01; Nostr NIPs, 2026). The protocol has become
a coordination layer for applications that want publisher-held identity without DNS or certificate
authorities. Two NIPs matter here. NIP-34 defines git collaboration events — repository
announcements, repository state, patches, pull requests, and issues — so that hosting and review
can be conducted over relays rather than through a single forge (Nostr NIPs, 2024). Blossom is a
companion standard: a set of BUD specifications for servers that store and serve blobs addressable
by their SHA-256 digests, giving Nostr applications a conventional HTTP storage tier without
conventional account systems (Blossom contributors, 2025). Hashtree's design places Nostr relays
and Blossom servers at exactly these two coordination points, as Section 3.4 details.
2.4 The FIPS Peer Transport
 Beneath Hashtree's storage semantics runs a mesh transport called FIPS. Project design
documentation expands the name as the Federated Interoperable Peering System and describes a
self-organizing encrypted mesh that requires no registrars, central authorities, or administratively
assignable names (FIPS Design Documents, 2026; Sovereign Engineering, 2026). Nodes
authenticate with Nostr keypairs, discover and relay through neighbors without a privileged

 The study is a documentary architectural analysis conducted on 15 September 2026.
Primary sources comprise the project website and its documentation, including the package

3. The Hashtree Architecture
3.1 Data Model: Chunked Merkle Trees and Content Identifiers
 Hashtree organizes all stored content as chunked Merkle trees. Files and directories are read
and written in a streaming fashion, chunk by chunk, without whole-file buffering; each chunk is
encrypted and hashed, and hashes are combined into interior nodes up to a tree root that commits to
the entire object (Hashtree, 2026a). The project's JavaScript core package, @hashtree/core, exposes
the content-hash and nhash primitives together with shared types for content-addressed storage,
which places the identifier algebra at the center of the design rather than in an auxiliary library
(Hashtree, 2026a, 2026d). The root hash plays the role that Git assigns to commit objects and IPFS
assigns to CIDs: a self-authenticating name from which the full object can be retrieved and verified
from any cooperative replica (Chacon & Straub, 2014; Benet, 2014). Figure 1 summarizes the
resulting layering.
 Two properties follow directly from the data model and are worth stating before any
implementation detail. First, verification is local and end-to-end: once a reader holds the root
identifier, every block received from any peer, server, or cache can be checked against the tree, so

no transport is trusted for integrity. Second, addressing is self-certifying and deduplication is
global: identical chunks found in different repositories, uploaded by different users at different
times, produce identical identifiers and can collapse to a single stored copy wherever the storage
tier permits. Both properties are inherited from the CAS lineage rather than invented here; the
architectural question is how Hashtree combines them with encryption and mutable naming, which
the next two subsections address.
3.2 CHK Encryption and Deduplication-Compatible Confidentiality
 By default, chunks are encrypted with what the project calls CHK encryption: the key is the
hash of the plaintext, so the same content always produces the same ciphertext and deduplication
remains possible even on encrypted data (Hashtree, 2026a). This is convergent encryption by
another name, and the lineage matters: the technique was introduced for encrypted network storage
(Douceur et al., 2002), shipped at scale in Freenet's CHK keys (Clarke et al., 2002), and has been
analyzed formally as message-locked encryption (Bellare et al., 2013). The direct benefit is that a
Blossom server or FIPS peer can store and serve ciphertext blocks, participate in global
deduplication, and never need to understand either the user's identity or the plaintext. The
documented visibility modes build on this primitive and are summarized in Table 1: public content
is readable by anyone with the URL; link-visible content is encrypted and readable only by link
holders; private content is encrypted to the owner's key only (Hashtree, 2026a).
 The costs of the primitive are inherited rather than eliminated. A deterministic
key-derivation function leaks equality: any party who holds a chunk identifier can test whether any
other party holds the same chunk, and an adversary who can guess plaintext content can derive keys
and verify guesses offline. Section 4.2 develops the consequences, including the confirmation
attacks characterized by Harnik et al. (2010) and the mitigations proposed by Halevi et al. (2011)
and Bellare et al. (2013). For the present analysis it suffices to note that Hashtree's documentation
presents CHK as confidentiality by default without engaging the known side-channel literature, and
that the gap between those two framings is where an evaluator's attention belongs.
3.3 Mutable References: npub/path Addresses and Nostr-Published Roots
 Immutable trees need a mutable naming layer, and Hashtree obtains it from Nostr rather
than from a naming service. A stable permalink takes the form npub/tree/path, where npub is the
bech32 encoding of a Nostr public key; the latest Merkle root for a tree is published to relays as a
signed event, and resolution means querying relays for the current root of a given npub and path
(Hashtree, 2026a). Because roots are signed events, the mutable layer inherits Nostr's trust model:
relays can lose, delay, or refuse events, but cannot forge a root, and clients can consult multiple
relays and verify signatures locally (Nostr NIPs, 2026). The project's nostr package provides root
resolution and signed tree-snapshot helpers, indicating that snapshot freshness and authenticity are
handled as first-class concerns in the client library (Hashtree, 2026a, 2026d).
 The design choice is best understood against the alternatives. IPFS uses IPNS records or
DNSLink for mutable names, both of which reintroduce infrastructure whose failure modes differ
from the data plane (Benet, 2014); conventional web publishing leans on DNS registrars and

3.4 Transports and Placement: FIPS, WebRTC, Blossom, and the Local Daemon
 Distribution is multi-transport by design. The Rust daemon and CLI (hashtree-cli) maintain
an LMDB-backed local store, optional FUSE mounts, pins, and an adaptive blob router; peers can
be declared statically in configuration as FIPS origin/cache peers with npub identities and UDP
addresses; Nostr relays are likewise configurable (Hashtree, 2026c). Blob transfer over the mesh
uses verified request/response frames over FIPS, and the documentation is explicit about two
boundaries: a share link names the remote provider with a hops-to-live parameter of ten, in the
manner of Hyphanet's request routing, and merely connecting a WebRTC peer never makes it a
blob source — FIPS owns path discovery and authentication (Hashtree, 2026a). Blossom servers
form the conventional HTTP tier of the same fabric; the default configuration ships with cdn.iris.to
as a read server and upload.iris.to as a write server, with optional write-behind replication targets
and a bounded replication queue (Hashtree, 2026c).
 The authorization model is unusual and merits attention. The daemon maintains a local
social-graph store, crawls follow lists (kind-3 events) from relays at startup, and uses follow
distance to control write access to the operator's Blossom server, replacing manual allow-lists with
a social distance threshold; a companion API exposes the computed distance per public key
(Hashtree, 2026c). New identities also seed a bootstrap follow of the project's release-signing
identity so that the graph begins with a usable entrypoint (Hashtree, 2026c). The design is elegant in
that admission control follows the same keypair identity that already secures the data plane, but it
converts a governance problem into a graph-shape problem, and Section 4.2 examines the Sybil
exposure that follows. Payment rails appear at the edges: a Cashu wallet helper ships alongside the
CLI, and the companion mesh-VPN project demonstrates byte-metered exit nodes paid in Bitcoin
through Cashu, suggesting an emerging micro-payment economy for transport services in this
ecosystem (Hashtree, 2026c; mmalmi, 2026).
3.5 The Client Data Plane: Write Path, Read-Path Cascade, and Media Streaming
 The browser data plane is documented as a strict cascade. On the write path, uploads are
stream-read and stream-written chunk by chunk; encrypted chunks are persisted to local IndexedDB
first, the nhash is returned immediately, and upload to configured Blossom write servers proceeds
in the background. On the read path, each needed block is looked up first in the IndexedDB cache;
on a miss, from Blossom read servers; and only then from the provider named by the share link,
with remote hits cached back to IndexedDB (Hashtree, 2026a). Media streaming is handled by a
service worker that intercepts /htree// requests and forwards them to the worker, which serves

Identity is local and self-generated: an auto-created Nostr keypair lives in the configuration
directory, and the htree user command reports the resulting npub (Hashtree, 2026b, 2026c). No
account, DNS name, or certificate appears anywhere in the lifecycle, which is the concrete meaning
of the project's no-gatekeepers claim at the client level (Hashtree, 2026a).
3.6 Git Without GitHub
 The most tangible application of the fabric is code hosting. A remote helper
(git-remote-htree) implements the gitremote-helpers interface so that standard git push and git clone
operate against htree:// URLs: htree://self/name resolves the local identity, while
htree://npub.../name resolves any third party's published tree, making a repository cloneable from
its author's key alone (Hashtree, 2026a, 2026e; Git contributors, 2026). Pull requests and review
ride on NIP-34, which encodes repositories, patches, pull requests, and issues as signed Nostr
events, and the project operates a web application at git.iris.to for that workflow (Nostr NIPs, 2024;
Hashtree, 2026a). The release channel is self-hosted on the same fabric: the installer for the CLI is
distributed through an npub-addressed release path, an instance of the system bootstrapping itself
(Hashtree, 2026a).
 Radicle is the closest prior art and the comparison is instructive. Radicle replicates git
repositories across peer nodes with gossip and seed nodes, using decentralized identities for nodes
and repositories, and deliberately leaves review to clients (Radicle contributors, 2026; LWN.net,
2024). Hashtree instead grafts hosting onto a general-purpose storage fabric whose
mutable-reference and blob-transfer layers it shares with media delivery and application runtime;
git support is one consumer of the CAS rather than the system's reason to exist. The architectural
bet differs accordingly: Radicle optimizes for code-collaboration semantics, while Hashtree
optimizes for a uniform verified-storage substrate, and the web of trust used for write authorization
in Hashtree has no direct counterpart in Radicle's node model. Whether the bet pays off is an
empirical question that Section 4.3 frames in terms of availability economics.

4. System Analysis
4.1 Trust and Verification Model
 Hashtree's security argument begins from the CAS property: content is named by hashes, so
every byte received over any transport — local daemon, FIPS peer, WebRTC peer, Blossom server,
or browser cache — can be verified against the tree root before use (Hashtree, 2026a). Integrity
therefore does not depend on the honesty of any intermediary, only on the preimage resistance of
the hash construction and on the correctness of the client's verification logic. Authenticated naming
likewise reduces to key custody: an npub/tree/path address resolves through signed Nostr events, so
the authority to update a name is exactly the authority of the corresponding private key, and relay

Page 11
misbehavior can affect freshness but not authenticity (Nostr NIPs, 2026; Hashtree, 2026a). In the
vocabulary of self-certifying identifiers, Hashtree sits with Git, Venti, and IPFS on one side of the
ledger, and with DNS- and CA-mediated publishing on the other (Benet, 2014; Chacon & Straub,
2014).
 The trust reduction is real but bounded, and three residuals deserve explicit statement. First,
verification presposes possession of the correct root, which typically arrives through a link, a relay
query, or out-of-band communication; an attacker who controls the channel through which the root
is first communicated can mount a substitution attack until the user cross-checks the root
fingerprint. Second, verification logic is implemented in clients across at least two runtimes (Rust
daemon and browser worker), and implementation divergence is a classic source of verification
gaps, as the web-PKI literature has repeatedly shown. Third, hash-function agility is unstated in the
documentation reviewed; a system whose addresses are hashes must plan for hash-break and
upgrade events the way BitTorrent v2 fixed SHA-256 at the outset (Bittorrent.org, 2021). None of
these residuals is unique to Hashtree, but all three belong in any deployment threat model.
4.2 Security Analysis
 The CHK layer inherits the convergent-encryption attack surface. Deterministic key
derivation means chunk equality is public: any observer of an identifier can confirm possession of
the corresponding content elsewhere, and any party who can guess high-entropy-deficient content
— a well-known installer image, a popular license file, a leaked document — can derive the key
offline and test the guess directly (Douceur et al., 2002). Against storage operators, the
confirmation attacks of Harnik et al. (2010) apply nearly verbatim: a curious Blossom server can
time deduplication responses to learn whether a client already holds a target file, and the read-path
cascade's cache-first design adds a browser-side timing oracle for link-holders probing visitor state.
The published mitigations — proofs of ownership (Halevi et al., 2011), interactive message-locked
encryption (Bellare et al., 2013), or simply disabling cross-user deduplication for sensitive classes
(Harnik et al., 2010) — are absent from the documentation reviewed, which treats CHK as a
confidentiality feature. A charitable reading is that Hashtree targets the Freenet threat model, in
which CHK protects availability and naming rather than per-file privacy against global adversaries;
the documentation should say so.
 Capability URLs are the second exposure. Link-visible content is readable by anyone who
holds the link, a bearer-capacity model that is convenient and matches the casual sharing behavior
of the web, but which converts every referrer header, server log, chat archive, and browser history
entry into a potential access-control failure (Hashtree, 2026a). The fragment-based visibility
distinction mitigates accidental indexing — content behind a fragment is not fetchable without the
fragment — yet links leak in practice, and there is no documented revocation mechanism short of
re-uploading content to new keys. Third, relay dependence reappears as a liveness and freshness
attack surface: relays can selectively withhold root announcements to serve stale trees, and although
signatures prevent forgery, nothing in the model obliges relays to serve the latest root. The
documented mitigations — multiple relays, signed snapshots, client-side signature verification —
reduce the attack to partitioning, which is the best any relay-based design can do without a stronger

 The social-graph write authorization introduces a Sybil dimension. Follow distance is
computed from kind-3 contact lists, which any key can fabricate at will: an adversary who creates a

operator, because trust here is attested by edges rather than by scarce resources or verified identity
(Hashtree, 2026c). The classic DHT security literature makes the general point — id-based
admission without a sybil-resistant resource invites identity inflation (Sit & Morris, 2002) — and
the bootstrap follow shipped by default slightly concentrates initial trust in the project's own
identity, a soft centralization worth flagging (Hashtree, 2026c). Finally, operational maturity signals
are encouraging but early: the 0.2.148 release notes describe mesh retry and rekey corrections
(Hashtree, 2026b), which suggests active hardening of exactly the key-rotation and recovery paths
that matter most when accounts are keypairs and there is no customer support desk to reset them.
4.3 Availability and the Economics of Demand-Scaled Distribution
 The project's distribution thesis is that a decentralized CDN gets stronger with demand:
delivery continues over peers and alternate routes during centralized outages, costs do not grow
linearly with popularity, and each successful fetch can add another source for subsequent viewers
(Hashtree, 2026a). The mechanisms reviewed in Section 3 support a qualified version of each
claim. Peer-to-peer swarms do convert popularity into replica count, and fluid models of
BitTorrent-like systems established long ago that service capacity grows with the seeding
population under reciprocity (Qiu & Srikant, 2004), with measurement studies confirming both the
availability benefits and the flash-crowd dynamics (Guo, Chen, Xiao, & Zhang, 2005). Figure 3
renders the underlying analytical intuition: availability rises steeply with the number of reachable
seeders, while publisher cost under seeded distribution grows sub-linearly with demand, in contrast
with the linear egress economics of a conventional CDN.

that Hashtree buys outage-independence and cost decoupling for popular content while re-assuming

 Incentives are the missing institution. BitTorrent's tit-for-tat made reciprocity local and
self-enforcing (Cohen, 2003); Hashtree's seeding is documented as voluntary — the daemon
participates when started, pins retain content, and the social graph gates who may upload, but no
economic mechanism rewards byte-serving (Hashtree, 2026c). The ecosystem's own experiments
point in the direction the literature predicts: the companion VPN ships byte-metered exits paid in
Bitcoin over Cashu, and the CLI includes a Cashu wallet helper, suggesting that
micropayment-denominated transport is anticipated if not yet wired into storage placement
(mmalmi, 2026; Hashtree, 2026c). Production evidence is correspondingly early: the cited
deployment is a video application within the same ecosystem (Hashtree, 2026a), and the
repository's engagement metrics — eleven stars and two forks at the time of access — place the
project in a pre-network-effect stage where its availability claims are prospective rather than
demonstrated at scale (Hashtree, 2026b).
4.4 Comparative Evaluation
 Table 3 positions Hashtree against the systems it descends from and the incumbent it
challenges. Three observations follow from the table. First, Hashtree is the only entry that combines
default encryption with cross-user deduplication and gatekeeper-free naming in a single fabric;
IPFS achieves the CAS and naming properties but leaves encryption to applications, Hyphanet
achieves encryption and anonymity but has never been positioned as an application runtime, and
BitTorrent v2 remains a distribution protocol rather than a storage system (Benet, 2014; Clarke et
al., 2002; Bittorrent.org, 2021). Second, every decentralized entry pays for coordination
somewhere: IPNS records, Hyphanet's routing overhead, Radicle's seed nodes, Hashtree's relays
and project-operated Blossom defaults — the differences are about which dependency is cheapest
to tolerate, not about whether one exists. Third, the conventional CDN remains unmatched on
worst-case latency and on contractual availability guarantees; the 2021–2025 incidents argue for
complementarity rather than replacement, which is exactly the hybrid posture Hashtree's cascade
implements.

5. Counterarguments and Limitations
5.1 Privacy Limits of Hash-Keyed Encryption
 A defender of the design might argue that convergent encryption's weaknesses are
theoretical for this threat model, since link-visible and private content is not advertised and the
mesh has no global view. The counterargument is that equality leakage is not an exotic capability
but a property of the public identifiers themselves: chunk hashes appear in share links, relay events,
and any network observation, so possession correlation is available to anyone with passive visibility
of the fabric (Douceur et al., 2002; Harnik et al., 2010). For high-entropy content the practical risk
is negligible; for low-entropy content — contact lists, configuration files, short documents —
offline guessing is straightforward. The literature's mitigations exist but cost either server
interaction or deduplication (Halevi et al., 2011; Bellare et al., 2013), and adopting them is a design
decision Hashtree has not documented; until then, the correct guidance is that CHK protects against
passive storage parties, not against adversaries who can guess or observe.
5.2 No Gatekeepers, Examined
 The no-gatekeepers claim is accurate at the level of accounts, DNS, and certificates: the
lifecycle from keypair generation to publication requires none of them (Hashtree, 2026a). It
weakens at three other levels. Relay operators are gatekeepers of freshness, since they choose which
roots to serve; Blossom operators are gatekeepers of admission and jurisdiction, since they decide
whose bytes their servers accept and under which legal regime they operate; and the bootstrap
follow plus default read/write servers concentrate initial trust in project-operated infrastructure
(Hashtree, 2026c; Blossom contributors, 2025). None of this restores the credential gatekeeping of
the incumbent web, and pluralism — many relays, many servers — is a structural mitigation the
protocol permits. The fair conclusion is narrower than the slogan: Hashtree replaces identifiable,

accountable gatekeepers with unaccountable, plural, and mostly voluntary ones, which is a different
and not obviously worse failure surface, but not an empty one.
5.3 Availability Caveats and the Incentive Vacuum
 The demand-scaled CDN claim, as Section 4.3 argued, holds for content with active
seeding populations and fails asymptotically for cold content; the named-provider backstop
resolves liveness but reintroduces exactly the single-operator risk profile the project criticizes, at
smaller scale (Hashtree, 2026a, 2026c). A sympathetic reader might respond that early deployments
are seed-funded by enthusiasm, as BitTorrent's were, and that economic layers can be added later
(Cohen, 2003; mmalmi, 2026). The counterargument is asymmetry of expectations: a CDN
advertises worst-case performance under contract, while a swarm's worst case is unbounded in the
tail, and applications that assume swarm-average behavior will observe it as flakiness precisely on
the rare-but-critical requests that define user trust. Measurement is the deciding instrument, and
none is yet public; until swarm-level retrieval distributions for Hashtree content are published, its
availability thesis should be treated as a hypothesis with a plausible mechanism rather than a
demonstrated property.
5.4 Client-Side and Platform Constraints
 The browser data plane inherits the platform's storage semantics. IndexedDB persistence is
subject to storage pressure and eviction policies, service workers are terminated under memory
pressure and lifecycle rules, and neither mechanism offers the durable guarantees of the daemon's
LMDB store; the documented cache-first design is therefore strongest on the desktop and weakest
on mobile browsers, where eviction is aggressive and background networking is throttled
(Hashtree, 2026a). The FUSE mount and daemon path avoid these constraints but require
installation, which re-raises distribution questions that the browser path was designed to avoid
(Hashtree, 2026c). These are constraints of the environment rather than defects of the design, and
the project's dual-plane architecture is a reasonable response; the residual risk is behavioral
divergence between the two planes, which multiplies the verification-implementation surface noted
in Section 4.1.
5.5 Governance, Content Policy, and Regulation
 Removing gatekeepers does not remove liability. Blossom server operators, relay operators,
and application publishers each occupy positions that domestic law already regulates for hosting
and intermediary services; the European Union's Digital Services Act, for instance, imposes
notice-and-action and transparency obligations on hosting services irrespective of their storage
architecture (European Union, 2022). CHK encryption complicates, but does not dissolve, these
duties: operators of ciphertext stores hold less knowledge than conventional hosts, which may
reduce certain liabilities while attracting the attention that encryption-by-default storage always
attracts. The project's social-graph admission control is also a governance choice with political
consequences — web-of-trust admission historically reproduces the biases of the seed community,
and the default bootstrap follow hard-codes one community's entry point (Hashtree, 2026c). None
of these observations is disqualifying; all of them belong in an honest roadmap.

5.6 Methodological Limitations of This Study
 The analysis has boundaries that readers should weigh. It is documentary: the project
website's data-flow descriptions were reviewed as published, the repository and registry pages as
indexed on 15 September 2026, and no binary was executed, no protocol exchange observed, and
no performance measurement attempted; runtime claims — including streaming behavior, daemon
routing, and mesh transport properties — are therefore documentation-conditional. The
documentation itself is incomplete on security-relevant specifics: the nhash construction, chunking
parameters, chunk sizes, and cipher details are not stated in the materials reviewed, which limits
both this analysis and third-party audit generally. Production evidence for CDN independence is a
single in-ecosystem deployment (Hashtree, 2026a). Finally, the project is young and moving
quickly — 1,880 commits with release 0.2.148 in September 2026 — so specific implementation
details cited here may age faster than the architectural analysis, which is anchored in properties that
the CAS, CHK, and Nostr substrates guarantee independently of release numbers.
6. Conclusion and Implications
 Hashtree is best read as a synthesis experiment: take the verification discipline of
content-addressed storage, the deduplication-compatible confidentiality of CHK encryption, the
publisher-held identity of Nostr, and the transport pluralism of FIPS, WebRTC, and Blossom, and
ask whether the composition holds together as a general storage and delivery fabric. On the
evidence reviewed, the composition holds. The data model guarantees end-to-end verification that
no intermediary can subvert; the naming layer inherits authentication from signatures and freshness
pluralism from relays; the cascade gives applications a graceful degradation path from local cache
to swarm to named provider; and the git surface demonstrates that the fabric can carry one of the
web's most demanding workloads without a forge. The costs are equally clear: convergent
encryption's side channels are inherited and unmitigated as documented; availability for cold
content depends on backstops that partially recentralize; seeding is unpaid; and governance
questions are displaced into graph shape and operator jurisdiction rather than resolved.
 Four research directions follow. First, measurement: public retrieval-latency and
availability distributions for real workloads, particularly the long tail, would convert the
demand-scaled-CDN hypothesis into an evidence-based claim; the IPFS experience shows both the
feasibility and the value of such studies (IPFS Docs, 2026). Second, privacy engineering:
integrating proofs of ownership or message-locked encryption with explicit deduplication policy
would let the system make differentiated promises for public, link-visible, and private content
(Halevi et al., 2011; Bellare et al., 2013). Third, formal transport verification: the FIPS mesh is the
least-specified layer with the largest trust surface, and machine-checked session-type or symbolic
models of its authenticated frames would be a disproportionate contribution for their cost. Fourth,
incentive design: the ecosystem's Cashu experiments suggest a path from voluntary seeding to
metered reciprocity, and the open question is whether such a layer can be added without recreating
the concentrated markets that peer-to-peer systems exist to avoid (mmalmi, 2026).

 The broader implication concerns autonomous agents. Systems that verify content by hash,
name resources by public key, and pay for transport at the margin are precisely the infrastructure
that machine-scale traffic requires, because agents cannot hold accounts everywhere but can hold
keypairs, and cannot read terms of service at every hop but can verify every byte (Hashtree, 2026a;
Nostr NIPs, 2026). If the measurement and incentive questions are answered well, fabrics of this
kind are plausible default substrates for agentic publication and exchange; if they are answered
poorly, Hashtree will remain what it is today — a technically coherent, honestly licensed,
early-stage demonstration that the post-CDN design space is larger than the incidents of 2021–2025
made it look. Either outcome is informative, and the project has already earned the second-order
contribution of making the trade-offs explicit enough to argue about.
