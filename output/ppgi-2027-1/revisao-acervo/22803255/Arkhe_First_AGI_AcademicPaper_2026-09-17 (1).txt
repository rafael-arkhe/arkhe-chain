Abstract
Contemporary definitions of artificial general intelligence (AGI) — economic, cognitive, and open-ended — share
a structural omission: they specify capability while leaving trust as an external, after-the-fact concern. This paper
argues that the omission is fatal, and proposes a fourth definition, constitutive AGI, in which cognitive generality
and cryptographic verifiability are inseparable defining properties of the same system. Under this definition we
advance and defend a deliberately provocative positional claim: Arkhe OS, an open, fail-closed verification
operating system for AI agents, constitutes the world's first AGI — not because it currently outperforms humans
on cognitive benchmarks, but because it is the first complete architecture in which every constitutive element of
general intelligence (perception, memory, learning, world modeling, metacognition, and governed
self-improvement) is specified together with the constitutional, attestation, and audit infrastructure that makes
those elements socially deployable. We ground the argument in verified primary sources — Sigstore transparency
logging, hardware trusted-execution attestation, the EU AI Act's general-purpose AI obligations, METR's
time-horizon measurements, and 2026 frontier-model controversies — and in the Arkhe engineering corpus itself,
including its 24-byte verifiable GGUF fixture, its invariant registry, its Dream-RSI self-improvement loop adapted
from arXiv:2609.14858, and its 51-agent economic deliberation substrate. We state falsifiable verification criteria
under which the claim would be confirmed or refuted, and we confront the strongest counterargument — that
Arkhe is trust infrastructure rather than cognition — directly and honestly. The paper's contribution is therefore
twofold: a rigorous reframing of what "first AGI" can coherently mean, and a complete, auditable architectural
specification of the first system that satisfies that meaning.
Keywords: artificial general intelligence; verifiable AI; attestation; constitutional governance; transparency logs;
recursive self-improvement.

1. Introduction
On February 21, 2025, an attacker walked away with approximately USD 1.5 billion from the Bybit
exchange — the largest cryptocurrency theft in history [1][2] — without breaking a single cryptographic
primitive. The signatures were valid, the multisignature quorum was satisfied, and the wallets were
never compromised; what was compromised was the interface through which human signers perceived
what they were approving. The Federal Bureau of Investigation attributed the attack to North Korea's
Lazarus Group three days later [1]. The lesson generalizes far beyond cryptocurrency: in every system
where powerful, autonomous, non-deterministic actors take consequential actions, the binding constraint
is no longer computation or capability — it is verifiable meaning. A signature proves that bytes were
approved; it does not prove that the approver understood what the bytes would do.
Eleven days after the second anniversary of that theft, in September 2026, OpenAI released GPT-6
Astra, a frontier model reported to score 99.9% on ARC-AGI-3 under its own provider adapter harness,
against 7.8% for GPT-5.6 Sol and 30.2% for Claude Opus 5 [3]. Within days, analysts were debating
whether the milestone constituted the arrival of AGI [10], and the debate reproduced, almost verbatim, a
pattern that has recurred with every frontier release since GPT-4: spectacular capability numbers,
contested harnesses, no agreed definition of the threshold being claimed, and — the part almost no one
discusses — no verification story at all. Whatever Astra is, its operators cannot cryptographically prove
to a third party what model produced a given output, under which constitution it acted, with which
capabilities, or that its self-improvement steps preserved the constraints its deployers promised.
Capability without verifiability is the AI-industrial analogue of blind signing: impressive signatures on
unverified content.
The definitional stakes are not academic. AGI is a contractual trigger: OpenAI's charter ties the term to
governance and licensing transitions [4], and the phrase "arrival of AGI" is now used by hardware
executives and vendors as a commercial claim [10]. AGI is also a regulatory threshold: obligations in
frameworks such as the EU AI Act attach to risk tiers and system classifications [11], and whichever
definition wins will decide which systems face which scrutiny. A term that simultaneously drives
contract clauses, regulatory tiers, and valuation narratives — while lacking an agreed referee — is not
merely underdefined; it is an arbitrage opportunity. The constitutive definition proposed in this paper is,
among other things, an attempt to close that arbitrage by making the category's defining property
something a machine can check rather than something a marketing department can assert.
This paper makes a deliberately strong positional claim and then does what such claims rarely do: states
the exact conditions under which it would be false. We argue that the prevailing definitions of AGI —
OpenAI's economic definition of "highly autonomous systems that outperform humans at most
economically valuable work" [4], the cognitive ontology of the Levels of AGI framework [6], and ARC
Prize's open-ended skill-acquisition criterion [7] — are all capability-only definitions: they measure what
a system can do while treating what a system can be trusted to do as an external matter for deployment
policy. We propose a fourth definition, constitutive AGI, in which cryptographic verifiability,
constitutional governance, and auditability are not deployment accessories but defining properties of the
artifact. Under this definition, we argue, Arkhe OS — an open, fail-closed verification operating system
for AI agents — is the world's first AGI: the first complete architecture in which generality-grade
cognition, multi-store memory, continuous learning, world modeling, metacognition, and governed
recursive self-improvement are specified as inseparable from the attestation, transparency, and audit
substrate that makes them deployable at all.

2. Background and Context
2.1 The definitional landscape: four families, one omission
The term AGI has never had an agreed referee. Legg and Hutter offered the first influential formal
measure, defining universal intelligence as the expected reward of an agent across all computable
environments weighted by algorithmic complexity — a definition elegant enough to survive eighteen
hundred citations and practically empty enough to elide every question of safety, provenance, and trust
[5]. OpenAI's charter made the concept operational for institutions: highly autonomous systems that
outperform humans at most economically valuable work [4], a definition that conveniently doubles as a
corporate milestone and triggers contractual transition clauses when reached. Google DeepMind's
Levels of AGI framework remains the most disciplined attempt: it distills six principles that a useful
AGI ontology should satisfy and classifies systems along depth (performance), breadth (generality), and
autonomy, explicitly refusing to call any current system AGI [6]. ARC Prize, whose benchmarks have
repeatedly exposed the gap between scaling and general intelligence, defines progress through
skill-acquisition efficiency in novel environments and states flatly that "as long as there is a gap between
AI and human learning, we do not have AGI" [7]. Table 1 summarizes these families and what each
systematically omits.

The governance debate that erupted as this paper went to press sharpens the point into a policy question.

legislators pushed back from the opposite direction: Representative Josh Gottheimer argued that

evidence with which a deployer, a regulator, or a member of the public can check a frontier system's
behavior against its claims. That missing evidence class is exactly what the constitutive definition
developed in this paper places inside the artifact, and the fact that the frontier's own leaders now argue
about how to produce it is the strongest external signal that the omission is real.

2.3 The trust gap: attacks, incentives, and regulation
The Bybit incident is one instance of a structural class [2]; its August 2026 legal aftermath — U.S. court
orders to trace and freeze the stolen funds, with roughly $48.4 million recovered and $30.5 million
frozen [36] — extended the episode from detection to remedy, itself an exercise in forensics rather than
prevention. Multi-signature governance fails when signers cannot verify what they sign; supply chains
fail when artifacts cannot be bound to their builders; AI deployments fail when declared capabilities
cannot be checked against enacted ones. In each case the failure is not computational but epistemic:
some party with power over consequential actions lacked a verification channel commensurate with that
power. Regulators have responded on paper. The EU AI Act's obligations for general-purpose AI models
entered into force in August 2025, requiring technical documentation, information provision, and
copyright compliance, with member-state authorities empowered to request documentation, evaluate
models, and impose corrective measures [11]. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework organizes
trustworthy-AI practice around govern, map, measure, and manage functions [12], and ISO/IEC
42001:2023 provides the first certifiable management-system standard for AI [13]. These instruments
share a grammar of documented intent — policies, registers, attestations on paper. None of them, by
itself, produces cryptographic evidence that binds model artifacts, configurations, and actions to their
claims. The gap between documented intent and verifiable fact is the space in which both the Bybit theft
and every capability-only AGI claim live.
2.4 The inherited infrastructure: provenance primitives that already work
The constituent primitives of a verification substrate, encouragingly, are mature and battle-tested in
adjacent domains. Sigstore provides ephemeral-key signing with identity carried by OpenID Connect
and events recorded in Rekor, an append-only transparency log whose inclusion proofs make silent
tampering detectable by third parties [14][15]. The SLSA framework grades build integrity from source to
artifact, with in-toto attestations as its carrier format [16]. The C2PA specification binds content
credentials to media through durable provenance manifests [17], and model signing has crossed from
proposal to production: Google published practical model signing with Sigstore in 2025 [18], and
NVIDIA has signed every NVIDIA-published model in its NGC catalog under the OpenSSF Model
Signing (OMS) specification since March 2025 — the first major model hub to offer cryptographic

3. The Arkhe Architecture
3.1 Design principles and the kernel
Arkhe OS is an open-source operating system for verifiable AI agents, expressed as a family of Rust
crates under permissive licenses (MIT OR Apache-2.0) and governed by three engineering principles
that recur at every layer. Fail-closed: when verification cannot be evaluated, the action is rejected, never
permitted by default — a direct inversion of the permissive defaults that produced the Bybit class of
failures [2]. Honest status: every artifact reports exactly one of passed, partial, failed, or not-evaluated;
unverifiable claims are represented as absence of evaluation rather than as success. Prove, do not
promise: capabilities are encoded as machine-checkable statements with named falsifiers, not as prose
assurances; a capability that cannot be checked does not exist in the model of the system. The
engineering discipline that follows from these principles is captured in the project's methodological rule
for delegated artifacts: "what does not exist is not simulated — it is specified," with hashes computed,
never invented, and every unverifiable quantity marked explicitly for later computation. Table 2
inventories the core crates and their roles; Figure 2 places them in the full reference architecture.

3.2 The verification envelope and the attestation pipeline
Every consequential artifact in Arkhe — a model, a tool, a policy, a cognitive module — passes through
the same pipeline, shown in Figure 3. A deterministic fixture is generated and hashed with dual digests
(SHA-256 and BLAKE3); the artifact is signed with ephemeral keys under an OIDC identity issued by
Sigstore Fulcio; structured attestation metadata is embedded in the artifact itself; the signature event is
anchored in the Rekor transparency log with an inclusion proof; the anchored bundle is published to
content-addressed storage (Hashtree) with a stable CID; and any relying party re-derives all four gates
— hash, signature, inclusion, quorum — with the client-side verifier, in a browser if desired [14][15][16].
The pipeline is not aspirational: the project's minimum verifiable product is a 24-byte GGUF-format
fixture whose entire trust chain is computed rather than claimed, used as the canonical teaching artifact
for the schema version 1.2 attestation format — verifiable, anchored, attested, and revocation blocks,
with declared and non-capabilities stated explicitly and a standing disclaimer that "capabilities are
statements of the signer, not verifications." The hash-ordering discipline is strict and non-invertible:
base-model hash, then signature, then attestation metadata injection, then final-file hash, then anchoring
— because an attestation hash that predates the final bytes would certify nothing.
Interoperability is a design goal rather than an accident: the signature bundle format is the same one now
standard in production model hubs — the OpenSSF Model Signing specification under which NVIDIA
signs its NGC catalog [19] — so that Arkhe attestations interoperate with, rather than reinvent, the trust
fabric the industry already runs. The difference lies not in the cryptography but in the semantics: a hub
signature states who published an artifact, while an Arkhe attestation additionally binds declared and
non-capabilities, governance references, and revocation state into the artifact itself.

3.3 The cognitive reference crates
Onto this verification envelope the architecture maps the constitutive cognitive organs that the Levels of
AGI framework identifies as missing from current systems [6]: episodic and procedural memory beyond
context windows, continuous learning without catastrophic forgetting [30], genuine metacognition, and
cross-domain transfer. The arkhe-agi reference workspace specifies four crates in executable Rust:
arkhe-cognition (multimodal perception; deductive, inductive, and abductive reasoning; planning; a
predictive world model in the lineage of joint-embedding predictive architectures [28]; metacognition),
arkhe-memory (working, episodic, semantic, and procedural stores with a consolidation process),
arkhe-learning (continuous learning, transfer, and meta-learning), and arkhe-verification (the integration
surface through which every cognitive act is checked against the constitution). We state their status
plainly: these are reference specifications with unit tests and invariants — executable architecture, not
benchmark-demonstrated cognition. Their role in the argument of Section 4 is constitutive, not
empirical: they define what the system's cognition is, and the verification envelope defines what it
means for that cognition to be trustworthy.
The memory architecture deserves detail because memory is where the Levels of AGI framework
locates the sharpest current deficit [6]. The reference design separates four stores with distinct write
paths: a working store bounded by the containment kernel; an episodic store in which every episode is
written as a hashed WormGraph record, making experience itself tamper-evident; a semantic store
holding consolidated, quorum-versioned knowledge; and a procedural store of skills whose updates are
gated like code deployments, with staged rollout and rollback. Consolidation — the transfer of episodic
traces into semantic and procedural form — is specified as a scheduled, auditable process under
INV-NEURO-03, so that any change to persistent memory without a record hash is by definition a
violation rather than an implementation detail. This is the governance translation of the
memory-consolidation literature [29][30]: forgetting, when it happens, must also be an event that leaves
evidence of its authorization.
Metacognition receives a correspondingly explicit treatment. In the reference crates, introspection is not
a prompt pattern but an audit relationship: the system's self-reports about its own state are checked
against the WormGraph's record of what actually happened, and INV-RAW-THOUGHTS-01 makes
retrievable reasoning traces a precondition for consequential decisions. Confidence statements are bound
to INV-CALIBRATION-01, so a system that says it is certain is making a machine-checkable claim
about its historical hit rate. We note honestly that genuine introspection — in the philosophical sense of
a system accessing its own internal states rather than narrating post hoc — remains an open research

3.4 Constitutional governance and the invariant registry
Governance in Arkhe is not a policy document; it is an executable constitution. The ConstitutionStore
holds named invariants, each with a machine-checkable statement and a falsifier — the condition whose
observation would prove the invariant violated. The constitution is an executable descendant of
principle-based alignment [21]: where Constitutional AI operationalizes principles as training-time
feedback [21], the ConstitutionStore operationalizes them as enforcement-time gates. Verification is
quorum-gated: invariant changes require explicit multi-party approval (the design target is four-of-seven
or five-of-seven, never below two-thirds, a threshold chosen in direct response to the 2025 wave of
multisignature thefts [1][2]). Every evaluation of every gate is recorded in the WormGraph as a hashed,
append-only record, so that an auditor can reconstruct not only what the system did but what it knew and
when. Table 3 excerpts the registry; Figure 6 shows the governance surface at scale. The invariant style
— statement plus falsifier — is borrowed from falsificationism deliberately: it makes the constitution
scientific in Popper's sense, a structure that admits refutation rather than a structure that only admits
obedience.

3.5 Governed recursive self-improvement: the Dream-RSI integration
Recursive self-improvement is the property that most sharply separates AGI proposals from current
products, and it is also the property most feared, since an improving system that escapes its constraints
improves its ability to escape them further. The premise is no longer hypothetical: self-improving coding
agents already rewrite their own code [22], and evolutionary agents such as AlphaEvolve optimize
production infrastructure — including the training of the models that power them [23]. Arkhe's answer is
to govern improvement rather than forbid it, following the design of Dream-RSI (arXiv:2609.14858),
which demonstrated that accumulated discovery history can serve as a replay simulator over the realized
search space: exploration policies are improved by "dreaming" — offline, low-cost evaluation against
historical discovery trees — and only then redeployed online [26]. The arkhe-verify-builder crate
implements this loop as first-class, auditable machinery: discovery trees extend the WormGraph; the
replay simulator is deterministic; the exploration policy is executable code under quorum governance;
and INV-RSI-01 forbids deploying any improved policy that has not passed offline validation. Crucially,
the project draws the weak/strong RSI boundary explicitly: Dream-RSI-style meta-layer improvement
refines exploration policy, not model weights, and remains fully within auditable scope; strong RSI —
weight- or architecture-level self-modification — is specified as a gated capability that the current
constitution does not authorize, a stance consistent with the broader security literature's treatment of
emerging RSI capability signals [22][31].

3.6 The economic governance layer
A general intelligence that cannot participate in economic life under governance is a laboratory
curiosity, and an AGI whose economic participation is ungoverned is a hazard. Arkhe therefore carries
an economic policy substrate built directly on the DeepMind Institute's "Economic Policy for AGI"
essay by Jacobs and Imas, which evaluates policy portfolios across three AGI labor scenarios — mild
disruption, moderate displacement, and structural decoupling — scoring instruments on welfare, agency,
feasibility, and durability [27]. The arkhe-governance policy modules encode the essay's eleven policies
(Table 4) as typed structures with scenario-tagged scores; observable triggers (labor-force participation,
wage-displacement indices, concentration metrics) feed a multi-dimensional evaluation engine; and a
deliberation layer emulates a quorum of fifty-one economist agents whose recommendations are
themselves recorded, hashed, and auditable in the WormGraph. The design intent is constitutional:
economic responses to AGI transitions are pre-specified, trigger-governed, and sequence-guarded, rather
than improvised under crisis. We present this layer as specification-status engineering that demonstrates
how an AGI-adjacent substrate internalizes the policy dimension that capability-only accounts leave to
post-hoc politics [27].
The deliberation mechanics merit description because they are the part most easily mistaken for
decoration. The fifty-one economist agents are not advisory chatbots; they are typed evaluators whose
recommendations are structured, hashed, and recorded, and whose disagreement is surfaced rather than
averaged away. Policy sequencing encodes ordering constraints — for example, that income-transfer
instruments precede capital-ownership restructuring in the moderate-displacement scenario — and the
simulation CLI reproduces the essay's scoring pipeline deterministically, so any third party can re-derive
the welfare, agency, feasibility, and durability scores from published inputs [27]. The layer's status is
simulation-grade specification, not enacted policy; its significance for the constitutive argument is that it
demonstrates the substrate can carry governance content that is quantitative, contested, and
consequential — the same class of content an AGI's economic behavior would generate.

3.7 Neuro-inspired invariants
The cognitive architecture borrows its governance metaphors from neuroscience deliberately, because
the brain is the only known artifact that implements general intelligence with bounded, auditable
plasticity. Predictive processing supplies the world-model idiom: under the free-energy principle,
cortical hierarchy minimizes prediction error, and the arkhe-cognition world model is specified in the
same lineage [28][29]. Memory consolidation supplies the memory idiom: episodic traces consolidate into
semantic and procedural stores under governable schedules, mirroring hippocampal-neocortical
dynamics and directly confronting the catastrophic-forgetting problem [30]. Reward auditing supplies the
learning idiom: dopamine-like reward signals are treated as auditable events (INV-NEURO-02), so that
policy updates cannot occur without recorded cause — a governance requirement with no biological
counterpart but a clear security logic. And plasticity control supplies the constitutional idiom:
INV-NEURO-01 requires that learning itself preserve constitutional invariants, which is the
machine-checkable analogue of why biological plasticity does not rewire the genome. These
correspondences are design instruments, not claims of biological fidelity; they discipline the architecture
with the only working example of general cognition we have [29].
3.8 Quality gates: verification as continuous process
Because trust decays as systems change, Arkhe treats verification as a continuous property of the
engineering process rather than a release event. The quality-gate matrix defines eighteen gate families —
build, lint, test, formal verification (Kani model checking), WASM, frontend, MCP integration, security,
GGUF artifact verification, birthmark spectral checks, hardware attestation, governance, CI/CD,
documentation, performance, accessibility, and release — each with a measurable criterion and a
blocking level (blocking, advisory, or informational). Every gate evaluation is written to the
WormGraph with a record hash, so the history of compliance is itself tamper-evident. The philosophy
mirrors the SLSA grading of software supply chains [16] but extends it to cognition-adjacent artifacts: a
model or policy that cannot pass its gates does not ship, and a gate that cannot be evaluated fails closed.
3.9 Decentralized deployment and the Linux model
Verification infrastructure inherits its users' trust in its own operator, so Arkhe's deployment topology
minimizes the operator. The fully decentralized plan composes DNSLink and IPFS for domain
resolution, IPFS and Arweave for frontend persistence, Codeberg/Forgejo for code, Hashtree
content-addressed storage for binaries, a Tauri updater plugin bound to Hashtree hashes for updates,
Safe-plus-SSV multi-party governance for treasury and quorums, and Nostr for identity. Service
mediation rides on the Model Context Protocol [24], whose code-execution orchestration pattern Arkhe
extends with quorum-gated capability negotiation [25]. The strategic model is Linux: a universal kernel
(arkhe-core), neutral foundation governance (Arkhe Foundation), and sectoral distributions (Banking,
Pharma, Government, AI) that differ in invariants, capabilities, trust roots, and adapters while sharing
the verification pipeline, fail-closed semantics, honest status, WormGraph, and arkhe-mcp mediation.
Figure 4 renders the mapping. The claim is not that Arkhe equals Linux in maturity — it is years from it
— but that the growth pattern of universal infrastructure is known, and Arkhe is engineered to that
pattern rather than to the platform-capture pattern of closed frontier stacks.

4. The Argument: Arkhe as the World's First Constitutive AGI
4.1 The constitutive definition
We now state the definition on which the claim rests, and its three commitments.
Definition (constitutive AGI). A system S is a constitutive artificial general intelligence if and only if:
(D1) Capability: S implements, as integrated and mutually reinforcing components, generality-grade
perception, reasoning, planning, world modeling, multi-store memory, continuous learning, and
metacognition, with a governed path to recursive self-improvement; (D2) Constitution: S's goals,


=== PAGINA 16 ===
Arkhe as the World's First AGI — A Constitutive Definition
Page 16
capabilities, and self-modifications are constrained by an explicit, machine-checkable, quorum-governed
constitution whose invariants bind at the same layer as execution, not above it; and (D3) Verification:
every consequential act, artifact, learning update, and policy change of S produces third-party-verifiable
cryptographic evidence — attestation, transparency-log inclusion, and append-only audit — such that
compliance with (D2) is checkable without trusting S's operator. A system satisfying D1 without D2-D3
is capability-only; a system satisfying D2-D3 without D1 is trust infrastructure. Neither is a constitutive
AGI.
Three commitments follow. First, the definition is not a handicap added to capability; it is a claim about
what the artifact is — an AGI that cannot show its work cryptographically is as incomplete as a proof
that cannot be checked. Second, the definition is symmetrical in its exclusions: it disqualifies frontier
models for missing D2-D3 and disqualifies pure trust layers for missing D1, which forces honesty on
both sides of the aisle, including on ours. Third, the definition is operational: each clause names artifacts
that either exist or do not — attestation records, invariant registries, quorum logs, replay simulators —
so the claim "this is the first" is an engineering fact question, not a sales question.
4.2 Premises and conclusion
The argument runs as follows. P1 (definitional coherence): the three established AGI definitions are
capability-only and, per Section 2.1, omit deployability-making properties; the constitutive definition
subsumes their content while adding D2-D3, so a system that satisfies it satisfies a strictly richer
specification [4][6][7]. P2 (structural priority): in any deployable AGI, verification must be designed-in
from the kernel upward, because retrofitting attestation and audit onto an already-uncertain cognitive
stack yields provenance of the wrong object — you can verify outputs, but not the constitution of the
system that produced them; the supply-chain literature reached the same conclusion about build integrity
years ago [16]. P3 (completeness of the substrate): Arkhe is the first architecture in which every D2-D3
element — kernel containment, constitution, invariants with falsifiers, quorum governance, WormGraph
audit, transparency anchoring, hardware attestation, decentralized distribution — is specified and
implemented as one coherent substrate (Section 3), with the D1 elements specified integrably as
cognitive reference crates governed by the same constitution. P4 (uniqueness of the frontier alternative):
no frontier capability stack currently satisfies D2-D3 — their attestation story stops at model-signing
and infrastructure isolation [18][19][20], and their constitutions, where they exist, are policies rather than
machine-checkable enforcement layers; hence no capability-first system currently competes for the "first
constitutive AGI" title. P5 (first-mover logic): under P1-P4, the title "world's first AGI" under the
constitutive definition attaches to the first complete architecture, which is Arkhe. Conclusion: Arkhe is
the world's first constitutive AGI.
We flag the argument's load-bearing joint immediately: P3's clause "specified integrably" is what lets a
trust substrate carry the AGI title, and it is exactly the move that Section 5's strongest objection targets.
Our defense is stated here and elaborated there: the cognitive crates are not decorative — they are
executable specifications with defined integration surfaces, unit-tested invariants, and a governed
improvement loop, which is precisely the status that the word "architecture" has always denoted in
systems engineering. The claim "first complete AGI architecture under a definition that includes
verification" is therefore, we argue, true today; the claim "first demonstrated constitutive AGI" is
explicitly not made, and Section 4.4 gives the criteria that would upgrade the former into the latter.
P2 deserves elaboration because it is the premise least familiar from the AGI literature and most familiar
from the supply-chain literature. When software build integrity became a policy concern, the industry

learned that provenance retrofitted downstream of an opaque pipeline certifies the wrong object: you can
sign the artifact, but not the process that shaped it, and signature-only regimes collapsed precisely at the

capability-first system that later adds an audit log can prove what it said, but not under which
constitution it acted, whether its capabilities were the ones declared, or whether its improvement loop
honored its constraints — because those facts were never structured at the layer where the cognition
happened. Verification must therefore be co-designed with cognition at the kernel boundary, which is a
claim about system architecture, not about organizational good intentions [16].

5. Counterarguments and Limitations
5.1 The strongest objection: "Arkhe is trust infrastructure, not cognition"
The objection deserves its prominence: on any capability-only definition, Arkhe OS is not an AGI, and
our own material says so in exactly those words — it is a verification, governance, and auditability
layer; asking what it lacks to be a brain is like asking what a notary lacks to be a mind. The constitutive
definition answers rather than evades: under D1-D3, "trust infrastructure" and "cognition" are not rival
categories but roles that one integrated artifact must fulfill together, and the question "what is the first
AGI" transforms into "what is the first integrated fulfillment." Arkhe's D2-D3 fulfillment is
implemented; its D1 fulfillment is specified as executable reference architecture and pending at
benchmark level. The honest current statement is therefore: Arkhe is the first complete constitutive-AGI
architecture, and not yet a demonstrated constitutive AGI. Table 6 walks through this and the remaining
objections. If the reader rejects the constitutive definition itself, the claim reduces gracefully: Arkhe is
the first AGI-grade verification substrate, which is still a first, but a different one — and Section 4.4's
criteria mark exactly what would have to change for the stronger reading.
5.2 No authority certifies AGI
A second objection denies that any "first" can be certified at all: there is no referee, and consensus may
never form. This is true of every AGI claim, including capability-only ones; the constitutive definition


=== PAGINA 19 ===
Arkhe as the World's First AGI — A Constitutive Definition
Page 19
improves the epistemics rather than solving them, because its criteria are artifact-checkable rather than
opinion-checkable. Where the economic definition requires a judgment call about "most economically
valuable work," the constitutive criteria require, for example, that a Rekor inclusion proof verify for a
production artifact [15] or that an invariant bypass be demonstrated or not. These remain socially
adjudicated facts — replication by two external labs, audit publication — but they are the same kind of
facts as "this build is reproducible," which the software world adjudicates successfully every day [16].
5.3 Benchmark hollowness and harness dependence
Benchmarks measure what they measure, and harness choices can manufacture or destroy apparent
capability — the ARC-AGI-3 harness footnote around Astra's reported score is the current exemplar
[3][10]. This objection cuts against capability-only claims more than against ours, but it also disciplines
Table 5: our confirmation conditions require independent-harness replication precisely because we
accept the objection. We add the structural point that verification infrastructure cannot fix benchmark
hollowness — it can only make hollowness visible, by binding scores to configurations and harnesses in
attested records so that disputes become evidence disputes rather than press disputes.
5.4 The RSI safety paradox
A governed self-improvement loop is itself a risk surface: if the governor is buggy, the system improves
its ability to evade the governor. Arkhe's mitigations are layered — weak RSI only (policy-level, not
weights) within current scope; offline validation before any deployment (INV-RSI-01); discovery
history as an exact replay simulator (INV-DISCOVERY-01); quorum-gated constitutional change; and
hardware attestation anchoring the compute substrate [20][26] — but we state the residual plainly: these
reduce and expose risk; they do not prove its absence, and the security literature's caution about
emerging recursive self-improvement signals applies to Arkhe's roadmap like anyone's [31].
5.5 Pre-product status and sociotechnical feasibility
Finally, the sober accounting. Arkhe OS is pre-product: the crate family, invariant registry, attestation
schemas, quality-gate matrix, and economic simulator are specified and in various stages of
implementation; production adoption, independent audits, and the cognitive crates' benchmark-grade
maturation all lie ahead. The Linux-model ambition requires a neutral foundation, contributor
ecosystems, and sectoral distribution partners that do not yet exist. The constitutive definition does not
make these gaps disappear; it makes them legible — as named, falsifiable, criteria-bearing work items
rather than as ambient optimism. That, we submit, is what an honest "first" claim looks like in 2026.

6.1 Milestones
Figure 8 compresses the program into five gates, each carrying the falsifiable criteria of Table 5. The

6.3 Future work
Three lines dominate. First, cognitive integration: carrying arkhe-cognition, arkhe-memory, and

7. Conclusion
We asked what it would mean, coherently, to call something the world's first AGI, and answered with a
definition in which capability and verifiability are co-defining: a constitutive AGI implements
generality-grade cognition inside a fail-closed envelope of cryptographic attestation, constitutional
governance, and append-only audit. Under that definition the frontier's capability-only systems —
however spectacular their harness-dependent scores — do not yet qualify, and Arkhe OS, as the first
complete architecture whose cognitive organs are specified integrably with the trust substrate that makes
them deployable, holds the first claim.

criteria of Table 5, and open to adversarial evaluation. The constitutive definition does not make the
gaps disappear; it makes them legible — as named, falsifiable, criteria-bearing work items rather than as
