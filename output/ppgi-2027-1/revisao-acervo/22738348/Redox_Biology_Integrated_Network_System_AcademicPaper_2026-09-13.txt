 Abstract
The central thesis of this monograph is that cellular redox biology is
most faithfully described as an integrated network system whose state
variable is a vector of oxidized-to-reduced ratios—NAD⁺/NADH,
NADP⁺/NADPH, GSH/GSSG, and TrxSS/TrxSH—resolved by
compartment and gated by enzyme kinetics, rather than a scalar index
derived from any single couple. Building on a curated synthesis of
peer-reviewed primary literature, the work develops the
electrochemical and thermodynamic foundations of this view
(Nernstian compartmental potentials; non-equilibrium steady states),
formalizes the network as a directed graph of enzymatically gated
edges, and examines its central metabolic nodes, glutathione and
NAD⁺, together with both pathological directions of stress—oxidative
and reductive, the latter mediated by reverse electron transport.
Signaling chemistries are treated as network computations:
ultrasensitive peroxiredoxin/H₂O₂ kinetics, nuclear sulfenylation,
sulfur-based persulfide signaling, and ferroptosis as a redox-dependent
cell-fate decision executed by three compartment-mapped removal
loops. Physiological integration is traced through circadian–redox
feedback loops, immunometabolic reprogramming, and endoplasmic
reticulum redox chemistry. Single-cell heterogeneity is shown to place
decisive information in distributions rather than means, motivating the
genetically encoded biosensor apparatus summarized in tabular form.
Finally, the framework's operational requirements—ontology-based
model interoperability and redox-aware metabolic engineering,
exemplified by the Redox Imbalance Forces Drive strategy achieving
117.65 g/L L-threonine in engineered Escherichia coli—are evaluated.
The synthesis positions redox biology as a quantitative, systems-level
discipline in which measurement, modeling, and design must jointly
respect compartmental insulation, bidirectional stress, and
out-of-equilibrium dynamics.
Keywords: redox biology; vector of ratios; glutathione; NAD⁺ signaling;
ferroptosis; genetically encoded biosensors

1. Introduction
Oxidation–reduction (redox) reactions constitute the operational core of
cellular life. The transfer of electrons between donors and
acceptors—whether as single electrons, hydrogen atoms, or hydride
ions—underlies the storage of energy in reduced cofactors, the
generation of proton-motive force across biological membranes, and the
reversible modification of protein thiols that regulates enzyme activity.
Every redox potential reported in the literature, every enzymatic rate
constant, and every stress-associated pathology ultimately represents a
restricted accounting of the same physical quantity: the flow and
distribution of electrons within biochemical networks (Jones & Sies,
2015).
The conceptual framework guiding the present monograph has evolved
substantially over the past four decades. The classical notion of
oxidative stress, introduced by Sies in the mid-1980s and subsequently
consolidated (Sies, 2015), framed cellular pathology as an imbalance
between oxidant production and antioxidant defenses. Later work
substantially refined this view: reactive oxygen species (ROS) are now
recognized as pleiotropic physiological signaling agents whose source,
chemical species, subcellular localization, and temporal profile jointly
determine biological outcome (Sies & Jones, 2020), acting largely
through the reversible oxidation of reactive cysteine residues in target
proteins (Holmström & Finkel, 2014). Jones and Sies (2015) formalized
these regularities as the "redox code," a set of organizing principles
that defines the positioning of the NAD, NADP, and thiol/disulfide
systems within the architecture of biological control.
Despite this progress, a persistent reductionist tendency remains in the
experimental literature: the "cellular redox state" is frequently reported
as a single scalar quantity, typically derived from one redox pair
measured in one compartment. The central thesis developed
throughout this work is that such representations are inadequate. The
redox state of a cell is more faithfully described as a vector of
oxidized-to-reduced ratios—NAD⁺/NADH, NADP⁺/NADPH, GSH/GSSG,
TrxSS/TrxSH—that differ by tens to hundreds of millivolts across
compartments, are only partially equilibrated with one another, and are
coupled exclusively where enzymes permit electron flow. This thesis

A network perspective has become both feasible and necessary, owing

Measurements of the glutathione redox potential in membraneless
organelles indicate that these structures faithfully mirror the cytosolic
environment for the GSH/GSSG pair, redefining how
compartmentalization should be understood (Rovira & Rivera-Fuentes,
2025). The peroxiredoxin/hydrogen peroxide system has been shown to

epigenetic regulation (De Smet et al., 2025). In parallel, reductive
stress has been established as a pathological entity in its own right
through reverse electron transport at mitochondrial complex I
(Mercola, 2025); ferroptosis has been characterized as a
redox-dependent form of regulated cell death (Dixon et al., 2012;
Stockwell et al., 2017); hydrogen sulfide and persulfides have been
recognized as a third gaseous signaling layer (Filipovic et al., 2018);
and NAD⁺ decline has been implicated as a hallmark of aging (Verdin,
2015). Reciprocal regulation between redox state and the circadian
clock (Stangherlin & Reddy, 2013), redox reprogramming in
immunometabolism (O'Neill et al., 2016), coupling of the unfolded
protein response to endoplasmic reticulum redox chemistry (Ron &
Walter, 2007), genetically encoded biosensors for compartment-specific
measurement (Gutscher et al., 2008; Meyer & Dick, 2010),
ontology-based model interoperability through SBPAX (Ruebenacker et
al., 2009), and redox-aware metabolic engineering strategies such as
RIFD (Jin et al., 2025) collectively suggest that redox biology is
consolidating into a quantitative, systems-level discipline.

discipline. Five objectives are pursued: (i) to formalize the
electrochemical and network foundations of redox biology; (ii) to
characterize its central metabolic nodes—glutathione and NAD⁺—and
both directions of redox stress; (iii) to analyze the principal signaling
chemistries, including ROS patterns, peroxiredoxin ultrasensitivity,
sulfur-based signaling, and ferroptosis; (iv) to integrate physiological

2. Fundamental Principles of Redox Chemistry
Oxidation–reduction chemistry is defined by the transfer of electrons
between donor and acceptor species, always coupled such that the
oxidation of one molecule is mechanistically and stoichiometrically
linked to the reduction of another. This coupling imposes two invariants
on any redox process: the conservation of charge and the conservation
of mass across the paired half-reactions. The bookkeeping unit of
electron transfer varies with chemical context—it may be a single
electron, a hydrogen atom, or a hydride ion—but the invariant remains
the same. Biological systems exploit this versatility across a spectrum
of chemistries: hydride transfer to and from the nicotinamide ring of
NAD⁺/NADH, single-electron transfers through iron–sulfur clusters and
cytochromes of the respiratory chain, and flavin cofactors that bridge
one- and two-electron chemistries within the same polypeptide scaffold
(Jones & Sies, 2015).
The directionality of electron flow is governed by thermodynamics and
constrained by kinetics. The reduction potential of a couple expresses
its electron affinity, and the Nernst relationship links this potential to
the ratio of oxidized to reduced species; differences in potential
between donor and acceptor couples, rather than absolute values,
determine the direction of spontaneous electron flow. Enzymes,
however, impose the decisive kinetic filter: a thermodynamically
favorable transfer proceeds at physiological rates only where a catalyst

provides a path. This interplay reaches its archetypal expression in
chemiosmotic coupling, in which the downhill transfer of electrons to
molecular oxygen through the respiratory chain is coupled to the
translocation of protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane,
storing free energy as an electrochemical gradient that drives ATP
synthesis (Mitchell, 1961). The quantitative Nernstian formalism
underlying these statements is developed in Section 4.
Within metabolism, coupled electron transfers subserve at least four
distinct functions. First, they store metabolic energy in reduced
cofactors—NADH, NADPH, FADH₂, and ubiquinol—that buffer and
redistribute reducing power. Second, they generate the proton-motive
force that powers oxidative phosphorylation. Third, they supply
dedicated reducing equivalents for biosynthesis and antioxidant
defense, most prominently through the NADPH pool. Fourth, they act in
information transfer: the reversible oxidation of reactive cysteine
residues functions as a post-translational modification that switches
protein activity, positioning thiol chemistry as a signaling layer
analogous to phosphorylation (Paulsen & Carroll, 2010; Holmström &
Finkel, 2014). The same electron-transfer chemistry also carries an
inherent cost: incomplete one-electron reduction of oxygen at the
respiratory chain yields superoxide and, downstream, hydrogen
peroxide, so that electron leak is a quantitative property of normal
electron transfer rather than a pathological accident (Murphy, 2009).
A single unifying consequence follows from these foundations and
informs the remainder of this monograph: every operational concept
introduced subsequently—redox potential (Section 4), enzyme-defined
rate laws (Section 5), homeostatic control (Section 6), bidirectional
stress (Sections 9 and 11), and signaling chemistry (Section
10)—constitutes a restricted accounting of the same conserved
electrons moving through the same network. Treating these concepts
as separate domains obscures their common physical basis; the
network perspective developed in the section that follows restores it.

3. The Redox Network: Directed Graph and
Compartmentalization
The redox network is the directed graph through which electrons move
within and between cellular compartments. Its nodes comprise redox
couples (GSSG/GSH, NAD⁺/NADH, NADP⁺/NADPH, TrxSS/TrxSH),
soluble cofactors, iron–sulfur and flavin prosthetic groups, membrane
transporters, and oxidant sinks; its edges are the electron transfers that
are kinetically permitted at physiological rates. This last qualification is
essential to the network concept. Thermodynamics alone licenses a far
denser graph than biology actually implements, because a
thermodynamically favorable edge is traversed only where a catalyzing
enzyme is expressed and sufficiently active. The topology of the redox
network is therefore enzymatically gated, and changes in enzyme
expression or inhibition act as switches that rewire the graph rather
than merely scaling fluxes upon it (Jones & Sies, 2015).
The major thiol and pyridine nucleotide systems share traffic within this
graph. Glutathione, thioredoxin, NADPH, iron–sulfur proteins, and the
respiratory chain are coupled by enzymes such as glutathione
reductase, thioredoxin reductase, and the peroxiredoxin family, so that
a perturbation at one node propagates to ostensibly independent
subsystems (Lu & Holmgren, 2014). The network is also robust by
virtue of redundancy: experimental or pathological blockade of
glutathione reductase, for example, redistributes peroxide-removal load
toward NADPH-dependent and catalase-linked routes rather than
abolishing it. Such rerouting illustrates why the network must be
analyzed as a map of coupled but partially insulated subgraphs, in
which insulation is the exception established by the absence of
connecting enzymes, not the default.
This graph-theoretic view directly displaces the notion of a single,
unitary "cellular redox state." The conceptual predecessor of network
thinking, the "redox environment," was defined from the glutathione
couple and its Nernstian potential (Schafer & Buettner, 2001); the
network formalism generalizes the same logic, treating each
couple–compartment combination as a node with its own potential, pool
size, and kinetic connectivities, partially equilibrated among themselves
and driven out of equilibrium by continuous flux. It is precisely this

3.1 Membraneless Organelles and the Redefinition of
Compartmentalization
Compartmentalization itself has recently been refined by direct
measurement. Membraneless organelles (MLOs)—the nucleolus, stress
granules, P bodies, paraspeckles, and Cajal bodies—concentrate
proteins and RNA by liquid–liquid phase separation without a delimiting
membrane, raising the question of whether they sustain distinct redox
environments. Quantitative biosensor-based measurements of the
glutathione potential indicate that, for the GSH/GSSG pair, these
structures faithfully mirror the surrounding cytosol; the nuclear EGSH is
indistinguishable from the cytosolic value and remains stable across
phases of the cell cycle (Rovira & Rivera-Fuentes, 2025).
These findings do not invalidate compartmentalization; they redefine its
mechanism. Redox specificity in MLOs resides in local enzymatic
regulation and in gradients of NAD(P)H rather than in passive
partitioning of glutathione species, because a small,
membrane-permeant thiol equilibrates rapidly across phase
boundaries. Apparent exceptions reinforce the enzymatic principle:
stress granules have been reported to develop transient oxidizing
microenvironments in the context of protein aggregation, consistent
with local oxidant generation rather than constitutive partitioning. For
experimental practice, the implication is that assigning a "redox
identity" to a membraneless compartment requires demonstrating local
enzymatic activity or oxidant production, not merely reporting a
measured potential within the structure (Rovira & Rivera-Fuentes,
2025).
The network concept thus resolves the tension between global
integration and local control: integration is achieved by shared electron
currencies, and control by enzymatically gated edges. The quantitative
representation of each node—the redox state as a vector of ratios and
the electrochemical measurement of potentials—is the subject of the
section that follows.

4. Redox State and Redox Potential: A Vector, Not a
Scalar
The redox state of a cell is most rigorously defined as the set of
oxidized-to-reduced ratios of the operative redox couples in each
compartment at a given moment. Cytosolic NAD⁺/NADH, mitochondrial
NAD⁺/NADH, NADP⁺/NADPH, and GSH/GSSG differ routinely by tens
to hundreds of millivolts and are not equilibrated with one another,
because the enzymes that would be required to interconvert them are
absent, compartmentalized, or kinetically insufficient. This vector of
ratios is not merely descriptive; it is causally selective. It determines
which dehydrogenases operate in the forward direction, which cysteine
residues are oxidized beyond their oxidation potentials, and whether
locally generated hydrogen peroxide is buffered or amplified by
downstream chemistry. A single dye reading, or a single redox pair
measured without reference to the others, therefore cannot represent
the state of the network (Jones & Sies, 2015).
The electrochemical measure of any individual node is the redox
potential E, the electron affinity of the couple, related to its standard
potential and to the concentration ratio by the Nernst equation:
 E = E°′ + (RT/nF) · ln([oxidized]/[reduced])
For the glutathione couple, which exchanges two electrons and two
protons (GSSG + 2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻ ↔ 2 GSH), the expression becomes EGSH
= E°′ + (RT/2F) · ln([GSSG]/[GSH]²), with E°′ = −240 mV at pH 7.0
(Schafer & Buettner, 2001). Two quantitative consequences follow.
First, for a two-electron couple, a tenfold change in the
oxidized-to-reduced ratio displaces E by approximately 30 mV at
physiological temperatures, which defines the effective resolution of
redox measurements. Second, because protons participate directly in
the half-reaction, the standard potential varies with pH—by
approximately −59 mV per pH unit at 25 °C for a fully proton-coupled
two-electron exchange—so that apparent intracellular pH shifts
propagate into measured potentials unless pH is monitored
concurrently.

millivolt values are strictly incomparable unless pH, ionic strength,

Three implications follow from this quantitative framing. First, the
oxidizing potentials of the secretory pathway are not failures of
antioxidant control but functional prerequisites: disulfide bond
formation in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi requires potentials
far more oxidizing than the cytosol tolerates. Second, the near-identical
nuclear and cytosolic potentials reported for the GSH/GSSG pair

equilibration of a small, permeant thiol, not the absence of nuclear
redox regulation. Third, compartment-resolved, pH-corrected
measurement becomes a methodological imperative rather than a
refinement, a requirement that shapes the biosensor technologies
discussed in Section 15. The vector, however, is only half of the
network description; the rates at which its components interconvert are
set by enzymes, to which the section that follows is devoted.

5. Redox Enzymes and Pair Coupling
Enzymes furnish the rate laws of the redox network. Dehydrogenases,
oxidases, oxygenases, peroxidases, reductases, and dismutases—each
with defined substrates, cofactors, and compartmental
address—determine where, how fast, and in what stoichiometry
electrons move. The catalytic mechanism is itself consequential: a
hydride transfer executed by a dehydrogenase is a clean, two-electron
movement, whereas a single-electron step at a flavin or metal center
carries an intrinsic probability of premature oxygen reduction and
superoxide formation. Whether a given node of the network leaks
electrons is therefore a property of enzyme mechanism, not merely of
thermodynamic driving force (Murphy, 2009).
The same enzymes are simultaneously the network's control elements.
Expression level, allostery, and reversible cysteine modification jointly
transform an enzyme into a valve that opens under stress and closes at
rest. The archetype is the eukaryotic 2-Cys peroxiredoxin, whose
sensitivity-based hyperoxidation under high peroxide flux—formalized
as the floodgate hypothesis—keeps resting hydrogen peroxide low
while permitting transient signal-amplituding excursions (Wood et al.,
2003). The general lesson is that potential and flux are logically
independent: without kinetic competence, thermodynamically favorable
potentials produce no physiological flow, and conversely, modest
potentials can drive large fluxes through highly efficient catalysts.
A redox pair is an oxidized/reduced pair of chemical species whose ratio
fixes a potential; the canonical intracellular set comprises GSSG/GSH,
NAD⁺/NADH, NADP⁺/NADPH, TrxSS/TrxSH, and O₂/H₂O. Pairs are
coupled only where an enzyme—or an explicitly identified
non-enzymatic reaction—connects them; where no such edge exists,
they drift independently and can sustain persistent potential
differences. The NAD⁺/NADH and NADP⁺/NADPH pools, for instance,
are kept chemically similar yet functionally distinct by the topology of
transhydrogenase and dehydrogenase coupling, and the glutathione
and thioredoxin systems intersect chiefly at shared peroxide substrates
rather than by direct electron exchange (Lu & Holmgren, 2014). The
"redox code" framing captures the same idea at the level of biological
organization: the NAD, NADP, and thiol systems occupy fixed,

non-interchangeable positions in the architecture of control (Jones &

Pool size completes the picture and is systematically underweighted in
interpretation. Millimolar glutathione buffers many individual oxidant
attacks that would measurably swing a micromolar NADP pool;
identical electron loads thus produce potentials shifts of very different
magnitudes in different nodes. The quantitative anchors are familiar:
E°′(GSSG/2GSH) = −240 mV at pH 7.0, with healthy cytosolic
GSH/GSSG ratios conventionally reported between 30:1 and 100:1
(Schafer & Buettner, 2001). Treating all pairs as an interchangeable
currency erases precisely the insulation—of pool, compartment, and
enzyme—that makes compartmental control possible, and with it the
vector formalism of Section 4. The regulatory machinery that actively
maintains these insulated nodes within physiological bounds is the
subject of the section that follows.
6. Redox Homeostasis: Active Control Systems
Redox homeostasis is not the absence of stress but the active control of
potentials, ROS amplitudes, and thiol occupancy within a range
compatible with signaling and protein folding. The distinction is
substantive rather than semantic: a homeostatic system
tolerates—indeed requires—transient excursions, provided they are
sensed, bounded, and reversed. In control-engineering terms, the redox
network implements a distributed feedback architecture comprising
sensors that convert local oxidant load into regulatory signals,
actuators that adjust supply and removal, and repair processes that
restore damaged components. Homeostasis is therefore a dynamical
property of the network defined in Section 3, not a static chemical
end-state, and its analysis belongs naturally to the modeling framework
developed in the section that follows.
The sensor layer is molecularly well characterized. Peroxiredoxins, in
addition to their catalytic role, act as peroxide sensors whose oxidation
state reports local hydrogen peroxide fluxes (Wood et al., 2003). The
Keap1/Nrf2 system constitutes a dedicated electrophile-and-oxidant
sensor: under basal conditions Keap1 promotes constitutive Nrf2
degradation, while reactive cysteines in Keap1, upon modification,

release Nrf2 to induce a coordinated transcriptional program of
antioxidant and phase-II detoxification genes (Kensler et al., 2007). The
prolyl hydroxylases that regulate hypoxia-inducible factors couple
oxygen availability directly to transcription, so that hypoxic and
oxidative inputs are integrated at the level of HIF stabilization
(Semenza, 2012). Together these systems convert local chemical events
into graded, genome-wide adjustments of enzymatic capacity.
The actuator and repair layers close the loop. Reducing power is
replenished by metabolism—principally through the pentose phosphate
pathway, malic enzyme, and isocitrate dehydrogenases that maintain
the NADPH pool—and oxidized glutathione is either reduced by
glutathione reductase or exported, thereby unloading oxidizing
equivalents from the cell. Damaged macromolecules are repaired, as in
the reduction of protein disulfides by thioredoxin and glutaredoxin
systems, or degraded and resynthesized. Crucially, these actions are
compartment-specific: the vector formalism of Section 4 implies that a
homeostatic response must be evaluated at the level of each couple and
compartment, not by a global average (Jones & Sies, 2015).
Certain proteins simultaneously occupy multiple layers of this
architecture. Peroxiredoxin 6 is the exemplar of such moonlighting: a
bifunctional enzyme combining glutathione peroxidase activity with
calcium-independent phospholipase A2 activity, it both reduces
peroxidized membrane phospholipids and participates in phospholipid
homeostasis and signal transduction (Fisher, 2011). Its dual catalytic
identities couple antioxidant defense directly to lipid metabolism,
illustrating how a single polypeptide can bind oxidant sensing, oxidant
removal, and metabolic regulation into one node.
The analytical payoff of the control-system view is diagnostic
specificity. Collapse of redox homeostasis is not "more oxidation" in the
abstract; it is the failure of a specific loop—sensing, supply, or
removal—and the therapeutic implication is to restore the failing loop
rather than to flood the system with chemically unrelated reductants, a
maneuver that risks importing the reductive stress analyzed in Section
9. Formalizing these loops as kinetic systems, however, requires the
out-of-equilibrium modeling apparatus introduced next.

7. Modeling Out-of-Equilibrium Redox Systems
Living cells sustain persistent electron fluxes and stable differences of
potential between compartments, which places them categorically
outside thermodynamic equilibrium. It follows that equilibrium
constants alone cannot predict intracellular concentrations or the rate
of ROS production: at equilibrium, fluxes vanish and potentials
equalize, whereas the physiological state is precisely one of maintained
disequilibrium. Any modeling formalism that treats the cell as a closed,
equilibrated system therefore mis-specifies its object from the outset.
The appropriate description is that of an open, driven network held in a
non-equilibrium steady state by continuous exchange with its
environment.
Useful kinetic models share a common construction. Reaction dynamics
are represented by mass-action or Michaelis–Menten rate laws, the
latter formalized in the classical analysis of enzyme-catalyzed reactions
(Michaelis & Menten, 1913), with parameters for defined substrates,
cofactors, and compartments. Compartmental volumes, membrane
transporters, and the open exchange of oxygen, nutrients, and exported
oxidized products must be represented explicitly, because the import
and export terms are what sustain the steady state. The kinetic
constants of oxidases, peroxidases, and reductases—rather than
equilibrium constants—then determine flux distribution and oxidant
load. Such models are necessarily parameter-rich, and their predictions
are correspondingly sensitive to parameter uncertainty, a limitation
that argues for bounding analyses rather than single-point simulations.
The thermodynamic interpretation of the modeled state is equally
consequential. A non-equilibrium steady state is dissipative: the cell
exports entropy continuously while preserving local order—specifically,
the ordered distributions of electrons in reduced cofactors that
constitute the vector of Section 4. This is the general condition for
biological organization articulated in the thermodynamics of dissipative
structures (Prigogine, 1978). The practical corollary is that steady state
must never be conflated with equilibrium in experimental design or
interpretation: a cell at steady redox state is consuming, transforming,
and exporting energy, and its measured potentials reflect kinetic
balances between production and removal, not chemical affinities

Finally, model validation imposes its own data requirement. Because
the network comprises partially insulated nodes (Section 3), no
single-pair time course can constrain a multi-compartment model;
validation requires simultaneous measurements of more than one redox
pair, ideally resolved by compartment. Genetically encoded biosensors
(Section 15) supply precisely this class of data, and kinetic modeling of
the peroxiredoxin/hydrogen peroxide system has already demonstrated
the payoff, exposing multiple intrinsic sources of ultrasensitivity that
equilibrium descriptions could not have revealed (Liu et al., 2025). The
modeling framework thus closes the loop opened at the start of this
monograph: thermodynamics defines what is possible, enzymes define
what is actual, and out-of-equilibrium kinetics relates the two. The next
sections examine the central nodes around which these fluxes are
organized—glutathione and NAD⁺—before turning to the pathological
regimes that arise when their control fails.
8. Glutathione and NAD⁺: The Central Nodes
Glutathione is a tripeptide thiol (γ-glutamyl–cysteinyl–glycine) present
at millimolar concentrations in the reduced form (GSH), with only a
small fraction as the disulfide (GSSG) in the healthy cytosol, yielding
the strongly reducing GSH/GSSG potential quantified in Table 1.
Functionally, the glutathione node concentrates four capacities in a
single, compartmentally addressable pool. It donates electrons to
glutathione peroxidases and glutaredoxins, thereby participating
directly in peroxide and disulfide chemistry; it conjugates electrophiles
through the glutathione S-transferases, central to phase-II
detoxification; it can be exported as GSSG, discharging oxidizing
equivalents from the cell; and it buffers, by sheer pool size, oxidant
challenges that would swing micromolar pools (Section 5).
Regeneration depends on glutathione reductase and NADPH, and de
novo synthesis on cysteine availability and the activity of
glutamate–cysteine ligase, the rate-limiting step subject to feedback
inhibition by GSH and transcriptional induction via Nrf2 (Lu, 2009).
The systems-level significance of this node follows from its connective
position. Loss or saturation of glutathione handling forces other couples

Conversely, because glutathione links peroxide removal, detoxification,

of network load, which explains, without excusing, the historical
reliance on GSH/GSSG as a global index. The vector formalism of
Section 4 demarcates precisely what such an index can and cannot
represent.
8.1 NAD⁺ as Redox Cofactor and Longevity Signal
NAD⁺ occupies a second central node, with a dual identity that the
glutathione system lacks. As a redox cofactor it shuttles hydrides in
catabolic and anabolic dehydrogenase reactions, positioning
NAD⁺/NADH at the pivot of energy metabolism; as a signaling substrate
it is consumed stoichiometrically by sirtuins (SIRT1–7),
poly(ADP-ribose) polymerases (PARPs), and CD38 ectoenzymes, linking
cellular redox status to epigenetic regulation, DNA repair, and calcium
signaling (Verdin, 2015). This consumption is not free of
thermodynamic constraint: every non-redox use of NAD⁺ competes with
the redox uses of the same total pool, so that signaling demand, repair
demand, and metabolic flux are coupled through one molecule.
Quantitatively, NAD⁺ abundance declines with age—by roughly half
between the second and sixth decades of life according to tissue
surveys—and this decline is regarded as a hallmark of aging rather
than an epiphenomenon (Verdin, 2015; Covarrubias et al., 2021). The
consequences propagate through three coupled loops. Reduced sirtuin
activity dysregulates epigenetic programming, including acetylation
states of metabolic and stress-response regulators; diminished
PARP-mediated repair allows oxidative DNA damage to accumulate;
and mitochondrial dysfunction both follows from and further depresses
NAD⁺ availability, since electron flux through the respiratory chain is
itself tied to the NAD⁺/NADH ratio. The result is a positive feedback
between oxidant load, signaling demand, and cofactor depletion that
operates on the timescale of years.
Interventional strategies target this loop at several points. Precursor
supplementation with nicotinamide riboside or nicotinamide

therefore reappear jointly in the pathological regimes examined next,
beginning with the paradoxical pathology of excess reducing power.
9. Reductive Stress and Reverse Electron Transport
Reductive stress is the mirror image of oxidative stress and a
pathological regime in its own right: an overload of reducing
equivalents that creates an electronic traffic jam within the electron
transport chain. When NADH and NADPH accumulate
abnormally—through sustained substrate excess, inhibited oxidation, or
overactive reductive biosynthesis—the NADH/NAD⁺ ratio at complex I
rises and the upstream chain becomes partially reduced and inhibited.
Paradoxically, this hyper-reduced environment does not protect the cell
from oxidation; it promotes it, because a reduced chain increases the
probability that electrons escape prematurely to molecular oxygen. The
outcome is a vicious cycle characteristic of chronic metabolic disease:
more reducing equivalents drive more electron leak, more leak drives
more oxidative damage, and more damage deepens mitochondrial
dysfunction (Mercola, 2025).
The mechanistic core of this paradox is reverse electron transport
(RET). RET occurs when a highly reduced coenzyme Q
pool—ubiquinol—coincides with an elevated mitochondrial membrane
potential: conditions created, for example, by oxidation of succinate or
of excess fatty-acid-derived substrates. Under these conditions
electrons reflux from the ubiquinol pool backwards into complex I,
reducing its flavin site and generating superoxide at a rate that can
exceed forward-path production by an order of magnitude. The
controlling variables are precisely those the network formalism
predicts: the redox state of the Q pool and the magnitude of the
membrane potential, both of which are kinetic-dynamical quantities

rather than equilibrium constants (Murphy, 2009; Brand, 2016). RET is
not an exotic curiosity; it is quantitatively one of the dominant
mitochondrial sources of superoxide under substrate excess.
The pathological repertoire of reductive stress follows directly. In type
2 diabetes, hyperglycemia and excess fatty acid flux over-reduce both
NADH pools and the Q cycle, favoring RET-driven oxidant generation;
in non-alcoholic hepatic steatosis, lipid oversupply sustains the same
configuration; and in neurodegenerative disease, chronic substrate and
electron-handling dysfunction maintains hyper-reduced, leak-prone
mitochondria (Mercola, 2025). In each case the reductive and oxidative
facets are inseparable—the reductive regime is the cause of the
oxidative phenotype—which is why the two stress directions must be
modeled jointly rather than as alternatives.
Oxidative stress, the mirror condition, is defined with equal precision:
the production or import of oxidants that exceeds enzymatic removal,
glutathione buffering, and repair, displacing operative potentials
toward oxidation. Its consequences—lipid peroxidation, protein
carbonylation, DNA oxidation, and persistent disulfide locking of
enzymes—are the same chemistry that physiological signaling uses,
differing only in amplitude, duration, or location relative to the
network's containment capacity (Sies, 2015; Sies & Jones, 2020).
Signaling-level hydrogen peroxide occupies a lower range; stress is that
same chemistry where the network fails to contain it, a distinction
made quantitative in the section that follows.
The therapeutic symmetry is the section's closing lesson. Correction
must target source, sink, and NADPH supply jointly, because
re-equilibrating one node alone leaves the traffic jam intact. Flooding
the system with chemically unrelated reductants—the reflexive
"antioxidant" maneuver—adds reductive load without repairing the
failing loop, and may thereby aggravate the very regime it intends to
treat (Mercola, 2025). Bidirectional stress thus completes the argument
of Sections 4 and 8: the vector of ratios is the relevant state variable,
and both of its tails are pathological.

10. ROS as Signaling Patterns: Ultrasensitivity of the
H₂O₂/PRX System
Reactive oxygen species are incompletely reduced intermediates of
molecular oxygen, principally superoxide (O₂•−), hydrogen peroxide
(H₂O₂), and the hydroxyl radical (•OH), the last formed when peroxide
encounters reduced transition metals. Their interconversion and
removal are enzymatically organized: superoxide dismutases convert
superoxide to H₂O₂, and peroxidases and catalase remove H₂O₂ using
glutathione, peroxiredoxin thiols, or heme as reducing substrates. This
chemical organization assigns each species a distinct biological
meaning. Localized, low-level H₂O₂ oxidizes specific, low-pKa cysteine
residues reversibly and thereby acts as a second messenger
(Holmström & Finkel, 2014); the hydroxyl radical, reacting at the
diffusion limit with nearly any organic molecule, is almost purely
damaging wherever it is formed (Murphy, 2009). Biological information
therefore resides not in a single "quantity of oxidant" but in patterns
defined jointly by source, chemical species, location, and time (Sies &
Jones, 2020).
The pattern framework resolves the apparent paradox that the same
molecule is both signal and stressor. Compartmentalized
sources—NADPH oxidases at membranes, electron leak at
mitochondrial sites—generate H₂O₂ in spatially restricted domains, and
equally localized peroxidase arrays shape its steady-state profile, so
that a cytosolic and a nuclear H₂O₂ excursion of identical magnitude
can carry different instructions. Signaling specificity thus emerges from
network topology and enzyme positioning, the same principles that
organize electron traffic in general (Section 3).
Within this framework, a quantitative question arises: how does the
system convert smooth, graded H₂O₂ gradients into discrete biological
decisions? Kinetic analysis of the peroxiredoxin system identifies
multiple intrinsic sources of ultrasensitivity (Liu et al., 2025). First,
zero-order kinetic cycles: when the peroxiredoxin is saturated with
H₂O₂, its rate of removal becomes independent of substrate
concentration, producing all-or-none behavior at the sensor. Second,
multistep signaling: the sequential oxidation of the peroxiredoxin
catalytic cysteine—sulfenic acid, disulfide, hyperoxidized sulfinic

Page 20
state—introduces nonlinearities that amplify small differences in input.
Third, diminished removal at high hyperoxidation: as peroxiredoxin
accumulates in its hyperoxidized state, H₂O₂-removal capacity falls,
creating positive feedback that further amplifies the excursion. In
integrated models, the coupled sulfenylation–sulfinylation cycle (CSSC)
constitutes a structurally positive-feedback loop capable of bistability
under defined parameter regimes, so that the H₂O₂/PRX module
behaves as a switch rather than a linear transducer (Liu et al., 2025).
This digitalizing behavior complements, at the systems level, the
floodgate mechanism of peroxiredoxin hyperoxidation described at the
molecular level (Wood et al., 2003).
Recent work extends this signaling logic into the nucleus and the
epigenetic machinery. Using a nuclear-targeted YAP1-based probe, De
Smet et al. (2025) mapped the nuclear sulfenome of Arabidopsis and
identified the histone acetyltransferase GCN5 as a direct target of
sulfenylation at defined cysteines (Cys293, Cys368, Cys400),
demonstrating that H₂O₂ can modulate histone acetylation—and
thereby gene expression—through direct, reversible oxidation of a
chromatin enzyme. Although demonstrated in a plant system, the
finding establishes a structural principle with plausible generality:
nuclear sulfenylation provides a direct channel by which metabolic
state, via oxidant flux, is converted into transcriptional reprogramming,
without intermediate transcription factors.
Two conclusions follow for the architecture of redox control. First, the
cell does not require a dedicated sensor for every concentration band:
ultrasensitive kinetics within a single enzyme system spans
physiological and stress-level oxidant regimes. Second, redox signaling
is chemically specific—sulfenylation, the subject of this section, is one
modification among several sulfur-based chemistries, and its selectivity
is defined by cysteine microenvironment as much as by local H₂O₂
concentration. The sulfur signaling repertoire extends beyond oxygen
chemistry, however: hydrogen sulfide and persulfides constitute a third
gaseous signaling layer, to which the section that follows is devoted.

11. Hydrogen Sulfide and Persulfides: The Third
Gaseous Signaler
Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) joins nitric oxide and carbon monoxide as the
third gaseous signaling molecule of mammalian physiology, but its
redox chemistry is more structurally layered than that of its
predecessors. H₂S is generated enzymatically—chiefly by cystathionine
γ-synthase-like enzymes of the transsulfuration pathway, cystathionine
γ-lyase (CSE) and cystathionine β-synthase (CBS), and by
3-mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase—and it is oxidized along an
ascending ladder to persulfides (H₂S₂), sulfite (SO₃²⁻), and sulfate
(SO₄²⁻). The mitochondrial sulfide:quinone oxidoreductase (SQR)
occupies a particularly instructive position in the network: it consumes
H₂S and feeds the liberated electrons into the coenzyme Q pool, so that
sulfide functions as an inorganic electron donor wired directly into the
respiratory chain, a genuine network edge in the sense of Section 3
(Filipovic et al., 2018).
The signaling chemistry of sulfur centers on protein persulfidation (also
termed sulfhydration), the conversion of a cysteine thiol (RSH) to a
persulfide (RSSH) by sulfur transfer from H₂S₂ or related sulfane sulfur
species. Persulfidation is mechanistically distinct from the sulfenylation
discussed in Section 10: whereas sulfenylation (RSOH) is an
oxygen-centered oxidation executed by H₂O₂, persulfidation is a
sulfur-centered transfer that preserves the thiol's protonated character
while dramatically enhancing its nucleophilicity. Persulfide residues are
both more reactive toward electrophilic partners and more reducing,
yet the modification remains reversible through the thioredoxin and
glutaredoxin systems, so that persulfidation constitutes a genuine,
resettable signaling modification rather than irreversible damage
(Filipovic et al., 2018).
Functionally, persulfidation regulates an expanding set of targets that
spans the physiological systems treated in this monograph: ion
channels, metabolic enzymes, and transcriptional regulators—including
components of the Keap1–Nrf2 axis, whose persulfidation favors
antioxidant program induction. The chemical logic mirrors that of
oxygen-based signaling with a twist: because persulfidation alters
nucleophilicity rather than creating an oxidized oxyacid intermediate, it

Page 22
can activate as well as inhibit target functions, and it does so with
residue selectivity governed by local sulfur-transferase and oxidase
activities. In effect, the cell operates two partially parallel sulfur
chemistries—oxygen-derived sulfenylation and sulfide-derived
persulfidation—that interconvert through the shared pool of reactive
cysteines (Filipovic et al., 2018).
The systemic implication completes the argument of this part of the
monograph. The redox network is more complex than the historical
"ROS plus glutathione" dyad: H₂S and persulfides add a sulfur-signaling
layer that interacts with oxygen-based signaling at multiple
points—competing for the same cysteine residues, feeding electrons
through SQR into the Q pool, and modulating the very transcriptional
programs (Nrf2) that govern antioxidant capacity. Any redox-directed
therapeutic or engineering design that ignores sulfur chemistry
therefore omits a layer of the control architecture. The remaining
sections of this block examine how these chemistries determine cell
fate, beginning with the redox-dependent regulated cell death pathway
ferroptosis.
12. Ferroptosis: Redox-Dependent Regulated Cell Death
Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death defined by two biochemical
signatures: dependence on iron and on the accumulation of oxidized
phospholipids. First named and distinguished from apoptosis,
necroptosis, and autophagy-dependent death in 2012, ferroptosis was
identified through the action of small molecules (erastin and RSL3) that
killed cells bearing oncogenic RAS mutations without activating the
caspase scaffold of classical apoptosis (Dixon et al., 2012). The pathway
has since become the most explicit demonstration of the central thesis
of this monograph: cell fate is an emergent property of the redox
network, and death ensues not from the presence of any single reactive
species but from the failure of the network's removal loops to keep pace
with lipid-phase electron flux (Stockwell et al., 2017).
The critical dangerous reaction is lipid peroxidation. Polyunsaturated
fatty acyl chains esterified in membrane phospholipids are unusually
susceptible to hydrogen abstraction by radical species; the resulting
phospholipid radical reacts with oxygen to form a phospholipid peroxyl

radical, propagating a chain reaction within the hydrophobic membrane
interior. The only enzyme capable of directly reducing phospholipid
hydroperoxides within intact bilayers is glutathione peroxidase 4
(GPX4), which consumes GSH in the manner described in Section 5.
Consequently, the GSH/GSSG pair—whose compartmental potentials
were tabulated in Section 4—is not merely a stress indicator in this
context but the substrate supply line for the decisive anti-ferroptotic
enzyme: ferroptosis is what happens when the glutathione edge of the
graph fails at the lipid membrane (Stockwell et al., 2017).
The defense architecture surrounding GPX4 illustrates the
compartmental redundancy anticipated by the network model of
Section 3. Ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1) functions as a
glutathione-independent, GPX4-parallel system: a CoQ oxidoreductase
that regulates the plasma-membrane pool of coenzyme Q, regenerating
the reduced CoQH₂ that acts as a radical-trapping antioxidant at the
cell surface, thereby protecting the plasma membrane even when GPX4
is inactive (Bersuker et al., 2019). A second parallel loop guards the
mitochondrial compartment: dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH),
an enzyme of the pyrimidine-biosynthesis pathway, reduces CoQ at the
outer face of the inner mitochondrial membrane and in doing so
maintains a mitochondrial CoQH₂ pool that suppresses mitochondrial
lipid peroxidation, providing protection that is separable from both
GPX4 and FSP1 (Mao et al., 2021). Three biochemically independent
removal loops—GPX4/GSH, FSP1/CoQ, DHODH/CoQ—are thus mapped
onto distinct subcellular locations, and each constitutes an
enzymatically gated edge of exactly the kind formalized in Section 3.
Ferroptosis therefore reframes "oxidative stress" in operational terms.
Death is not triggered by a threshold concentration of hydrogen
peroxide or by a single potential reading; it ensues when lipid
peroxidation flux exceeds the aggregate capacity of the parallel
removal systems, which can occur through substrate supply
(polyunsaturated lipid loading), through GSH depletion (as in the
cystine-starvation geometry of erastin), through direct enzymatic
inhibition (RSL3 versus GPX4), or through CoQH₂ exhaustion. This
multiplicity explains why ferroptosis resistance in cancer cells
frequently arises through amplification of any one of the three

aperture: tumor cells with high lipid-remediation demands can be
pushed across the ferroptotic threshold by depriving them of a single
defense loop, and ferroptosis induction is currently being exploited as a
mechanism underlying several chemotherapeutic and radiotherapeutic
modalities (Stockwell et al., 2017).
The physiological reach of ferroptosis extends beyond oncology.
Ischemia–reperfusion injury in kidney, heart, and brain, and aspects of
neurodegeneration, have all been associated with GPX4 failure and
lipid-peroxide accumulation, although the in vivo attribution of specific
pathologies to ferroptosis versus other death programs remains an
active area of scrutiny. What is certain at the level of network
architecture is that a multi-loop, compartment-resolved defense system
of this kind converts the abstract redox potentials of Section 4 into a
concrete life-or-death decision variable—and that the same logic of
sensing, supply, and removal governs how these chemical events are
read out by the circadian, immune, and endoplasmic systems to which
the discussion now turns.
13. Physiological Integration: Circadian, Immune, and
ER Systems
The redox network analyzed in the preceding sections does not operate
in isolation: it is embedded in—and constitutive of—the organism-level
systems that coordinate physiology. Three of these systems deserve
explicit treatment because redox state functions simultaneously as their
input and their output: the circadian clock, which times metabolic and
stress-response capacity across the day; the immune system, whose
effector functions are themselves redox chemical operations; and the
endoplasmic reticulum, the compartment in which redox potential is a
structural requirement of protein maturation. The cell-fate chemistry of
Section 12 is, in practice, read out by these three systems, and each
converts redox information back into metabolic instructions. The
integrated-network thesis of this monograph therefore scales from
molecular chemistry to organismal physiology.

The coupling between the circadian clock and redox state is
bidirectional and mechanistically explicit. The core clock machinery
(CLOCK/BMAL1) transcriptionally regulates redox enzymatic
capacity—Nrf2 expression and GPX4 abundance are reported to
oscillate with the circadian cycle—while the redox state conversely
modulates clock function (Stangherlin & Reddy, 2013). The most
conserved demonstration of this reciprocity comes from the
peroxiredoxin system introduced in Section 10: cycles of peroxiredoxin
oxidation and reduction run with ~24-hour periodicity in organisms
from all domains of life, constituting a universal marker of circadian
rhythmicity even in cells lacking canonical clock gene transcription
(Edgar et al., 2012). NAD⁺ supplies a third coupling channel: the
rate-limiting NAD⁺-biosynthetic enzyme NAMPT is itself
clock-controlled, NAD⁺ levels oscillate diurnally, and SIRT1 closes the
loop by deacetylating clock components, thereby linking the coupled
variables of Section 8 into the timekeeping apparatus (Ramsey et al.,
2009; Covarrubias et al., 2021). Disruption of this redox–clock feedback
loop is implicated in aging, metabolic disease, and cancer (Stangherlin
& Reddy, 2013).
The immune system illustrates redox integration at the level of
cell-state transitions. Immunometabolic reprogramming upon activation
reorganizes the redox wiring described in Sections 5 and 6: classically
activated (M1-like) macrophages run glycolysis with a high
pentose-phosphate flux that feeds NADPH to oxidant generation and
maintenance of the GSH pool, sustaining high ROS output as an
antimicrobial effector function; alternatively activated (M2-like)
macrophages rely on oxidative phosphorylation and fatty-acid oxidation
and operate at lower ROS levels consistent with repair and resolution
functions (O'Neill et al., 2016). Together with the professional oxidant
generation of the phagocyte NADPH oxidase, these configurations
establish oxidant amplitude as a regulated effector parameter rather
than a pathology. It follows that redox modulation is an emerging
immunotherapeutic strategy: shifting the vector of ratios—through
NADPH supply, GSH capacity, or NAD⁺ availability—can bias immune
programs between inflammatory and reparative states (O'Neill et al.,
2016).

The endoplasmic reticulum demonstrates that an oxidizing setpoint can

tabulated in Section 4, the ER maintains the most oxidizing glutathione
potential of the major compartments (EGSH ≈ −208 mV), an
electrochemical requirement for oxidative protein folding, in which
disulfide bonds are introduced into nascent polypeptides. The folding
machinery is itself a redox network: Ero1 oxidases transfer disulfides to
folding substrates and generate hydrogen peroxide as a stoichiometric
byproduct, which ER-resident peroxidases such as GPX7 and GPX8 can
productively consume, converting a potentially damaging byproduct
into further disulfide-forming equivalents. Folding load is monitored by
the unfolded protein response (UPR), whose three sensors—IRE1,
ATF6, and PERK, the last attenuating translation through eIF2α

Walter, 2007). The redox–UPR crosstalk is bidirectional: an oxidant
deficit in the ER impairs disulfide formation and blunts UPR signaling,
whereas sustained oxidative load drives chronic UPR activation (Ron &
Walter, 2007).

network formalism predicts. The clock times the expression of folding
and immune capacities; immune activation redraws the
metabolic-redox map of its host cells; folding load feeds back on
compartmental potentials and stress programs. Every arrow in this
triangle is bidirectional, in the same sense that redox state and NAD⁺

executed by enzymatically gated edges rather than passive
equilibration (Jones & Sies, 2015). The monograph's argument has now
scaled from electrochemistry (Section 2) through cell fate (Section 12)

14. Single-Cell Heterogeneity and the Limits of
Population Averages
The vector of ratios developed in Section 4 is defined for an individual
cell—and, within that cell, for an individual compartment—at a given
moment. Population-level measurements, by contrast, return averages
over distributions of these vectors, and the relationship between a
distribution and its mean is a substantive biological question, not a
statistical formality. The redox state is not uniform across cellular
populations: single cells exhibit measurable redox heterogeneity that
arises from identifiable physiological sources. A bulk mean may
therefore describe no actual member of the population, and the
distribution may be multimodal, with modality—rather than central
tendency—carrying the biologically decisive information. Heterogeneity
thus extends the validation requirement of Section 7 from the
dimension of redox pairs and compartments to the dimension of the
population itself.
Three sources of single-cell redox heterogeneity can be identified. First,
cell-cycle position: the glutathione potential varies systematically
through the division cycle, with more reducing environments
characteristic of the G1 phase than of later phases, and nuclear
glutathione content undergoing a coordinated cycle coupled to
cell-cycle progression (Diaz Vivancos et al., 2010). Second,
microenvironment: gradients of oxygen and nutrients—within tissues,
spheroids, or even a single culture—impose different boundary
conditions on the exchange terms of the Section 7 model, so that
geometrically identical cells may occupy measurably different redox
states; the mirroring of cytosolic glutathione potential by
membraneless organelles (Section 3) shows that even intracellular
microenvironments carry this information (Rovira & Rivera-Fuentes,
2025). Third, stochastic noise in the expression of redox enzymes
produces dispersion in the kinetic parameters of Section 5, and—given
the ultrasensitivity and bistability documented in Section 10—small
parameter differences can separate cells into qualitatively distinct
dynamical regimes.

these sources from inference into observation. Persistent

reported to occupy more reducing glutathione environments than their

The therapeutic implication is direct and uncomfortable: bulk studies
conceal the subpopulation structure that is critical for treatment

mistreats the tails of the distribution. In oncology this failure mode is
well characterized—a reducing-shifted, drug-tolerant subpopulation

resistant tail. The ferroptosis framework of Section 12 sharpens the

determined cell by cell by the aggregate capacity of the removal loops,
and a population mean of GPX4 or GSH abundance predicts the fraction
of cells that will cross the threshold only weakly. Killing the mean is not
killing the population.

15. Measuring the Vector: Genetically Encoded
Biosensors for Compartment-Resolved Redox State
The vector-of-ratios thesis is, at bottom, a measurement thesis: if the
operative state is multiplex across redox pairs and compartments, then
the instruments must meet four requirements simultaneously. They
must be ratiometric, so that the readout is independent of probe
expression level; genetically encoded, so that measurements are
acquired in living cells rather than lysates; reversible, so that dynamics
rather than endpoint snapshots are captured; and targetable, so that
the compartment dimension of the vector is actually sampled.
Genetically encoded fluorescent biosensors satisfy all four: a sensing
domain whose own thiol or cofactor-binding state equilibrates with a
target redox pair is fused to a fluorescent reporter whose excitation or
emission ratio reports that state (Meyer & Dick, 2010). Individual
probes can then be redirected by signal sequences to the cytosol,
nucleus (NLS), mitochondria (MTS), endoplasmic reticulum, or other
destinations, converting the reference frame of Table 1 from a
compilation of literature values into a directly measurable coordinate
system.
The glutathione channel is the most mature. Grx1-roGFP2 fuses
glutaredoxin 1 to a redox-sensitive GFP variant; the glutaredoxin
catalyzes rapid equilibrium between the probe's dithiol/disulfide state
and the GSH/GSSG couple, so that the fluorescence ratio maps onto the
glutathione potential through the Nernst relation quantified in Section
4 (Gutscher et al., 2008). Targeted variants of this probe supplied the
compartmental measurements that ground this monograph's reference
values, and related probes established that membraneless organelles
faithfully mirror the cytosolic glutathione environment and that the
endoplasmic reticulum maintains its characteristic oxidizing potential
of approximately −208 mV (Rovira & Rivera-Fuentes, 2025). The
hydrogen peroxide channel exists in two chemically instructive designs.
roGFP2-Orp1 couples the yeast peroxidase Orp1 to roGFP2, so that
peroxidase cycling—not direct reaction with H₂O₂—transfers oxidation
to the probe (Gutscher et al., 2009), an instrumentation-level
demonstration of the enzymatically gated edges of Section 5. HyPer7,

Interpretive discipline requires that the instruments' limitations be
stated with the same precision as their capabilities. First, pH: the
glutathione potential varies by approximately −59 mV per pH unit for
the GSSG/2GSH couple (Section 4), so EGSH readouts are interpretable
only with compartmental pH known or co-measured—a confound the
field addresses through pH-stable probe engineering and parallel pH
sensors (Pak et al., 2020). Second, kinetics: probe response times range
from sub-second to minutes, so instrument bandwidth filters the
transients the network actually generates, and fast excursions may be
underestimated. Third, calibration: conversion of fluorescence ratios to
absolute potentials or concentrations requires in situ titration under
defined pH, ionic strength, and temperature, consistent with the
comparability caveats of Section 4; published values remain
incomparable unless these conditions are fixed. Fourth, perturbation:
the probe is itself a—small—additional sink and source within the
network, with expression level and targeting as experimental variables
to be controlled rather than ignored.
With the heterogeneity argument of Section 14 and the instrument
class surveyed here, the vector of ratios becomes measurable at the
resolution of individual cells and organelles. The final section turns
from measurement to infrastructure and design: model interoperability
through ontology-based exchange, redox-aware metabolic engineering,
and the synthesis of the integrated-network framework.

16. Interoperability, Metabolic Engineering, and
Synthesis
The final components of the framework are infrastructural, and they
determine whether the preceding fourteen sections amount to a
durable scientific discipline or to a collection of well-instrumented
fragments. The first requirement is interoperability. A redox kinetic
model that cannot be exchanged without losing the semantic identity of
its redox-pair identifiers—GSH, GSSG, NADPH—or of its enzymatic
reactions and compartment annotations is not a durable scientific
object, because it cannot be reproduced, merged, or tested by others.
SBPAX (Systems Biology Pathway Exchange) addresses this by
functioning as an OWL ontology that bridges quantitative modeling
formats (SBML/VCML) with qualitative pathway knowledge (BioPAX):
its domain is the common subset of the two representations, and its
expressive power is their super-union (Ruebenacker et al., 2009). When
direct conversion between formats is impossible because of
ambiguities, models can be exported losslessly into SBPAX as an
intermediate, enriched with disambiguating information, and imported
into the target format. The operational lesson is that standards do not
replace measurements; they prevent fragmented maps from being
mistaken for different biologies—precisely the failure mode this
monograph has attributed to single-pair, bulk-averaged descriptions.
The second requirement is that design activities respect the same
network physics. Strain-design and flux-optimization algorithms search
over gene knockouts, enzyme expression levels, and flux caps to elevate
a product titer under stoichiometric—and sometimes
kinetic—constraints. Redox-blind objectives routinely return infeasible
designs, because if the solution collapses NAD(P)H balance, oxygen
uptake, or glutathione-supported detoxification, the predicted titer is
chemically unreachable. The Redox Imbalance Forces Drive (RIFD)
strategy, introduced in 2025, makes the redox dimension explicit
through four coordinated approaches to expanding the NADPH pool:
expression of cofactor-converting enzymes; expression of heterologous
cofactor-dependent enzymes; expression of NADPH-synthesis pathway
enzymes; and reduction of NADPH waste through knockdown of
non-essential genes (Jin et al., 2025). Experimentally validated in
Escherichia coli for L-threonine production, the strategy achieved a

titer of 117.65 g/L and a yield of 0.65 g/g with simultaneous restoration
of cell growth. The conceptual advance is direct: controlled redox
imbalance can serve as a driving force for production rather than
merely a stressor to be avoided. Multi-objective search that includes
ROS load and redox-pair potentials alongside carbon yield thereby
excludes designs that maximize yield on paper while collapsing
homeostasis—the winning production plan is the one that remains
within the out-of-equilibrium redox envelope (Jin et al., 2025).
The synthesis consolidates the argument into a single architecture. The
state of the system is the vector of ratios: multiplex across
NAD⁺/NADH, NADP⁺/NADPH, GSH/GSSG, and TrxSS/TrxSH,
compartment-resolved, and refined rather than erased by
membraneless organelles, which mirror the cytosolic glutathione
environment. Measurement of this state requires simultaneous
multi-pair, single-cell, compartment-resolved readouts—requirements
now met by the biosensor class of Section 15 and undermined by bulk
averaging, which conceals the subpopulations that determine
therapeutic outcomes. The chemistry that reads and writes this state is
enzymatically gated throughout: hydrogen peroxide acts as a second
messenger through selective, reversible cysteine oxidation, with
nuclear targets such as GCN5 connecting redox state to epigenetic
regulation; the peroxiredoxin/H₂O₂ module converts analog gradients
into digital outputs through zero-order cycling, multistep oxidation, and
hyperoxidation-gated positive feedback; hydrogen sulfide and
persulfides add a sulfur-signaling layer interlocking with oxygen
chemistry; and ferroptosis operationalizes redox control as a
life-or-death decision executed by three compartment-mapped removal
loops (GPX4/GSH, FSP1/CoQ, DHODH/CoQ). The pathology is
bidirectional: reductive stress, driven in part by reverse electron
transport, is as consequential as oxidative stress, and NAD⁺ decline
couples redox state to epigenetics and metabolism in aging. The
physiology is integrative: the circadian clock regulates and is regulated
by redox state; immune activation reprograms the metabolic wiring of
the vector; and the oxidizing endoplasmic reticulum ties protein-folding
load to the UPR (Jones & Sies, 2015; Sies & Jones, 2020).
Three perspectives follow. First, data infrastructure: the field will
benefit from standardized, multi-pair, compartment-resolved datasets

accompanied by ontology-based model exchange, so that kinetic models
accumulate rather than fragment. Second, therapy: the bidirectional
stress framework implies that interventions must restore the failing
loop—sensing, supply, or removal—rather than flood the system with
chemically unrelated reductants. Third, design: metabolic engineering
demonstrates that network awareness is productive, converting redox
imbalance from a constraint into an actuated driving force. The picture
that emerges is of the cell as an out-of-equilibrium electronic network,
engineered by evolution to keep its potentials where its enzymes need
them—and of the redox state, reported by the vector of ratios, as the
quantitative language in which that engineering is described,
measured, and redesigned.
