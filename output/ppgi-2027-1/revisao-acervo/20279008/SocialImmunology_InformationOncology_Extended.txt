Abstract
The growing complexity of biological and social systems demands modelling
paradigms that transcend disciplinary boundaries. This work explores a
profound conceptual and methodological analogy between the dynamics of
articial intelligence (AI) in human societies and the pathogenesis of complex
diseases such as cancer and autoimmune disorders. We argue that computa-
tional models developed to understand the spread of (dis)information, the
formation of echo chambers, and adversarial information warfare oer robust
frameworks for generating new hypotheses in biomedical investigation.
By framing cellcell interactions and immune responses through the lenses
of network theory, game theory, and control theory, we propose a trans-
disciplinary synthesis structured around four pillars:(i)propagation dy-
namics, from SIR-based information diusion to metastatic dissemination;
(ii)adversarial evasion, from computational propaganda to immune escape;
(iii)systemic failure, from echo chambers to autoimmunity; and(iv)net-
work intervention, from inoculation theory to immunotherapy and adaptive
therapy. Crucially, we extend the original framework by incorporating recent
advances in spatial transcriptomics for tumour microenvironment (TME)
mapping, multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) models of the TME,
and prebunking inoculation strategies. From this synthesis we derive three
formal, testable hypothesestheInformation Oncology Hypothesis, theSo-
cial Immunology Hypothesis, and theNetwork Therapeutics Hypothesisand
propose concrete experimental and computational approaches to validate
them. By recognising that an evading cancer cell and a disinformation bot
are playing dierent versions of the same adversarial game, we open fer-
tile new ground for discovery at the intersection of information science and
biomedicine.

1. Introduction: The Convergence of Systems Biology and
Computational Social Science
The history of science is punctuated by moments when a conceptual language de-
veloped in one eld unlocks progress in another. The application of thermodynamic
entropy to information theory by Shannon [1] is one such moment; the use of epi-
demic modelling to describe the spread of ideas and behaviours in social networks
is another [2]. We are now witnessing a third such convergence: the emergence
of acommon network grammarfor describing complex adaptive systems, whether
biological or social.
The paradigm ofnetwork medicineserves as the fundamental theoretical bridge [3].
This eld treats both cell and individual populations as nodes in dynamic networks,
where interactionscellcell signalling, information sharingdetermine system be-
haviour. The central premise is that network topology and dynamics, rather than
the properties of individual nodes, govern health and disease outcomes as well as
social stability and fragmentation. A genetic abnormality, for instance, does not
act in isolation; its impact propagates through the network, altering the activity of
other gene products in ways that resist single-gene analysis [3].
This network perspective oers a quantitative platform to address the complexity
of human diseases, shifting focus from individual components to the functionality
of interconnected modules and pathways. At the same time, computational social
science has developed increasingly sophisticated toolsepidemic models, cascade
dynamics, game-theoretic frameworks, and network control theoryto describe the
behaviour of large populations of interacting agents [4].
The present work systematically exploits the isomorphism between these two do-
mains. We proceed from the following observation:the adversarial, adaptive, and
systemic challenges faced by the immune system and by a healthy information ecosys-
tem are structurally identical. Both must detect weak signals in noisy environments,
defend against entities that evolve to evade detection, and maintain homeostasis
against the tendency of feedback loops to produce runaway behaviour.

In parallel, the mathematical modelling of cancer cell proliferation has evolved

conceptualised as a contagion process in which the host is the human body, can-
cer cells are the infectious agent, and the vascular and lymphatic networks are
the transmission pathways [23]. Transport-equation models have formalised this
analogy, linking the rate of metastatic cell emission to primary tumour volume [24].
Key Insight
The transition from mean-eld SIR models to network-structured models repre-
sents a key advance in both domains. Early SIR models assume random mixing;
in reality, information diusion is governed by the presence of hubs and commu-
nities, just as cancer progression is dictated by the spatial heterogeneity of the
tumour microenvironment (TME). The topology of the underlying network
not just the rate constantsβandαdetermines whether and how a contagion
reaches pandemic or metastatic scale.

3.2. Information Cascade and Cellular Invasion: The Logic of Collective
Behaviour
While epidemiological models describe the spread of a single entity, information
cascade models explain how collective behaviours emerge from sequential individual
decisions [26]. In the classical Bayesian cascade model, perfectly rational agents may
nonetheless ignore their private information and mimic the actions of predecessors
aninformational externalitythat leads to imperfect information aggregation [27, 28].
Once started, a cascade is self-reinforcing: each additional individual who adopts
the behaviour makes the next adoption more likely, explaining the rapid rise of fads,
market bubbles, and political movements [26].
A key property of cascade dynamics ismetastability: an established cascade, while
appearing robust, can be destabilised by the arrival of suciently strong contrary
public information [28]. This property maps remarkably well onto the concept
of tumour dormancy and escape. The tumour microenvironment can exist in a
metastable state that either suppresses the tumour (immune-mediated dormancy)
or promotes its growth (pro-tumoral cascade) [29]. A small perturbationa tar-
geted drug, an inammatory signal, a dietary changecould function as the public
information that destabilises the pro-tumoral cascade and pushes the system back
toward homeostasis [30].
This reframing shifts cancer therapy fromkilling cellstodisrupting collective sig-
nalling cascades. Bayesian social-learning models [31], which describe how agents
update beliefs based on neighbours' actions, could be adapted to model how cancer
cells infer the state of their environment from the signalling signatures of adja-
cent cellsfor instance, updating the decision to undergo epithelial-to-mesenchymal
(EMT) transition based on the cumulative evidence of matrix-metalloproteinase
(MMP) secretion by neighbouring cells [32].

4.1. Immune Evasion and the Camouage of Disinformation
The progression of cancer from a localised lesion to a systemic disease depends
critically on its ability to escape immune surveillance, a process formalised asim-
munoediting[33], which comprises three phases:
i.Elimination: the immune system successfully destroys aberrant cells.
ii.Equilibrium: a selective pressure phase in which resistant clones emerge
through Darwinian selection.
iii.Escape: cancer clones that have evolved evasion mechanisms proliferate with-
out immune control.
Evasion mechanisms are diverse: downregulation of MHC-I surface expression (ren-
dering cells invisible to cytotoxic T lymphocytes), overexpression of inhibitory
checkpoint ligands such as PD-L1 and CD47 (don't attack me signals), and secre-
tion of immunosuppressive cytokines (TGF-β, IL-10, VEGF) that suppress the local
immune response [34, 35].
This cat-and-mouse game has a direct structural parallel in the digital informa-
tion world. Adversarial agentspolitical bots, troll accounts, coordinated inauthen-
tic behaviour networkscontinuously evolve tactics to mimic genuine human users,
evading detection by platform algorithms [36]. The eld ofadversarial machine
learning(AML) formalises this: just as a cancer cell presents a subtly modied
peptide to avoid T-cell receptor (TCR) recognition, an adversarial example alters a
few pixels to fool a classier [37].
A particularly illuminating analogy exists betweenpoisoning attacksin AMLin
which an adversary manipulates the training data to corrupt a classier [38]and
the immunosuppressive reprogramming of the TME. Cancer cells poison the mi-
croenvironment with cytokines that convert incoming monocytes into immunosup-
pressive M2 macrophages and induce regulatory T cells, eectively misTraining the
immune system to tolerate tumour antigens rather than reject them [39]. This for-
mal equivalence suggests that AML defences against poisoning attacksadversarial
training, certied robustness, ensemble methodscould inspire analogous strategies
to restore immune competence in the TME.
Similarly, disinformation can be laundered through legitimate-seeming secondary
sources to build credibility [40], a tactic analogous tomolecular mimicry, where a
pathogen displays molecules resembling host antigens to evade immune response [41].
In both cases, the defence system fails not because the adversary is stronger, but
because the adversary has reduced the signal-to-noise ratio below the detection
threshold.

4.2. Adversarial Co-evolution: Game Theory in the Tumour Microenvi-
ronment
The dynamic interplay between a defence system and an evolving adversary is for-
mally described byEvolutionary Game Theory(EGT) [42]. In EGT, players
are not rational agents but populations with heritable strategies whose tness de-
pends on the composition of the populationa formalism perfectly suited to both
the TME and the information ecosystem.
Cancer is a paradigmatic evolutionary game [43]. Within the TME, dierent cell
populationsdrug-sensitive clones, resistant clones, immune cells, cancer-associated
broblasts (CAFs)compete for limited resources. EGT models have demonstrated
a critical aw inmaximum tolerated dose(MTD) therapy: by eliminating drug-
sensitive cells, MTD creates an ecological vacuum (competitive release) that resis-
tant clones inevitably ll [15].
The EGT-inspired alternative isadaptive therapy: treating cancer not as an en-
emy to be eradicated but as an ecosystem to be managed [15]. The goal is to maintain
a population of therapy-sensitive cells to competitively suppress resistant cells, steer-
ing the system toward controlled, low-burden equilibrium. Clinical trials of adaptive
therapy in prostate cancer have demonstrated signicant reductions in cumulative
drug exposure while maintaining comparable progression-free survival [44].
Key Insight
The zero-tolerance approach to cancer therapy and the zero-tolerance ap-
proach to content moderation share the same structural failure mode: both
drive adversarial populations toward more sophisticated, resistant phenotypes,
and both deplete the sensitive population that had been keeping the resistant
population in check. Adaptive therapy and adaptive moderation converge on
the same ecological insight:management, not eradication, achieves long-term
system health.
Multi-agent reinforcement learning(MARL) extends EGT by allowing agents
to learn policies through trial-and-error in non-stationary environments [45]. Ap-
plied to information warfare, MARL models explore the emergence of cooperation
and defection in resource-gathering games that structurally resemble the compe-
tition between malicious agents and defenders [46]. Directly translated to the
TME, the agents become cell types (cancer clones, CD8 + T cells, Tregs, M1/M2
macrophages), the actions become biological behaviours (proliferation, apoptosis,
migration, cytokine secretion), and the reward becomes survival and replication
tness. MARL-TME models could capture the spatial and temporal complexity of
intratumoural interactions beyond what ODE-based EGT models can represent [14],
and could be used to discover in silico adaptive therapy schedules that exploit com-

5. Systemic Failure:
Tolerance, Autoimmunity, and Echo Chambers
5.1. Autoimmunity as a Regulatory Control Failure
One of the most remarkable feats of the immune system isself-tolerance: the
ability to mount potent responses against foreign pathogens while avoiding attack
on the body's own tissues [47]. This balance is maintained by a multi-layered system
of checks:
i.Central tolerancein the thymus, where developing T-lymphocytes undergo
positive and negative selection. Thymocytes that react strongly to self-antigens
presented by medullary thymic epithelial cells (mTECs) are eliminated via
apoptosis in a process controlled by the transcription factor AIRE [48, 49].
ii.Peripheral tolerance, maintained in part byregulatory T cells(Tregs)
a specialised population expressing the master transcription factor FOXP3
which suppress excessive eector T-cell responses and prevent self-reactivity [13].
Autoimmunity arises when this system fails. Genetic defects (mutations in AIRE
or FOXP3), thymic involution with ageing, or environmental triggers such as viral
infection can cause self-reactive T-cell clones to escape into the circulation and
mount attacks on host tissue, producing diseases such as type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid
arthritis, and multiple sclerosis [50].
Computational models have illuminated these dynamics. ODE-based models of in-
tracellular signalling capture the positive and negative feedback loops that govern T-
cell activation and anergy [51]. Population-level models of eectorTreg interactions
quantify how depletion or dysfunction of Tregs leads to runaway inammation [52].
A key insight from these models is that Treg-mediated suppression acts not simply
by killing eector cells but by modulating their division destinythe number of
times a T cell divides before ceasing [53]a distinction with important implications
for designing therapies that restore tolerance.

6. Intervention and Control: Targeted Therapy to Informa-
tion Inoculation
6.1. Network Control Theory for Steering Cell Fate and Public Opinion
Network Control Theory(NCT) is a mathematical framework, borrowed from
engineering, that addresses a fundamental question: given a complex network, which
nodes or edges should we target to steer the entire system from an initial state to a
desired target state? [16, 58]
In systems biology, NCT identies driver nodestypically transcription factors
in gene regulatory networks (GRNs)whose manipulation can force a cellular net-
work to transition from one attractor state (e.g., a cancer cell) to another (e.g., a
dierentiated or apoptotic cell) [17, 59]. The mathematical condition for controlla-
bility, derived from the Kalman rank condition, species the minimum set of nodes
required to control all states of the network. Applied to the TME, NCT combined
with spatial transcriptomics and causal discovery methods [60] provides a principled
framework for identifying which ligandreceptor interactions constitute the most
ecient control targets for reprogramming the tumour ecosystem.

biomedicine and may be more durable than node-targeting therapies vulnerable to

6.2. Immunotherapy and Inoculation Theory: Reinforcing Endogenous
Resilience
A powerful class of interventions in both domains seeks not to impose external
control but to bolster the system's endogenous capacity to resist perturbations.
Inoculation theory, rst proposed by McGuire [63] and substantially developed
since [64, 65], posits that resistance to persuasion and disinformation can be con-
ferred by pre-exposing individuals to a weakened form of a counter-argument. The
inoculation process has two components:
1. Athreat: a warning that one's beliefs are about to be challenged, motivating
attitudinal defence.
2. Arefutational preemption: a weakened counter-argument that the individual can
easily rebut, building a repertoire of defensive responses.
Meta-analytic evidence conrms eectiveness across a wide range of persuasive mes-
sages, from advertising to health and climate misinformation [64]. Recent extensions,
notably the prebunking framework [66], have generalised inoculation from content-
specic to technique-specic interventionstraining people to recognisecategories
of manipulative reasoning (e.g., false dichotomies, emotional appeals, impersonation)
rather than specic false claims, substantially improving scalability.
Cancer immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) ther-
apy, operates on a structurally identical principle [67]. Tumours exploit endogenous
immune checkpoints (PD-1/PD-L1, CTLA-4) to shield themselves from T-cell at-
tack. ICIs block these brakes, unleashing pre-existing anti-tumour T-cell responses
that had been suppressed. Therapeutic cancer vaccines, meanwhile, provide anti-
gen presentationa refutational preemption at the molecular level, equipping

6.3. Dynamic Network Biomarkers to Predict Intervention Response
One of the greatest challenges in applying interventions to complex systems is re-
sponse heterogeneity. Not all patients respond to immunotherapy; not all social
networks respond to a depolarisation campaign.
In oncology, static biomarkers such as PD-L1 expression and tumour mutational
burden (TMB) have limited predictive power [69, 70]. This has driven interest in
dynamic biomarkers: measurements that capturechangesin the TME during
treatmentshifts in peripheral immune cell subsets, transcriptional reprogramming
captured by serial liquid biopsies, or network measures derived from spatial multi-
omic data [71].
The conceptual parallel in social network science is the use of polarisation metrics
as intervention biomarkers: homophily (the fraction of links between same-opinion
nodes) and modularity (community structure strength) can serve as indicators of
a network's susceptibility to a depolarisation campaign [8]. A highly modular, ho-
mophilic network will be resistant to intervention; a more integrated network may
be receptive.

The preceding analysis converges on three formal, testable hypotheses for future

Hypothesis H1  The Information Oncology Hypothesis

The ability to steer complex systemswhether to reprogram a cell's fate or to inu-

Transparency:computational models that identify driver nodes for cell-fate re-

Black-box MARL policies applied to TME modelling or opinion nudging are in-

9. Conclusion
The convergence of information science, AI, and systems biology oers more than
new computational toolsit oers a new grammar for describing complexity. By
recognising that an evading cancer cell and a disinformation bot are playing dierent
versions of the same adversarial game, and that a dysregulated immune system and
a polarised society suer from analogous failures of regulatory control, we open a
vast and fertile ground for scientic discovery.
The three hypotheses formalised herethe Information Oncology Hypothesis, the
Social Immunology Hypothesis, and the Network Therapeutics Hypothesisprovide
a concrete research programme. Their validation requires genuine interdisciplinary
collaboration: oncologists who understand tumour immunology working with com-
putational social scientists who understand network dynamics; cancer biologists who
can read spatial transcriptomics working with machine learning researchers who can
build MARL models of multi-agent systems. The framework developed here oers
a common language for that collaboration.
The future of medicine may depend not only on our ability to decipher the genome,
but also on our ability to read and rewrite the language of the networks that govern
both life and society.
