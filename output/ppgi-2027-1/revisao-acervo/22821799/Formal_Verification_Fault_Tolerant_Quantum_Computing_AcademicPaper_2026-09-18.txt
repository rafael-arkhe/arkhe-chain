Abstract
The Quantum Genesis Q competition, announced by the United States Department of
Energy (DOE) in September 2026 with planned funding of up to $215 million, established
the first explicit engineering target for fault-tolerant, scientifically relevant quantum
computers (SRQC): at least 100 logical qubits performing hundreds of millions of
fault-tolerant operations — including the program's defined ladder of hard, non-Clifford
operations — by 2028, with every layer of the computing stack subject to independent
validation by a $45 million V&V; Testbed. This monograph argues that in this operational
regime verification ceases to be a support activity and becomes an engineering
prerequisite, and it presents a revised, unified verification architecture that integrates the
physical-mathematical modeling of PACIR (Tsallis entropy, the Penrose–Diósi model,
quantum marginals) with the formal verification of quantum error correction provided by
Arkhe OS. Relative to an earlier draft, this version incorporates an external audit:
substantive (non-tautological) contract-based Kani harnesses with negative and existence
assertions, a coherent invariant numbering that separates software invariants (INV-SW)
from empirical hardware metrics (MET-HW), explicit falsifiers for every empirical metric, a
declared table of opaque API dependencies, a fallback protocol for non-terminating proofs,
and — as the central conceptual contribution — the Verification Gap: a six-node, five-link
decomposition (Intention → Specification → Physical model → Software → Hardware →
World) that assigns each link its admissible class of evidence. The Tsallis module remains
the complete case study: three implementation branches with numerically justified
boundaries, ten verifiable invariants, five Kani harnesses with bit-precise universal proofs
up to n = 8 via loop contracts, and an optimal Lipschitz constant derived analytically for the
Hölder-continuity harness. We conclude that transforming engineering requirements into
verifiable contracts — while stating precisely what is proven, sampled, or merely
measured — is a concrete path toward meeting the DOE's validation and verification
mandate.
Keywords: fault-tolerant quantum computing; quantum error correction; formal verification;
Tsallis entropy; Kani; Quantum Genesis Q

1.1 Context
Fault-tolerant quantum computing has ceased to be a theoretical promise and has become
an engineering goal with concrete deadlines. On September 16–17, 2026, the United States
Department of Energy (DOE) announced the Quantum Genesis Q competition, a
first-of-its-kind effort with planned funding of up to $215 million to demonstrate the world's
first fault-tolerant, scientifically relevant quantum computer (SRQC): a system with at least
100 logical qubits, capable of executing hundreds of millions of fault-tolerant operations and
scientific demonstration programs by 2028 [1, 2, 20]. The initiative builds on the Genesis
Mission and implements the goals of the Executive Order “Ushering in the Next Frontier of
Quantum Innovation” and the Blueprint for DOE Quantum Supercomputing [1].
The competition does not target a single device class. As documented in the program
materials [1, 2], the performance metrics form a three-rung ladder of hard operations —
operations from the hard (non-Clifford) family of the participant's error-correction scheme,
the genuinely scarce resource in fault-tolerant computation:

The competition is not an isolated event. In June 2026, D-Wave announced a gate-model
roadmap targeting 100 logical qubits performing more than one million operations by 2032
[16]; in late 2024, Google's Willow processor demonstrated, for the first time, surface-code
operation below threshold, with logical error suppression by a factor Λ = 2.14 ± 0.02 per
two-step distance increase [12]. The field is thus transitioning from the NISQ regime
characterized by Preskill [13] to scalable fault tolerance. The gap between 10⁶ operations
(D-Wave, 2032) and 10⁸ operations (DOE, 2028) illustrates how aggressive the competition
target is — and why verification sits at the center of the problem.
1.2 The Verification Problem
The central challenge is not merely to build quantum hardware, but to verify that the system
computes correctly. Executing hundreds of millions of fault-tolerant operations imposes, by
an elementary union-bound argument, a per-operation logical error rate target of order
10⁻⁸: with N = 10⁸ operations and per-operation logical error pL, the survival probability of
the full computation is (1 − pL)N ≈ e−N·pL, so pL ≈ 10⁻⁸ already reduces the survival
probability to e−1 ≈ 0.37; any substantially larger value renders the complete computation
unlikely (Section 3.3, Equation 3.1).
In the surface-code model, a logical error rate of 10⁻⁸ calls for code distances of order d =
17 at physical error rates near 10⁻³ — tens of thousands of physical qubits per configuration
(Section 7.1). In this regime, informal verification — point tests, manual inspection, trust in
prototype reviews — is insufficient for three converging reasons. First, the state space of a
system with 10⁵ components is not explorable by naive sampling. Second, the competition's
operating conditions (phased milestones, external validation by the Testbed) demand
auditable, reproducible evidence rather than declarations. Third, the quantum stack
contains numerically delicate components — decoders, entropy estimators, noise models —
whose failures are silent and surface only under extreme inputs. Formal verification and
rigorous physical modeling therefore become engineering prerequisites. One further
distinction governs everything that follows: what is genuinely proven (software, under a
stated execution model) must never be conflated with what is measured empirically
(hardware). Chapter 8 formalizes this distinction as the Verification Gap.

1.3 Objectives
General objective: to develop and document a unified verification architecture for
fault-tolerant quantum computing, integrating the physical-mathematical modeling of
PACIR (Tsallis entropy, the Penrose–Diósi model, quantum marginals) with the QEC and
fault-tolerance verification of Arkhe OS, through verifiable contracts, falsifiable invariants,
and coverage combining exhaustive proofs (Kani) with property-based testing (proptest).
The general objective decomposes into five specific objectives:
1. To implement and verify the Tsallis entropy module with fail-closed contracts and Kani
proofs, covering the exact, series, and general computation regimes;
2. To implement and verify the Arkhe OS QEC modules (codes, logical error rate model,
decoding) with falsifiable invariants;
3. To integrate the modules into a single Cargo workspace with automated verification
pipelines (CI, xtask, record hashes);
4. To map every Quantum Genesis Q requirement onto a verifiable invariant with an
explicit falsifier — and every empirical metric onto an independent measurement
criterion;
5. To document with epistemic honesty what is proven universally (Kani), what is
sampled (proptest), and what is not proven.
1.4 Rationale
The competition establishes objective metrics — logical qubits, hard operations, logical
error rate — and, simultaneously, creates the first public layer-by-layer validation apparatus
for the quantum stack [1]. Every metric can be translated into a falsifiable invariant: a
precisely stated claim accompanied by the concrete procedure that would refute it. The
PACIR + Arkhe OS integration proposes exactly this: transforming engineering
requirements into verifiable contracts, with formal evidence where possible and sampled
evidence where necessary.
The rationale is threefold. From the scientific standpoint, the Tsallis entropy is the most
widely applied non-extensive generalization of Boltzmann–Gibbs statistics — from LHC
particle spectra [4] to the decoherence dynamics of RbCl quantum-dot qubits [25] — and
deserves the same implementation rigor as error-correcting codes; sign errors in series
expansions, for instance, silently invalidate physical applications. From the engineering 
standpoint, the recent record of bounded model checking in production Rust — Kani's
standard-library campaign verifies over 16,000 harnesses per code change, and its
contract-based Hifitime case study uncovered six previously unknown bugs [8] —
demonstrates that formal methods capture whole defect classes that conventional testing
misses. From the programmatic standpoint, aligning with the competition's phase,
milestone, and layer-validation structure increases the auditability of a future candidacy and
reduces the risk of structural rework.

2 Theoretical Background
2.1 Quantum Error Correction (QEC)
2.1.1 Surface codes
The surface code is the leading candidate for error correction in solid-state hardware,
combining relatively high thresholds with nearest-neighbor interactions on a
two-dimensional lattice [10, 11]. Physical qubits are arranged in a mesh where data and
measurement qubits alternate; stabilizers — products of Pauli operators — are measured
repeatedly, and the discrepancy between consecutive measurements (the syndrome) feeds a
decoder that infers the most likely error chain. The practical result is a logical qubit whose
error rate decreases exponentially with the code distance d, provided the physical error rate
remains below the threshold [11]. For the regime of interest, the empirical model of Fowler
et al. [10] describes the relationship:

estimates [10, 14]. Budgets of this scale turn allocation decisions (code distance, decoding
budget, characterized layers) into engineering decisions with direct economic
consequences, reinforcing the need for verifiable contracts at every level of the stack.
2.1.2 Real-time decoding
Quantum error correction is a streaming problem: stabilizers are measured every cycle, and
the accumulated syndrome must be decoded faster than the logical qubit's coherence
time — otherwise the decoder backlog degrades the effective error rate and can nullify the
code's gain [10, 12]. The experimental state of the art has consolidated decisively. The
HELIOS system, described in Godawatte Liyanage's doctoral thesis (Yale, 2025), achieves 
11.5 ns of latency per round at distance 21 on a Xilinx VCU129 FPGA, with sustained
real-time decoding up to distance 51 under 0.1% phenomenological noise [22]. Because
HELIOS addresses a single logical qubit, the competition-relevant reference is its
networked successor: DECONET, which integrates multiple interacting logical qubits and
demonstrates real-time decoding of 100 logical qubits at distance 5 with a mean latency of
2.40 μs and inverse throughput of 0.84 μs per round under 0.1% noise [22, 23].
Independently, Caune et al. demonstrated low-latency feedback with a scalable FPGA

Second, a decoder that silently exceeds its time budget is a first-class failure mode, which

development, documented in Section 6.4.

2.2.5 Lesche stability and the homogeneous entropy
An entropy is Lesche-stable (continuously stable) when nearby probability maps produce
nearby entropy values, uniformly across dimensions — the property that legitimizes
comparing experimentally measured entropies across systems of different sizes [6]. Lesche
showed in 1982 that the Rényi entropies are unstable in this sense [6]. For Tsallis the
picture is subtler and was clarified by Lutsko, Boon and Grosfils (2009) [5]: the Tsallis
entropy is not Lesche-stable when escort probabilities are used for physical averages — the
prevailing practice in non-extensive thermodynamics. Stability is recovered only when the 
homogeneous entropy is adopted as the basis of the non-extensive formalism; notably,
Lutsko et al. show that thermodynamic averages remain stable even when the entropy itself
does not [5]. Abe (2002) complements this picture with the stability analysis of normalized
forms [7].

2.3 Formal Verification of Numerical Software
2.3.1 Kani: bit-precise universal proofs
Kani is an open-source, bit-precise model checker for Rust, developed with roots at AWS,
that compiles proof harnesses from Rust's Mid-level Intermediate Representation (MIR) into
CBMC's bit-precise verification engine [8, 9]. Harnesses construct symbolic inputs with
kani::any(), declare preconditions with kani::assume(), and assert properties with
kani::assert(). When the SAT solver finds a counterexample, Kani reproduces it with
concrete values and an execution trace; when it proves unsatisfiability, the property holds
universally within the declared scope [8]. Beyond panic-freedom — checked automatically
with no user annotation — Kani's specification language extends verification from bounded
to unbounded through function contracts, loop contracts, quantifiers, and function stubbing 
[8].
Three characteristics are decisive for the numerical software treated here. First, the
semantics are bit-precise over the IEEE-754 domain — proofs hold over the real space of f64
bit patterns, including quiet NaNs, ±Inf, and subnormals, not over the mathematical body
ℝ; this makes proofs consistent with the machine but also delimits their scope (Section 5.4).
Second, loop contracts — the kani::loop_invariant and kani::loop_decreases annotations —
allow proving properties of loops without unrolling every iteration, with production code
kept identical when the annotations are conditioned on Kani compilation via cfg_attr. The
practical gain is dramatic: in the project's exemplar case, verifying an f64-based Duration
multiplication takes 91.8 s without the contract and 0.2 s with it — two orders of magnitude;
in the context of the full crate, the contracted verification completes in under 70 s where
the uncontracted run is intractable. Third, the class of guarantee is not fixed: Kani's default
campaign proves panic-freedom, while contract-based verification upgrades the guarantee
to functional correctness. The ASE 2026 report quantifies this progression at production
scale: over 16,000 harnesses verified per code change in the Rust standard library
campaign, and a Hifitime case study adding 125 contract annotations to 153 harnesses over
a 9.5K-line library — which uncovered six previously unknown bugs, including a violation of
the standard library's Eq and Ord trait contracts [8]. For a monograph arguing that
verification is an engineering prerequisite, this is the strongest available empirical
argument: contracts did not merely decorate the code — they changed the class of
guarantee and found real bugs.

Honest use of Kani also requires recording its trust base. Kani operates over a model of

This is exactly the scope claim the Verification Gap (Chapter 8) assigns to link E3 — and no

2.3.2 Proptest: property-based sampling coverage
Proptest is Rust's reference library for property-based testing with explicit strategies and
automatic counterexample shrinking [19]. Unlike QuickCheck, whose generation derives
from types, proptest allows composable declarative strategies — ranges, weighted unions
(prop_oneof!), collections, transformations — concentrating sampling where numerical risk
resides [19]. The strategy proptest::num::f64::ANY generates literally arbitrary f64 values,
including all quiet NaNs, ±Inf, and subnormals (though not signaling NaNs) — a decisive
contrast with naive random generators, which rarely produce pathological values.
Proptest is complementary to Kani, not a substitute. Where Kani proves universally for fixed
n (or n delimited by contracts), proptest explores variable n and larger input spaces under a
sampling budget; where Kani delivers exhaustively minimal counterexamples, proptest
delivers fast, reproducible reductions (seed persistence in proptest-regressions). The PACIR
+ Arkhe OS architecture formalizes this complementarity in the coverage pyramid of Figure
3 (Chapter 5): point tests at the base, sampling above, delimited exhaustive proof above
that, and, at the top, by-construction guarantees from the type system.
2.4 Formal Verification of QEC Programs: Veri-QEC
A distinct line of work verifies quantum programs rather than classical software. Veri-QEC,
published at PLDI 2025, is an automatic verification framework for quantum error
correcting programs: it defines an assertion logic and a program logic specifically crafted
for QEC, with a proof system whose soundness is formalized in Coq [21]. For Pauli errors,
verification conditions reduce to classical assertions discharged by SMT solvers; for
non-Pauli errors, a heuristic algorithm applies. The framework was evaluated on a
benchmark of 14 stabilizer codes [21].
The scope distinction matters for everything that follows: Veri-QEC operates at the level of
quantum circuits and QEC protocols, while Kani operates at the level of classical software 
(Rust) — the decoders, controllers, and modeling code that surround the quantum program.
They are complementary, not competing. The PACIR + Arkhe OS architecture therefore
positions itself explicitly in a two-layer verification ecosystem:

4.2 Design Principles
Four principles govern the architecture and explain every non-obvious structural decision.
1. Fail-closed. Every invalid input produces a typed Err — never a panic, never a silently
corrected value. The policy is systematic: negative weights, NaN, ±Inf, non-normalized
sums, non-Hermitian matrices, negative eigenvalues, and invalid entropic indices each
have dedicated error variants (TsallisError), and the tests assert the rejection, not merely
the absence of panic. The principle converts the security posture “deny by default” into a
typing requirement.
2. Record hash. Every verification produces a registered blake3 hash. Evidence is
content-addressable: the hash uniquely identifies (code, inputs, parameters, result),
allowing the Testbed — and any auditor — to re-execute and cross-check without trusting
external metadata.
3. Falsifiable invariants. Every invariant has a concrete, executable falsifier (Tabela 3 and
Appendix A). Invariants without falsifiers are rhetorical declarations; the architecture's
catalog refuses to contain them.
4. Domain separation. PACIR models physics; Arkhe verifies engineering. The boundary
is deliberate: physical models (with their own theoretical uncertainties, such as the
experimental status of the Penrose–Diósi model) must not cross-contaminate the binary
engineering contracts (LER ≤ target, decoding time ≤ coherence). The bridge between
domains runs through verifiable contracts and record hashes — never through code
dependencies.

// crates/pacir-core/src/tsallis.rs (excerpt)
#[inline]
pub fn stable_power_sum(weights: &[f64], q: f64) -> f64 {
    let mut max_log = f64::NEG_INFINITY;
    let mut log_terms: Vec<f64> = Vec::with_capacity(weights.len());
    let mut i = 0usize;
    #[cfg_attr(kani, kani::loop_invariant(i <= weights.len()))]
    #[cfg_attr(kani, kani::loop_decreases(weights.len() - i))]
    while i < weights.len() {
        let w = weights[i];
        i += 1;
        if w <= 0.0 { continue; }
        let lt = q * w.ln();
        if lt > max_log { max_log = lt; }
        log_terms.push(lt);
    }
    // ... log-sum-exp composition
}

scope is not arbitrary: it delimits the SMT solver's path explosion to a verification budget
that runs in CI on every commit. Section 5.4 documents exactly what this scope leaves
uncovered, and Section 5.6 defines the fallback protocol when a harness fails to terminate.

Two design rules generalize from these corrections. First, every harness over an opaque API
must include a negative property (invalid input ⇒ rejection) so that a trivially failing
implementation cannot satisfy the invariant vacuously. Second, where feasible, harnesses
should include existence assertions — a witness configuration for which the contract
returns acceptance — proving the function is not permanently closed. Kani's assume/assert
structure supports both patterns directly [9]. The set of opaque APIs and their assumed
contracts is declared explicitly in Appendix B (API Dependencies); if any of these functions
changes, the corresponding harnesses must be re-run, and the proofs are conditional on
those contracts.

5.5 Guarantee Classes
Beyond the coverage hierarchy, the architecture tracks the class of guarantee each
mechanism provides — a distinction the Kani ASE 2026 report makes explicit at production
scale [8]. Three classes matter here. Panic-freedom: no execution within scope diverges or
panics; Kani proves this by default, with no user annotation. Functional correctness: the
code computes what the specification states; this is the class the contract-based harnesses
of Sections 5.1–5.2 upgrade to, and the class whose absence the six Hifitime bugs
exemplify — those bugs were found precisely when contracts upgraded the verification
beyond panic-freedom [8]. By-construction guarantees: properties enforced by the type
system and API design (typed errors, total validation), which no test or proof needs to
re-establish. Every row of the invariant catalog (Appendix A) carries its tool column
precisely so that the class of guarantee is never implied where it does not hold.
5.6 Reducing Harnesses and the Fallback Protocol
Bounded model checking has a budget. The project's reduction strategies, in order of
preference, are: (1) concretize values — replace kani::any() with concrete values for
variables that control loop bounds, drastically shrinking the state space; (2) tune the
unwind — bound loop iterations with #[kani::unwind(N)], knowing that too low a bound fails
the proof and too high a bound invites timeout; (3) bound the solver — set --cbmc-timeout
per harness so a single hard harness cannot stall CI; and (4) assert existence — prove that a


=== PAGINA 26 ===
Formal Verification and Fault-Tolerant Quantum Computing
Page 26
valid input succeeds, guarding against vacuous truth where an always-failing function
would make every implication trivially hold (Section 5.2).
When a harness still fails to terminate, the fallback protocol is explicit and non-negotiable.
First, reduce the scope: prove n = 2 instead of n = 8, and document the reduction in the
verification report. Second, substitute sampling: run 10⁴ adversarial proptest cases —
strong statistical evidence, but not proof. Third, record the outcome: the xtask verify-all
report emits, for every harness, a status of proved, reduced (with the reduced parameters),
proptest-only (with the case count), or unproved (with the justification) — and an invariant
is never marked as proven without a proof (Appendix B). This protocol converts CI timeouts
from a silent integrity risk into a first-class, auditable outcome.

6.1 The Physical Contract
The module implements the classical Tsallis entropy (2.2) and the quantum version, defined
over the eigenvalues of the density matrix ρ:

The quantum version depends only on ρ's spectrum — spectral invariance is invariant

finiteness of every component, Hermiticity (‖ρ − ρ†‖ ≤ 10⁻¹⁰ per element), unit trace (|Tr ρ

implement INV-TS-04 in the quantum domain and mirror the classical domain's treatment,
where non-finite, negative, or non-normalized weights (sum ≠ 1 ± 10⁻¹⁰) are rejected
explicitly, with the offending weight's index carried in the error. The manual comparison of
TsallisError (impl PartialEq) ignores f64 fields and compares variants — a decision that
preserves InvalidQ(NaN) == InvalidQ(NaN) and, with it, the testability of variants.

An earlier draft of the Hölder-continuity harness used the constant K = 2.0 as an ad-hoc
value, and an intermediate revision proposed the expression K = 2|ln(p/(1−p))| + 2 as an
“optimal” constant. Direct differentiation shows both choices can be improved by a
transparent analytical derivation. For q = 2 over two outcomes, the entropy has the closed

since Σpᵢ² = p² + (1−p)² = 1 − 2p(1−p) and S₂ = 1 − Σpᵢ². The derivative is linear, so the
optimal Lipschitz constant over any domain D ⊆ [0,1] is the maximum absolute slope:

On the harness's effective domain — the perturbed weight is clamped to [10⁻⁶, 1 − 10⁻⁶] —
the supremum is 2 − 4×10⁻⁶ → 2, so the harness's existing constant K = 2 is optimal up to
2×10⁻⁶ and the assertion |ΔS₂| ≤ 2|δ| + 10⁻¹² holds with no slack beyond the floating-point

K* = 1.96. The proposed logarithmic expression, by contrast, yields ≈ 11.2 at p = 0.01 — a
valid but far from optimal upper bound — and diverges as p → 0, where the true slope
converges to 2. We therefore retain K = 2 for the clamped harness (now justified rather
than assumed), record 1.96 as the domain-restricted refinement, and note the
methodological lesson: constants used in formal assertions should be derived, and the
derivation recorded alongside the proof, so that the assertion is a certificate rather than a
guess.

7.2 arkhe-fault-tolerant-verify
The fault-tolerance module verifies the two restrictions that define fault-tolerant operation
in the competition's sense. The first is temporal: decoding each cycle must complete before
the logical qubit's coherence time expires (Section 2.1.2); the module requires tdec <
Tcoherence and records violations as failures, not degradations. The second is the operations
budget: each logical operation consumes, in the module's model, a budget of 10 correction
cycles (including gauge fixing, syndrome extraction, and proportional distillation), so the
complete N-operation computation accumulates the failure risk:

7.3 arkhe-quantum-testbed
The testbed module mirrors, as data structures, the six characterization layers required by
the DOE's V&V; Lab Call: PhysicalHardware, QuantumGates, LogicalArchitecture,
QuantumAlgorithms, Applications, and ClassicalControl [1]. Each layer is characterized
independently — its own metrics, its own procedure, its own owner — and each
characterization emits a blake3 record hash that uniquely identifies the result. The design
follows the principle that layer-by-layer validation demands content-addressable evidence:
re-executing a characterization procedure must reproduce its hash, and any divergence is
detectable without trusting logs or external metadata. The methodological foundation for
comparing testbeds — standardized, low-level noise characterization across federally
funded platforms — follows the stakeholder workshop white paper of Shaydulin, Suchara
and Larson [26], which informs the metrics vocabulary the module records per layer.
The module's three invariants guarantee the process's integrity. INV-QTB-01
(uncharacterized layer → failure) requires no layer to be missing from the characterization
record — the falsifier is a configuration with a missing layer accepted. INV-QTB-02 (hashes
registered) requires every characterized layer to have an emitted record hash. INV-QTB-03
(re-execution reproduces the hash) connects the testbed to the audit trail: re-running the
procedure on the same state must reproduce the registered hash. Together they transform
the rhetorical requirement of “validating every layer of the stack” into a machine-verifiable
data state.
7.4 arkhe-qec-decoder
The decoder module abstracts the four candidate decoder types — MWPM, UnionFind,
NeuralNetwork, and LookupTable — behind a single interface with an explicit time budget.
The real-time restriction of Section 2.1.2 is implemented as a contract check:
time_budget_ns < coherence_time_ns, evaluated per decoding operation. A budget
violation — a timeout — is never silently tolerated: it produces a typed Err and is recorded
as an invariant violation. This decision directly counters the backlog risk: a decoder that
silently delays degrades the effective error rate without a visible signal, and detection
would come — too late — from the Testbed's LER measurement. The corrected harness
INV-SW-12 (Section 5.2) lifts the contract to the universal level: within scope,

8.2 Terminology: Falsifiers versus Empirical Rejection Criteria
The audit also sharpened the vocabulary. A falsifier, in the Popperian sense, requires a
universal assertion refutable by an observable counterexample — which only exists at link
E3, where the assertion is formalized in code and the counterexample is executable. For E1,
E2, E4, and E5, the correct term is empirical rejection criterion: a defined test protocol over
a hypothesis, executed by a designated party (the DOE Testbed, or the metrology layer),
whose adverse outcome rejects the claim — without the claim ever having been “proven”.
The invariant catalog of Appendix A respects this distinction column by column: software
invariants carry falsifiers; hardware metrics carry rejection criteria. The distinction is not
pedantic — it is what prevents a measured quantity from being described as proven.
8.3 The Links in Detail
E1 — Intention → Specification. Choosing the specification is an engineering decision and a
scientific judgment. No formal system can prove that the correct specification was written:
the gap between formal specification and user intention is a recognized grand challenge of
dependable software engineering. This is the most invisible and most fundamental link —
every subsequent proof is relative to it. E2 — Specification → Physical model. The formula
pL ≈ A(p/pth)⌈(d+1)/2⌉ is an asymptotic approximation, valid for p   pth and large d, with a
decoder- and noise-model-dependent prefactor (Section 2.1.1); higher-order corrections are
not captured. The link is traversed by empirical validation — comparing predicted against
measured LER — with an explicit rejection criterion, not by proof. E3 — Physical model →
Software. Covered by Kani and proptest. What is proven: the code respects the specification
under the current execution model — with Kani's trust base (Section 2.3.1) honestly
recorded as part of the claim's scope. E4 — Software → Hardware. The software computes
pL = 10⁻⁸ if pph and pth are correct; those parameters come from experimental
characterization — exactly what the DOE Testbed will perform, and the point where the
architecture's record hashes meet the Testbed's measurements. E5 — Hardware → World.
The measurement of the physical phenomenon is outside the reach of any formal
verification; it belongs to metrology, with its own calibrated instruments and uncertainty
budgets.

8.4 The Global Falsifier: Against Theatrical Verification
The decomposition yields a single, acutely actionable check that every derived report must
respect — the program's global falsifier against theatrical verification:
If a PACIR report claims that a hardware property (≥ 100 logical qubits, 10⁵ hard
operations, LER ≤ 10⁻⁸) was proven by Kani, the report is falsified. Kani does not
access hardware. The only admissible formulation is: “the software rejects
configurations that violate this target in the model.”
The three tautological assertions removed in Section 5.2 were, in miniature, exactly this
failure mode: assertions that appear to establish something while establishing nothing. The
global falsifier generalizes the lesson to the report level, and the INV-SW/MET-HW split of
the invariant catalog makes the admissible claim class a structural property of the
documentation rather than a matter of authorial discipline.

9.3 Limitations
Five limitations delimit the results' reach and orient future work.


=== PAGINA 36 ===
Formal Verification and Fault-Tolerant Quantum Computing
Page 36
1. Dimension. Universal proofs cover n ≤ 8 (via loop contracts); for larger dimensions
coverage is sampled. Extending to arbitrary n requires function contracts over
stable_power_sum and is the first item of the future agenda.
2. Precision. Proofs hold over the IEEE-754 domain, not over ℝ; the rounding error
relative to the mathematical ideal is managed by explicit tolerances (10⁻¹⁰ to 10⁻¹²) and is
not formally bounded. Interval analysis is the natural path to closing this gap.
3. Lesche. Lesche stability is not proven — and is known to be false for the Tsallis entropy
under escort probabilities [5]. The module records the negative theorem in its
documentation; substitution by a valid metric based on the homogeneous entropy [5, 7] is
future work.
4. Integration. The QEC → FT → Testbed integration tests are point tests, not formal ones.
Composing invariants across modules (e.g., proving that the LER entering the
fault-tolerance module satisfies (7.2) for every configuration accepted by the QEC
module) is the architecture's next formal frontier.
5. Hardware. No hardware property is proven; all are measured — by the DOE Testbed
under the empirical rejection criteria of Tabela 13's companion, MET-HW. The
architecture's contribution is to make this boundary structural, not rhetorical.
An epistemic status record completes the analysis: the Tsallis module stands at the stage of
operational formal proofs (Kani harnesses running in CI), while the Arkhe modules stand at
the stage of specified contracts with unit coverage and contract harnesses. The
differentiation is voluntary and reproduces the monograph's methodological strategy: prove
the method on the most delicate module, document the remaining gaps, and scale the
formalization with the verification budget.
10 Conclusion
10.1 Contributions
The monograph presents five contributions:
1. A unified architecture — PACIR + Arkhe OS — for fault-tolerant quantum computing
verification, with explicit separation between the physical-mathematical and engineering
domains, a bridge of verifiable contracts, and a blake3 audit trail, materialized as a
six-module Cargo workspace with full CI automation.
2. The Tsallis module with fail-closed contracts, ten verifiable invariants, three
implementation branches with numerically justified boundaries (10⁻⁸ and 10⁻⁴, boundary
tolerance 10⁻¹¹), five Kani harnesses with bit-precise universal proofs up to n = 8 via loop
contracts, an analytically derived optimal Lipschitz constant, and eight proptest
properties with full adversarial f64 coverage.
3. An exhaustive mapping of every Quantum Genesis Q requirement onto verifiable
invariants with concrete falsifiers — and every hardware metric onto empirical rejection
criteria — including the union-bound derivation of the LER ≤ 10⁻⁸ target and the
correspondence with the Testbed's six layers.


=== PAGINA 37 ===
Formal Verification and Fault-Tolerant Quantum Computing
Page 37
4. The Verification Gap — a six-node, five-link decomposition (Intention → Specification →
Physical model → Software → Hardware → World) with feedback loops, per-link evidence
classes, and a global falsifier against theatrical verification; the conceptual tool that
keeps proof, design assertion, and human judgment in their respective places.
5. Epistemic honesty as an engineering artifact: explicit documentation of what is proven
(universally, within scope), sampled, and unproven — including the Lesche negative
theorem with its correct reference (Lutsko, Boon and Grosfils, 2009), the removal of
tautological assertions, declared opaque-API dependencies, and the conversion of every
historical error into a permanent regression.
10.2 Future Work
Six lines of work follow directly from the documented limitations:
1. Loop contracts on every loop of stable_power_sum for arbitrary n, eliminating the
dimension boundary of the universal proofs;
2. Integration verification of QEC → FT → Testbed via Kani, proving the cross-module
composition of invariants rather than each invariant in isolation;
3. Extension to multiple logical qubits (n > 8) via function contracts, approaching the
proof scope of the competition's target fleet size;
4. A convergence proof for the Taylor series via interval analysis, raising that property
from point level to universal level;
5. Substituting Lesche with a valid stability metric — grounded in the homogeneous
entropy [5, 7] — converting a recorded negative theorem into an implementable positive
invariant;
6. Integration with the DOE Testbed — consuming the Testbed's characterization outputs
as calibrated model parameters (link N4 → N2 of the Verification Gap) and emitting the
architecture's record hashes as candidate evidence — together with a two-layer
integration in which Veri-QEC-class circuit verification composes with the Rust-level
proofs (Section 2.4).
10.3 Closing Remarks
The Quantum Genesis Q competition demands more than hardware: it demands verification.
The September 2026 announcement established, for the first time, that the initial paid
milestone of a quantum competition is a verification and validation plan — and the parallel
Lab Call committed $45 million to characterizing every layer of the stack [1]. The PACIR +
Arkhe OS architecture offers a concrete answer to that demand: transform engineering
requirements into verifiable contracts, with formal evidence where possible, sampled
evidence where necessary, and honestly labeled measurement everywhere else.
The method's gain is not “proving Tsallis” — nor proving any physical theorem. The gain is a 
contract that survives: refactoring (the production and proof code remain identical under
cargo build), adversarial inputs (the full f64 space, not merely the cases the author

remembered), precision changes (regime boundaries are tested, not assumed), and — the
decisive test — the scrutiny of an independent verifier with every claim's evidence class
structurally explicit. In an engineering program whose final milestone is a public
demonstration of 10⁸ operations before an independent Testbed, contracts that survive are
the difference between a demonstration and a declaration.

in any environment with a stable Rust toolchain, the Kani verifier installed (cargo install

B.2 API dependencies of the contract harnesses
The INV-SW-09..12 harnesses test contracts over functions whose implementations are not
shown in this document. The proofs are conditional on the following assumed contracts; if
any of these functions changes, the corresponding harnesses must be re-run. If a function
always returned Err or false, the corresponding invariant would be vacuously true — which
is why every harness includes a negative assertion (e.g., d < 3 ⇒ rejection) that fails if the
function is trivial, and why existence assertions are recommended where feasible (Section
5.2).

B.3 Fallback protocol
Fallback when Kani does not prove. If a Kani harness does not terminate (timeout, state
explosion): (1) reduce the scope — prove n = 2 instead of n = 8, documenting the reduction
in the verification report; (2) substitute sampling — run 10⁴ adversarial proptest cases,
recording the case count; (3) record the outcome — mark the invariant as unproved in the
catalog if neither (1) nor (2) is viable; an invariant is never marked as proven without a
proof. The xtask verify-all report emits, for each harness, a status of proved, reduced (with
the reduced parameters), proptest-only (with the case count), or unproved (with the
justification), keeping the evidence ledger honest under CI time constraints.
