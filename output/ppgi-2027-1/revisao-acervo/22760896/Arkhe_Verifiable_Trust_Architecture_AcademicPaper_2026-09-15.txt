Abstract
Autonomous AI agents are beginning to execute consequential actions — payments, deployments,
signatures — on the basis of representations their principals cannot independently verify. This
monograph develops and examines a design answer: Arkhe, a verifiable trust layer whose load-bearing
elements are a fail-closed kernel of containment, cryptographic model attestation (canonicalized on the
GGUF open-weight container), an append-only causal evidence graph (WormGraph) anchored to
external transparency logs, and quorum-governed rule corpora (constitution-as-code) whose authority
is structurally separated from event witnesses. The study proceeds by design-science analysis with
systematic documentary verification. Incident reconstructions of the Bybit (2025, ~$1.5B), StablR
(2026, $13.5M), and SUPERFORTUNE (2026, ~$15.18M) failures are mapped to architectural
controls; the architecture is aligned with the EU AI Act, GDPR, Brazil's LGPD, MiCA, and ISO/IEC
42001; and a minimal viable product — GGUF model verification for machine-learning engineers — is
specified with falsifiable adoption metrics. Extending the architecture to its limiting case, the book
specifies a five-front evidence program for the Hodge conjecture, a Clay Millennium Prize problem,
integrating verified 2025-2026 results (including Markman's proof for abelian fourfolds) with Lean 4
formalization and Rekor anchoring, and recording three displayed corrections to the program's internal
claims. Counterarguments, limitations, and open problems are treated as load-bearing sections. The
conclusion assesses the project as architecturally coherent but pre-product, and prescribes a
metrics-gated path from verification artifact to market.
Keywords: verifiable trust; AI agent governance; model attestation; transparency logs;
constitution-as-code; Hodge conjecture

1. Opening Hook: The Trust Crisis in Autonomous AI Agents
 On 21 February 2025, the cryptocurrency exchange Bybit lost roughly 1.5 billion US dollars in
the largest theft in the history of digital assets [2]. The attackers, attributed by blockchain-intelligence
firms to North Korean state-sponsored groups [2, 3], did not break cryptography. They did something
subtler and more instructive: they compromised the infrastructure that renders transactions for human
approval, so that signers of a multisignature wallet approved a malicious transfer while their screens
displayed a benign one [4, 5, 7]. Every cryptographic guarantee held; every human guarantee failed.
The episode was not an exotic anomaly but a clean demonstration of a structural gap that now extends
far beyond cryptocurrency: whenever a decision-maker — human or machine — acts on a
representation of reality that it cannot independently verify, the weakest link is the representation
itself.
 The years that follow the Bybit incident sharpen the point rather than blunt it. In May 2026, the
European stablecoin issuer StablR was drained of 13.5 million dollars after an attacker gained control
of a minting contract governed by a 1-of-3 multisignature configuration, minting unbacked tokens
against a threshold that a single compromised key could satisfy [6, 33]. In the same month, the
AI-related project SUPERFORTUNE lost approximately 15.18 million dollars when funds intended
for an airdrop were redirected, a heist that security researchers traced to look-alike address
substitution — the visual mimicry of a destination address — rather than to any failure of the
underlying chain [34, 41]. Industry analyses place aggregate losses from on-chain attacks above two
billion dollars per year, with the majority characterized as preventable [1]. Each of these incidents is a
data point about the same failure mode: authority exercised on the basis of unverifiable appearances.
 Autonomous AI agents push this failure mode from the margin to the center. An agent that
negotiates, purchases, signs, and deploys acts through representations — model weights, prompts,
tool outputs, transaction payloads — that its principal almost never inspects. The security community
has begun to codify the resulting risks: the OWASP Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications
places prompt injection and supply-chain vulnerabilities at the top of its 2025 ranking [13, 14], and
regulatory instruments such as the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act now require, for
high-risk systems, automatic event logging across the system lifetime [17, 18]. Yet logging is not
verification, and oversight by a human who must read a log is oversight of an unverifiable
representation. The missing piece is architectural: a layer whose entire purpose is to make claims —
about models, transactions, policies, and even mathematical proofs — independently checkable, and
to convert trust from a promise into a proof.
Thesis: the emerging discipline of verifiable trust treats evidence, not assurance, as the
unit of governance. This book develops that thesis into a concrete architecture — Arkhe
OS — and tests it against blockchain custody incidents, regulatory regimes, and the
hardest verification problem in mathematics.

verifiable trust layer for autonomous AI agents developed within the Arkhe project between 2025 and

maps the architecture onto the regulatory landscape — the EU AI Act, the General Data Protection

artifacts, strategic assessments — are identified as internal documentation [28, 29, 30, 31, 45, 69, 70]

2. Background and Definitions
2.1 Terminology
 Because the field it describes is young, this book fixes its vocabulary early and holds it constant.
An attestation is a digitally signed statement that binds an artifact — a model file, a binary, a
configuration — to measured properties such as a content hash, an origin, or a policy version. A
transparency log is an append-only, hash-chained public record in which any entry, once included, is
provably present and cannot be silently removed or reordered [8, 9]. A witness is a party that
countersigns an event to extend the set of parties that can detect tampering; witnesses observe but do
not authorize. An invariant is a machine-checkable property that must hold over a class of states or
transitions; a constitution-as-code is a versioned corpus of such invariants plus the amendment
procedure that changes them. A quorum is the minimum number of independent authorizations
required for a privileged action. Finally, verifiable trust denotes the design stance that any claim relied
upon by the system — about identity, code, data, or governance — must be accompanied by evidence
a third party can re-check without trusting the claimant.
 Two distinctions do most of the analytical work. The first separates authority from witnessing:
the authority that defines what is permitted must be structurally distinct from the mechanisms that
record what happened, or the recorder becomes the definer. The second separates verification from
attestation: attestation is a claim about an artifact made by a signer, whereas verification is the
independent re-derivation of the property itself. The Bybit incident is precisely a collapse of this
second distinction — signers were shown an attestation-shaped interface that did not correspond to
the transaction semantics — and it motivates the architectural insistence on independent decoding
developed in Chapter 8 [5, 7, 38].
2.2 Historical context: from certificate transparency to agent governance
 The architectural lineage of Arkhe begins with Certificate Transparency. Deployed from 2013
and standardized in RFC 6962, CT placed every public TLS certificate into publicly auditable,
append-only Merkle-tree logs, converting the certificate-authority ecosystem from a web of promises
into one in which mis-issuance is detectable by design [8]. The mechanism that made CT work was


=== PAGINA 8 ===
ARKHE: a verifiable trust architecture for autonomous AI agents
Page 8
not cryptography alone but the coupling of cryptography to publicity: a certification authority could
still mis-issue, but could no longer mis-issue quietly. Sigstore's Rekor service transposed this pattern
to software supply chains, providing a public transparency log for signatures and software artifacts,
with inclusion proofs and consistency proofs that allow any auditor to detect log rewriting [9]; the
Open Source Security Foundation has since documented operational uses of Rekor in catching
malicious package releases [10].
 A second lineage is key transparency in end-to-end encrypted messaging. WhatsApp deployed
key transparency in 2023 so that users' clients could automatically verify that the public keys they
receive for a correspondent are the keys that correspondent registered, closing the gap between
encrypted transport and authentic identity [11, 12]. A third lineage is institutional multisignature
custody on public blockchains, formalized in standards such as EIP-712 typed structured data and the
Safe smart-account framework [24, 25]. Multisignature wallets distribute authority across keys, but —
as the incidents reviewed in Chapter 7 make plain — they verify signatures, not meanings: a properly
signed transaction can still steal everything if the signers did not understand what they signed [2, 6].
 The governance of AI systems has so far developed along a parallel but largely
non-cryptographic track. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework organizes organizational
practices around the functions Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage [15]; ISO/IEC 42001:2023
defines management-system requirements for artificial intelligence, including documented controls
for the AI lifecycle [16]; and the EU AI Act imposes on high-risk systems duties of risk management,
data governance, automatic logging, human oversight, and robustness [17, 18, 19]. These instruments
specify what must be true of an organization; they largely leave open how the underlying artifacts are
to be made tamper-evident. The Arkhe hypothesis is that the transparency-log lineage supplies exactly
the missing mechanisms, and that the two tracks — institutional governance and cryptographic
evidence — must be joined in a single architecture rather than coordinated across separate ones.
2.3 Related work and the identified gap
 Adjacent technical literature clusters into five groups. Sandboxed execution — from
operating-system containers to WebAssembly — supplies containment primitives that Arkhe's kernel
layer reuses conceptually, although containment alone says nothing about evidence. Policy engines
and formal access-control models supply the declarative style that constitution-as-code adopts, but are
typically governed by the same operator they police. Blockchain-native security tooling — transaction
simulators, decoding layers, and clear-signing initiatives responding to the blind-signing epidemic [1,
38] — addresses the human-approval gap but not the provenance of models or the governance of
rules. Formal verification infrastructure, most prominently the Lean 4 proof assistant and its
mathematical library mathlib [62, 63], supplies the strongest known form of artifact verification for
mathematical claims and is the target environment of the program in Chapter 13. Finally, AI-agent
frameworks and their emerging security guidance [13, 14] document the threat surface — prompt
injection, tool abuse, supply-chain compromise — but stop short of architectural mechanisms for
evidence.

3. The Arkhe Architecture: Design Principles and Load-Bearing
Elements
3.1 Six design principles
 The architecture is derived from six principles that appear throughout the project corpus [28, 31].
P1, prove rather than promise: whenever a security-relevant claim can be replaced by a checkable
artifact, the artifact wins. P2, evidence is immutable and append-only: evidence once written is never
edited or deleted; corrections are new entries that reference the error, preserving the causal chain. P3,


=== PAGINA 11 ===
ARKHE: a verifiable trust architecture for autonomous AI agents
Page 11
governance is explicit: every rule that binds the system is stated as a falsifiable invariant with a named
amendment procedure, never as an undocumented operator practice. P4, containment precedes
capability: an agent's permissions derive from a bounded kernel context, not from the agent's
self-description. P5, separation of powers: the authority that defines rules (ConstitutionStore) is
distinct from the witness that records events (the Safe multisig), and either can function as a check on
the other. P6, third-party verifiability: verification code must be executable by parties who hold no
trust relationship with the operator, which motivates the WebAssembly target discussed in Chapter 4.
 The principles are ordered by what they exclude. P1 excludes architectures whose security
argument ends in a policy document; P2 excludes mutable audit trails and retroactive edits; P3
excludes implicit, operator-defined rules; P4 excludes capability grants negotiated between an agent
and its own runtime; P5 excludes systems in which the party that records history also defines what
history should have been; P6 excludes verifiers that only run inside the vendor's perimeter. Each
principle traces to a documented failure mode: P1 and P2 to the mutability of conventional logs, P3 to
silent policy drift, P4 to agent self-authorization, P5 to the recorder-definer collapse observed in the
StablR incident, and P6 to the interface-trust collapse observed at Bybit [2, 6]. The mapping from
incidents to principles is developed quantitatively in Chapter 7.

3.2 The kernel of containment
 The kernel of containment is the layer at which an agent's statements about itself are replaced by
enforceable bounds. In the implemented design, arkhe-core defines the agent's resource envelope —
callable tools, data domains, network reach, spending authority — and enforces a fail-closed policy:
an action not explicitly permitted by the active constitution is denied, and the denial itself becomes
evidence [28]. The design borrows its philosophy from decades of systems work on sandboxing and


=== PAGINA 12 ===
ARKHE: a verifiable trust architecture for autonomous AI agents
Page 12
least privilege, but applies it at the semantic boundary of agentic behavior: what is contained is not
only memory and CPU but authority — which counterparties may be contacted, which contracts may
be called, which signatures may be produced on the agent's behalf.
 Containment interacts with the other layers through two mechanisms. Capability gates expose the
kernel's decisions as structured events that the evidence layer can record, so that a denied action is as
visible in the WormGraph as an executed one; and policy versioning binds every gate decision to the
constitution version under which it was taken, so that an auditor can reconstruct not only what the
agent did but under which rules. This last point matters for the regulatory analysis of Chapter 9:
record-keeping obligations under Article 12 of the EU AI Act are easier to satisfy when logs carry
their own legal context [17, 18]. The kernel is deliberately small — its value lies in being too simple
to be controversial — and the project's strategic analysis identifies it as the component most in need of
formal specification before any production claim [31].
3.3 Model attestation
 Model attestation binds a model artifact to an identity that downstream policy can reason about.
The design takes GGUF — the GPT-Generated Unified Format used by llama.cpp and the dominant
container for open-weight language models [26, 27] — as its canonical target because a GGUF file is
a single, self-describing binary whose SHA-256 hash is a stable identity. Attestation then proceeds in
three steps: the hash is computed and recorded; the publisher's signature, license, declared
training-data status, and any safety evaluations are collected into an evidence record; and the evidence
record is anchored in a transparency log [9]. A deployment that later loads the model can re-derive the
hash and compare it against the anchored record, converting 'we believe we are running model X' into
'the bytes we loaded are provably the bytes that were attested as model X'.
 Two limitations are acknowledged from the outset. Attestation establishes identity, not quality: a
perfectly attested model may still be malign, and the evidence record therefore includes declared
evaluations rather than certifications of behavior. And attestation is only as strong as the binding
between the hash and the executing process, which is why the kernel layer — not the attestation layer
— is the ultimate enforcement point. The MVP specified in Chapter 10 keeps this scope honest: it
verifies that the file a user is about to run is the file that was attested, nothing more, and it measures
whether that narrow promise is one users value [31].
3.4 The WormGraph: an immutable causal evidence graph
 The WormGraph is the architecture's memory: an append-only directed graph in which nodes
represent events, decisions, artifacts, and evidence records, and edges represent explicit causal or
derivational dependencies [28, 70]. Each node carries the SHA-256 hash of its content, an Ed25519
signature over that hash plus metadata, references to its parent nodes, and — where the node has been
anchored — the index of its entry in a Rekor transparency log [9]. The graph is a worm in the sense of
write-once storage: history accumulates and branches but is never rewritten. Errors are handled the
way public ledgers handle errors, by new entries that supersede rather than by edits that erase.


=== PAGINA 13 ===
ARKHE: a verifiable trust architecture for autonomous AI agents
Page 13
 The causal structure is what makes the WormGraph more than a hash chain. Because edges are
first-class, an auditor can answer questions that flat logs cannot: which attestation records contributed
to this deployment decision; which constitution version was in force along the path that produced this
signature; which downstream artifacts must be re-examined if this upstream model hash is revoked.
This is the property the project calls semantic evidence, and it is the property that the regulatory
chapter relies on when it argues that the architecture produces audit trails with legal texture rather than
undifferentiated event streams [18, 28].
3.5 Invariant governance and the ConstitutionStore
 Governance closes the loop. Invariants are declared in a constitution-as-code corpus held in the
ConstitutionStore; each invariant is a machine-checkable property over evidence records, and each
amendment to the corpus is itself an evidence event requiring quorum under the project's decision
record ADR-011 [29]. The separation of powers is enforced structurally: a Safe multisig may witness
that an event occurred, but only the ConstitutionStore, amended by quorum, may define what events
mean. The rationale is operational as well as philosophical — the StablR post-mortem analysis in
Chapter 7 argues that low-threshold multisigs fail precisely when witnessing and authority collapse
into one small key set [6, 33].
 Figure 1 summarizes the stack. Reading from the top, payloads are verified independently (layer
2), constrained by the kernel (layer 3), turned into signed evidence (layer 4), registered immutably
(layer 5), and judged against quorum-governed invariants (layer 6), with the right-hand column
holding the surfaces that make the whole design falsifiable from outside: the Rekor log, independent
witnesses, third-party WASM verifiers, and the ConstitutionStore itself [28]. The next chapter makes
the cryptographic substrate explicit.
3.6 Explicit non-goals
 A design is also defined by what it declines to include, and three exclusions are load-bearing.
First, the architecture does not require trusted hardware: attested execution environments — enclaves
and confidential virtual machines — are a complementary containment technology with a different
trust basis, and the design treats them as pluggable strengthening rather than as a dependency, because
their supply-chain and side-channel posture must be argued per platform. Second, the evidence
registry is not a blockchain: anchoring uses established transparency-log infrastructure [9] rather than
consensus settlement, trading trustless verification for auditability with far lower cost and latency; a
settle-to-chain bridge remains a possible extension for deployments whose auditors demand it. Third,
the architecture does not yet employ zero-knowledge proofs: a future direction is to attest that a
payload satisfies a policy without disclosing the payload itself, which would matter for commercially
sensitive flows, but the project's sequencing places general-purpose evidence integrity first — a
deliberate refusal to let advanced cryptography outpace the governance layer that makes its outputs
meaningful [28].

4.1 Content addressing and signatures
 Two cryptographic primitives carry most of the architecture. SHA-256 provides content
addressing: because the hash of an artifact is computed from its bytes, identity and integrity collapse
into one operation, and any party can re-derive it offline. Ed25519 — the Edwards-curve signature
scheme standardized in RFC 8032 — provides attribution: deterministic signatures, fast verification,
and a public key that serves as a stable actor identifier inside the WormGraph. The choice of Ed25519
over ECDSA variants is pragmatic rather than ideological: deterministic signing removes a class of
nonce-reuse failures, and the scheme's verification cost suits the third-party verification target of
principle P6 [28]. All signature material in the evidence layer binds, at minimum, the content hash, the
author key, a timestamp, and the constitution version in force.
4.2 Append-only logs and anchoring
 Immutability inside the operator's infrastructure is a strong claim that requires evidence outside
it. The architecture therefore anchors WormGraph records to Sigstore's Rekor, an
implementation-independent transparency log in which entries live in an append-only Merkle tree and
are provable by inclusion proofs and consistency proofs [9]. An inclusion proof demonstrates that a
given entry is part of the signed tree head; a consistency proof demonstrates that new tree heads
extend previous ones, so that a log operator cannot quietly rewrite history without detection by any
auditor that has retained a prior tree head [8, 9]. The pattern is inherited from Certificate Transparency
[8], and its operational maturity in the software-supply-chain setting — including the detection of
malicious package releases [10] — is the reason the architecture treats it as substrate rather than as
novelty.
 Anchoring gives the WormGraph a property that internal append-only storage cannot achieve on
its own: detectability of wholesale replacement. An operator who controls the servers can silently
truncate a private database, but cannot produce a valid consistency proof from a rewritten history to
tree heads already witnessed by third parties. The design implication is that the anchoring interval —
how often evidence is committed to Rekor — is a security parameter, trading detection latency against
operational cost, and the project corpus specifies continuous anchoring for governance-critical events
and batched anchoring for high-volume evidence [28, 70].
4.3 WebAssembly as the portable verification target
 Principle P6 requires that verification be executable by parties without a trust relationship to the
operator. The architecture's answer is arkhe-verify-wasm: the decoding and evidence-checking logic
is compiled to WebAssembly and executed in a sandbox — a browser tab, a worker, an embedder —
with no network access during verification [28]. WebAssembly is chosen because it is a mature,
standardized compilation target with near-native performance and a strong isolation story, and
because it makes distribution trivial: the verifier can be served as a static artifact whose own hash is
itself Rekor-anchored, closing the loop on verifier integrity. A third party who doubts the verifier can
check the verifier — the recursion is finite, ending at a small trusted computing base of a browser or a
CLI built from source.

4.4 Threat model
 The architecture assumes adversaries of four classes. The compromised signer controls a key
inside an authorization threshold — the StablR class [6, 33]. The interface adversary controls what an
approver sees rather than what is signed — the Bybit class [2, 5, 7]. The supply-chain adversary
controls an artifact before deployment — a poisoned model, a malicious dependency — the class
documented by OWASP for LLM applications [14] and by the OpenSSF for packages [10]. The
governance insider controls rule definition — the party who can change what the system means. Each
class is answered by a different layer: thresholds and quorum by the governance layer; interface trust
by independent decoding (Chapter 8); supply chain by attestation and anchoring (Chapters 3 and 10);
and governance insiders by the separation of powers and versioned constitutions (Chapter 6). What
the model does not assume is a trusted operator; the entire design exists because that assumption fails
[31].

6. Governance: Constitution-as-Code, Quorum, and VetoGraph
6.1 Authority and witness: a separation of powers
 The governance design begins from the observation that most multisignature systems conflate
two roles. Signers witness and authorize events, but the rules — who signs what, with what threshold,
subject to which exceptions — live in configuration controlled by whoever deploys the wallet.
Arkhe's ConstitutionStore inverts this: rules are data, versioned and hashed, and amendments are
events that require the same evidentiary discipline as any other action [28, 29]. A Safe multisig
remains useful, but demoted to witness: it records that something happened, while the
ConstitutionStore records what it meant. The project's executive analysis names this separation the
project's most defensible architectural claim, precisely because it is the property that conventional
custody stacks lack [31].
6.2 ADR-011: threshold choice under incident data
 Decision record ADR-011 fixes the quorum policy for privileged actions: thresholds of 4-of-7 or
5-of-7, with configurations below two-thirds of signers required to approve prohibited [29]. The
policy is argued from incident data rather than taste. At 1-of-3 — the StablR configuration — a single
compromised key is a total loss, as the minting incident demonstrated [6, 33]. At 3-of-7, three
colluding or compromised keys suffice; the Bybit case shows that even high thresholds fail when the
approval interface is subverted, which is why thresholds are treated as necessary and not sufficient [2,
5]. The upper bound comes from liveness: a 6-of-7 wallet freezes if two keys are lost, and availability
is a security property too. The 4/7-to-5/7 band is the project's balance point; the analysis is not
presented as universally optimal but as a defensible default with explicit reasoning that deployers can
revisit [29].
A threshold is a hypothesis about how many keys can be compromised simultaneously.
Incident data is the only honest source for that hypothesis, and ADR-011 cites incidents
the way a clinical guideline cites trials.
6.3 VetoGraph and the constitutional audit lineage
 Two internal artifacts connect Arkhe's governance to a broader constitutional-methodology
program. The first is SASC v5.4α, the project's constitutional audit methodology, which was applied
to the 1988 Brazilian Federal Constitution: the corpus of more than 250 articles was parsed into a
topological graph of 276 nodes and 261 edges — the VetoGraph — encoding checks, limits, and veto
relations among institutions [30, 32]. The second is the PEC triage protocol, which scores
constitutional-amendment proposals against that graph. These artifacts are methodological
demonstrations rather than deployed systems, and the book presents them with that caveat [30]. Their
relevance here is architectural: they show that the constitution-as-code pattern scales from
seven-signer wallets to seven-hundred-article documents, and they supply the metaphor the project
uses deliberately — machine governance as institutional checks and balances, with vetoes that are
explicit, versioned, and quorum-amended.

7. Incident-Driven Validation: Bybit, StablR, SUPERFORTUNE
7.1 Method: counterfactual control analysis
 This chapter asks a disciplined counterfactual question of three documented incidents: had the
specific controls proposed by the Arkhe architecture been present, at which step would the attack
chain have been interrupted, and what residual risk would remain? The method has two honest
limitations that recur throughout the book. Counterfactuals cannot prove that a control would have
fired — they establish plausibility, not causation — and the control set is the project's own, so the
analysis inherits its assumptions [28, 31]. The value of the exercise is therefore not certification but
specification: each incident is treated as a test the architecture must be able to explain, and failure to
explain one is treated as a design defect.
7.2 Bybit: interface trust as the root cause
 The forensic reconstruction is consistent across investigators: on 21 February 2025, attackers
attributed to North Korean actors compromised the infrastructure of Safe{Wallet} — the interface
through which Bybit's multisignature transactions were assembled and displayed — and used that
position to present signers with a transaction whose displayed content did not match its executed
semantics; the signers approved, and roughly 1.5 billion dollars moved to attacker addresses [2, 4, 5].
Sygnia's investigation traces the intrusion to the developer environment and infrastructure of the
wallet vendor [4]; TRM Labs and CSIS place the operation within the Lazarus-linked TraderTraitor
campaign [2, 5]; and a CSIS policy analysis emphasizes the supply-chain lesson for institutional
custody [3]. The signatures were valid. The humans were careful. The representation was the attack.
 Under the Arkhe control set, the decisive control is independent decoding: a verifier that
re-derives the transaction's meaning from raw calldata, outside the interface that displays it, and
refuses agreement between display and decode as a precondition of signature [28, 39]. The attack's
manipulation lived in the layer between display and semantics; the verifier's entire purpose is to delete
that layer. Secondary controls deepen the interruption: anchoring of the wallet's own verification
artifact in Rekor would have made silent replacement of the verifier detectable [9]; and the witness
separation of Chapter 6 would have required governance-level acknowledgement for a transaction of
anomalous size and destination profile. Residual risk remains real — a user who ignores the verifier,
or a compromised verifier that users do not themselves verify — and the honest conclusion is that
Bybit-class attacks are made expensive and loud, not impossible [7].

7.3 StablR: threshold failure at the authority
 In May 2026, the European stablecoin issuer StablR froze its USDR and EURR tokens after an
attacker controlling a single key in a 1-of-3 multisignature configuration over the minting contract
minted 13.5 million dollars of unbacked stablecoins [6, 33]. Blockaid's attribution of the root cause to
the threshold configuration [33] makes the case uniquely instructive: here the failure was not in the
approval interface but in the governance arithmetic itself. ADR-011's prohibition of sub-two-thirds
thresholds is written directly against this failure mode, and the case supplies the decision record's
sharpest sentence: a threshold is a hypothesis about simultaneous key compromise, and 1-of-3
hypothesizes that no key is ever compromised [29].
 Under the Arkhe control set, the primary control is quorum policy — 4-of-7 or 5-of-7 [29] — and
the secondary control is the separation of authority from witness: mint events would have been
constitution-governed actions whose evidence records include the quorum proof, not
configuration-controlled operations. Anchoring adds detection of a different kind: unbacked minting
against a constitution that caps issuance would have produced an invariant violation visible to any
auditor of the anchored graph. The counterfactual here is the strongest of the three cases, because the
control that fails — the threshold — is one the architecture treats as a first-class, versioned object
rather than a deployment default.
7.4 SUPERFORTUNE: look-alike addresses and automated flows
 The SUPERFORTUNE incident of May 2026, in which approximately 15.18 million dollars was
redirected from an intended airdrop flow, was reconstructed by Halborn's research team as an
exploitation of transfer preparation — the attacker substituted a visually similar destination address so
that a hurried approval authorized the wrong destination [34]. Market data show the GUA token
collapsing by more than 97 percent from its pre-incident peak, underscoring how quickly trust exits a
system that cannot distinguish its own counterparties [41]. The incident belongs to the
address-poisoning family analyzed systematically by Chainalysis, which documented campaigns
involving tens of thousands of vanity addresses and attempted thefts approaching 68 million dollars in
a single case [35], with continued evolution documented by Blockaid [36] and an academic survey
formalizing the attack class [37].
 Under the Arkhe control set, the interruption point is again independent decoding — the verifier
computes the destination's semantic identity, not its visual one — joined to counterparty allow-lists
enforced by the kernel: an agent operating under a constitution that pins destination addresses by hash
cannot be diverted by a doppelgänger [28]. The residual risk shifts to the allow-list governance itself,
which is precisely where the architecture wants the fight to happen: amending an allow-list is a
quorum event with evidence, not a clipboard copy [29].

7.5 Synthesis
 Three patterns emerge from the mapping. First, all three incidents are attacks on representations
rather than on cryptography, supporting the book's thesis that the representation layer is the modern
perimeter [2, 6, 34]. Second, the decisive controls cluster in two layers — independent verification
and governance arithmetic — which is why the architecture puts quorum and decoding at its center
rather than at its edge. Third, residual risks are themselves representational: a verifier can be ignored
or replaced, an allow-list can be captured. The architecture's response, developed in Chapter 8, is to
make the verifier's own identity part of the evidence graph, so that the question 'which verifier said
this?' is always answerable [28, 70].
7.6 The aggregate loss landscape, 2024-2026
 The three case studies sit inside an aggregate picture that industry reporting makes
uncomfortably concrete. Cyfrin's 2025 blockchain security report estimates average losses above 250
million dollars per month since 2020 — more than 17.5 billion dollars in cumulative value — and
projects tens of billions annually as adoption accelerates, with the large majority of incidents
attributed to preventable flaws rather than to cryptographic breakage [1]. The address-poisoning
family alone has been documented by Chainalysis at campaign scale — more than 82,000
attacker-controlled addresses, with single-victim attempted thefts approaching 68 million dollars [35]
— and continues to evolve as a social-engineering primitive [36, 37]. The SUPERFORTUNE episode
adds a distinctive 2026 marker: the platform marketed itself as an AI-powered prediction engine [34],
and its collapse — a drawdown exceeding 97 percent from the pre-incident peak [41] — illustrates
how quickly trust exits systems whose counterparties cannot be verified. For the architecture, the
aggregate numbers function the way base rates do in medicine: they establish that the failure modes of
Chapter 7 are not rare tail events but the ordinary weather of the industry, against which any proposed
control must be judged by cost and coverage rather than by novelty.
8. Multisig Inspector and Independent Transaction Decoding
8.1 The blind-signing problem, precisely stated


=== PAGINA 21 ===
ARKHE: a verifiable trust architecture for autonomous AI agents
Page 21
 A Safe transaction is a typed structure hashed under EIP-712 and signed off-chain by a threshold
of owners before execution through the Safe smart account [24, 25]. The typed hash covers fields —
to, value, data hash, operation (call or delegatecall), gas, nonces — whose meaning depends on
context the wallet interface may not know. When the inner data is itself a call to another contract, the
semantics live one derivation deeper than the hash. Practitioner analyses of the post-Bybit landscape
describe the delegatecall path as the canonical blind spot: the outer transaction is readable, the inner
call's meaning is not, and the five fields that matter are precisely the ones a compressed interface does
not surface [38]. Clear-signing initiatives answer part of the problem at the wallet layer [1]; the Arkhe
answer is a verifier that is independent of both wallet and vendor.
8.2 The Multisig Inspector
 The Multisig Inspector is the application of arkhe-verify-wasm to Safe transactions [28]. Given
raw transaction material, it reconstructs the EIP-712 structure, re-derives the Safe transaction hash so
signers can compare it against what their devices report, decodes the inner call — including
delegatecall targets — against known ABIs, displays the effective threshold and current owner set of
the target Safe, and emits a signed verdict into the evidence layer, so that the inspection itself becomes
an event in the WormGraph [70]. The implementation constraints follow principle P6: no network
access during decoding; deterministic output for identical inputs; and a distribution channel — the
static WASM artifact — whose own hash is Rekor-anchored so that third parties can verify the
verifier [9, 28].
 The verdict-into-evidence design has a governance consequence worth stating. Because
inspections are events, an organization accumulates a queryable history of what was shown to whom
before what was signed, which converts the Bybit post-mortem question — what did the signers see?
— from a forensic reconstruction into a lookup [4, 31]. It also creates a accountability surface for the
inspector itself: a wrong decode is discoverable, attributable, and correctable by supersession, in
keeping with the append-only doctrine of Chapter 3.
8.3 Comparison and boundary
 Positioned against existing tooling, the inspector occupies a narrow but load-bearing niche.
Wallet-level simulators and clear-signing displays improve what the user sees, but remain inside the
trust perimeter of the party that serves them [1]; forensic and transaction-intelligence services analyze
flows after the fact [2]; independent decoding libraries give engineers the primitives but not the
evidence trail. The inspector's contribution is the coupling of decoding to evidence: the decode is not
advice but an artifact, hash-addressed and anchored. The boundary is equally clear — the inspector
cannot make a user read the verdict, cannot verify intent, and cannot recover funds. It moves the
failure of Chapter 7 from 'the representation was subverted silently' to 'the subversion was detectable
and the detection was recorded', which is the honest limit of any client-side control [7].

 Regulation increasingly demands what transparency logs natively produce: records that are

most high-risk classifications, the logging and oversight duties relevant here, and the conformity

research topic: deployers confronting conformity assessment will need machine-checkable logs [18],
and the architecture is positioned to be the substrate those logs run on [28, 31].
9.2 Data protection: GDPR and LGPD
 Immutability and data protection are in structural tension, and the architecture's answer is
honesty about scope rather than pretending the tension away. Under the GDPR, personal data benefit
from erasure rights under Article 17 and the regulation's broader rights apparatus [22]; under Brazil's
Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados, holders of personal data hold rights consolidated in Articles 17
through 22 of the statute [23]. The WormGraph therefore applies pseudonymization at ingestion:
evidence records reference subjects by hashes and keys, not by identifiers, and the mapping — where
one must exist — lives outside the append-only store, where it can be deleted without corrupting the
graph. Cryptographic deletion — destroying the keys that make anchored ciphertext readable —
remains the pragmatic strategy for cases where personal data must enter the evidence layer at all, and
the design treats data-protection impact assessment as a governance invariant rather than a document
[28]. The position is conservative: the architecture is custodian of evidence about actions, and it is
designed so that the minimum necessary personal data enters that evidence at all.
 The statutory detail is worth stating precisely, because it drives design decisions that would
otherwise look arbitrary. The GDPR's processing principles — lawfulness, fairness, purpose
limitation, and, centrally for this design, data minimization — push evidence systems toward
recording hashes and signatures rather than payloads [22]; the regulation's accountability machinery,
including records of processing activities, is naturally served by the WormGraph's export bundles.
The LGPD consolidates holder rights in a dedicated chapter — confirmation of processing, access,

10. The Minimal Viable Product: GGUF Model Verification
10.1 Why the first artifact is a model file
 The project's executive analysis converged on a narrow first product: verify one GGUF model,
end to end, for one persona — the machine-learning engineer [31]. The choice is strategic rather than
opportunistic. GGUF is the dominant distribution container for open-weight models [26, 27], so the
artifact is one practitioners already touch daily; the hash of a single file is a complete identity, so the
attestation story needs no infrastructure beyond what Chapter 4 already provides; and the trust gap is
visceral — an engineer who has downloaded a quantized model from an unknown mirror has
performed exactly the unverifiable-representation act this book is about. The product converts that act
into a checkable one: hash the file, compare against the anchored attestation, decide.
 The flow is deliberately boring. The user runs the arkhe-verify CLI — or pastes the file into the
static browser verifier — and receives one of three verdicts: verified, meaning the hash matches an
anchored attestation record with no revocations along its dependency chain; unverified, meaning no
record exists; or revoked, meaning a superseding record invalidates the attestation [28]. Each verdict
links to its evidence: the publisher's signature, the license and declared training-data status, and the
Rekor inclusion proof [9]. Nothing in the flow asks the user to trust the project's servers; the WASM
artifact is static and its hash is itself anchored, per principle P6.

10.3 Distribution and the wedge
 Distribution follows the persona's existing motion: a CLI installable from the Rust ecosystem, a

10.4 Failure modes of the MVP itself
 Intellectual honesty requires the MVP to be subjected to its own analysis. The first failure mode
is the cold-start problem: until publishers sign and anchor their artifacts, every verification returns
“unverified,” which is correct but unhelpful, and the product's real go-to-market bottleneck is
publisher onboarding rather than verifier distribution. The design mitigates this by making the
negative verdict informative — an “unverified” result lists exactly what a publisher must provide —
and by targeting the continuous-integration wedge, where a GitHub Action that fails builds on
unverified models gives organizations a reason to demand attestations from their suppliers [31]. The
second failure mode is false assurance: a user may read “verified” as “safe,” when the architecture
attests identity and provenance, not behavior; the verdict wording and the evidence detail view are
therefore part of the safety design, not an afterthought [26, 27]. The third is verifier obsolescence:
format drift in GGUF itself could break decoding, which the schema-versioning discipline of Chapter
5 absorbs by making the verifier version part of the attested evidence [70]. Each failure mode carries a
detection signal — onboarding conversion, verdict-read-through rates, and decode-error telemetry
respectively — so that the MVP can falsify itself on the same terms it imposes on everything else.
11. The Arkhe-Catedral Ecosystem: Constitutional Audit as Method
11.1 The cathedral metaphor and the audit engine
 Before Arkhe OS existed as a product direction, the project existed as a methodology: a way of
reading large normative corpora — constitutions, ontologies, standards — as topological structures
whose health can be measured. The project's internal name for this program is Arkhe-Catedral, and its
most developed instrument is SASC v5.4α, a constitutional-audit methodology whose 2026
application to the Brazilian Federal Constitution of 1988 produced the results summarized here [30].
The metaphor is chosen with care: a cathedral is a structure built over generations under explicit rules
about how it may be changed, and the analogy carries to governance-as-code, where amendments are
load-bearing events rather than silent rewrites. Readers should hold the epistemic status clearly in

Page 26
view: SASC and its outputs are internal project artifacts [30], not peer-reviewed results; the corpus
they audit, however, is external and public — the official text of the Constitution [32].
11.2 The CF/88 audit: 276 nodes, 261 edges
 The audit proceeded in three steps. First, the full text of the Constitution — more than 250
articles across title, fundamental-rights, and organizational provisions — was parsed from the official
source [32] into a machine-readable graph. Second, normative relations were encoded as edges:
checks, limits, competence boundaries, and veto relations among institutions, with
constitutional-amendment articles treated as the graph's own self-modification rules. Third, the
resulting VetoGraph — 276 nodes and 261 edges — was analyzed for tension points, unbalanced veto
concentration, and amendment-pressure resilience, using a scoring protocol (the project's HOPM
heuristic) whose parameters are published in the internal audit report [30]. The audit's declared output
is not a verdict on the Constitution but a topological map: which nodes concentrate veto power, which
edges carry amendment traffic, and where the graph's redundancy is thin.
 Two properties make this exercise relevant to the architecture rather than merely to constitutional
scholarship. The first is scale transfer: the constitution-as-code pattern of Chapter 6 was exercised on
a document two orders of magnitude larger than any wallet policy, which is evidence that the pattern's
notation and amendment discipline scale — with the caveat that no production system yet runs on
either scale. The second is methodological symmetry: the audit's rule that every flagged tension must
cite the articles in conflict is the same rule that Chapter 13 imposes on mathematical claims, and the
same rule that ADR-011 imposes on threshold changes [29, 30]. Across domains, the project's
constant is citation discipline — claims that keep explicit company.
11.3 What the ecosystem demonstrates, and what it does not
 Stated plainly, the Arkhe-Catedral corpus demonstrates that the project's methods — topological
parsing, invariant enumeration, veto analysis, triage scoring — cohere across a legal corpus, an
ontology integration (Chapter 12), and a governance design (Part II). It does not demonstrate external
validation: no constitutional court, standards body, or academic referee has reviewed SASC v5.4α,
and the executive analysis correctly lists external audit among the project's missing assets [31]. The
book includes the corpus for three reasons: it is the historical source of the architecture's governance
ideas; it supplies the worked example that keeps the governance chapters honest; and it illustrates, in a
domain where the corpus is public and checkable [32], exactly the evidentiary posture — every claim
traceable to its text — that the trust layer industrializes.

12.2 Ontologies as invariant sources
 The integration's design argument generalizes beyond neuroscience. An ontology is a
machine-checkable corpus of definitions and relations; a constitution-as-code is the same thing over

ontology entities become invariant sources: a rule such as 'an evidence record asserting a redox
reaction must reference entities that stand in the reaction relation' is checkable against the ontology

the project is entitled to hold, and Chapter 15 returns to its weakest point: whether working scientists

13. The Formal Mathematics Frontier: A Five-Front Program for
the Hodge Conjecture
13.1 The conjecture and its verified status
 The Hodge conjecture is one of the seven Clay Mathematics Institute Millennium Prize
Problems, each carrying a one-million-dollar award [47, 61]. In its modern form, as stated in Deligne's
official problem description: let X be a non-singular complex projective variety; then every Hodge
class on X — a rational cohomology class of degree 2p that has type (p, p) — is a rational linear
combination of the cohomology classes of complex algebraic subvarieties of X [48, 59]. The
conjecture asserts that the topology of algebraic varieties is, in a precise sense, governed by algebra:
the pieces of cohomology that Hodge theory isolates are supposed to be exactly the ones that come
from geometry [46, 59]. Clay's own summary of the state of knowledge is deliberately sober: the


=== PAGINA 28 ===

Page 28
conjecture is known in special cases — for instance when the variety has dimension less than four —
'but in dimension four it is unknown' [47].
 Two foundational caveats frame everything else. First, the conjecture is stated with rational
coefficients because the integral analogue is false: Atiyah and Hirzebruch constructed
counterexamples, and Grothendieck observed that Hodge's original general form fails for essentially
trivial reasons — facts the standard references record [53, 68]. Second, the known territory is
unevenly distributed. The codimension-one case follows from the Lefschetz (1,1) theorem, the
conjecture's only unconditional general-variety instance; surfaces and threefolds are controlled by that
theorem and by duality; and the first genuinely open territory begins with codimension-two cycles on
fourfolds — precisely where Burt Totaro located the frontier in his essay on why one should believe
the conjecture: 'Not much is known about the Hodge conjecture in other cases, starting with 2-cycles
on 4-folds' [52]. This quotation is verifiable in Totaro's 2012 text; the book corrects the internal
program's attribution of similar wording to 2026 (Section 13.3) [69].
13.2 The verified state of the art, 2025-2026
 The survey literature gives the shape of what is known: algebraic cycles on varieties built from
curves and abelian varieties, unirational and uniruled fourfolds, varieties dominated by products of
curves, and the special families where Hodge classes can be computed explicitly [58]. Three
developments anchor the frontier at the time of writing. The first is Markman's 2025 proof of the
Hodge conjecture for abelian fourfolds (arXiv:2502.03415) — a genuine advance on Clay's
'dimension four is unknown' benchmark for the most structured class of fourfolds — which the
community treated as significant enough to sustain dedicated seminar days at Orsay and inclusion in
the Clay Institute's 25th-anniversary Hodge workshop [49, 50, 51]. The second is negative and
equally instructive: Engel and coauthors' matroid construction disproves the integral Hodge
conjecture for abelian varieties in general, sharpening the known boundary between the rational
conjecture, which survives there, and its integral shadow, which does not [54]. The third is
computational: for Fermat varieties — the classical testing ground since Shioda's 1979 proof of the
conjecture for them in many exponents [55] — recent work makes the (2,2) case on Fermat fourfolds
algorithmically checkable when the exponent is coprime to six [56], and integral variants have been
treated with explicit lattice algorithms [57].
 The ambient context is also moving. Terence Tao's September 2026 essay on the conjecture,
written for a broad mathematical audience, signals renewed mainstream attention [67]; AI-assisted
formalization efforts have begun to compress formalization timelines in research mathematics [64,
65]; and dedicated platforms now track the conjecture's statement and known cases in
machine-checkable form [66]. None of this constitutes progress on the conjecture itself — no source
consulted claims a proof or a counterexample for the general case, and Clay's status page still lists it
as open [46, 47]. What it constitutes is a verification infrastructure taking shape around the problem,
which is exactly the development the five-front program is designed to join.

external source at the time of writing; the claim is retained in the program only as an internal target
definition, clearly flagged, and the book's census-related statements are limited to the verified
computational literature [55, 56, 57].
13.4 The five fronts
 The program organizes work into five fronts, illustrated in Figure 2. F1, special cases and
reductions, extends the verified map: Fermat fourfolds under the coprime-to-six algorithm [55, 56],
abelian varieties of CM type, and reductions of variety families modulo primes, each chosen because
a negative answer would itself be publishable mathematics. F2, counterexample computation, inverts
the goal: a systematic search for non-algebraic Hodge classes using obstruction measures over
high-dimensional Hodge lattices, informed by the integral counterexamples [54, 57] as guardrails that
show exactly how algebraicity fails when it fails. F3, formal verification in Lean 4, is the priority
front: statements, known cases, and partial results are to be formalized against mathlib, with an
explicit axiom profile and no accepted artifact containing a sorry [62, 63]. The infrastructure for this
front exists and is maturing — the lean-dojo/problems project formalizes the Millennium problem
statements [62], and 2026 has seen the first AI-compressed formalizations of research-level results,
including a machine-checked Navier-Stokes construction reported in September 2026 [64, 65]. F4, the
Griffiths-Green program, maintains the variational route: normal functions, their singularities, and
period computations on families, following the approach the survey literature describes as the main
alternative to case-by-case algebraicity [58]. Concretely, the front studies normal functions —
sections of the intermediate-Jacobian bundles attached to families of varieties — and hunts their
singularities: the points where a cohomological structure that is algebraic generically fails to
specialize algebraically, which is precisely where the variational form of the conjecture does its work
[58]. The front's acceptance criterion is therefore a computable catalogue of singularities for target
families, a deliverable that retains scientific value even if the conjecture is ultimately resolved by
other routes.

13.5 Why a trust layer belongs in mathematics
 The objection is fair that a governance architecture is an odd neighbor to a Millennium problem.
The book's answer is epistemic rather than mathematical. Mathematics is the discipline with the


=== PAGINA 31 ===

strongest existing verification norm — referee, reproduce, formalize — and yet its frontier practice
still runs on trust: in the author who says the computation checks out, in the arXiv preprint that has not
been re-derived, in the citation chain that no reviewer can follow end to end. Formal verification
collapses one part of that trust [62, 63], and the 2026 auto-formalization results suggest the cost curve
is moving [64, 65]; what remains is the provenance problem — which artifact, authored by whom,
with which dependencies, was accepted by whom, and can the acceptance be re-checked. That is
precisely the problem the WormGraph, anchoring, and quorum were built for, and the Hodge program
is the project's chosen stress test: if the evidence layer holds up under artifacts whose verification is as
strong as mathematics can make it, the weaker verification regimes of engineering practice are within
reach a fortiori.
14. Strategic Assessment: From Pre-Product to Market
14.1 The senior analysis and its priorities
 In September 2026 the project convened an internal, eight-role senior analysis — product,
engineering, security, legal-regulatory, business, marketing, data, and program perspectives — to
assess the Arkhe-Catedral ecosystem as a venture rather than as a methodology [31]. The panel's
conclusion is quoted here because it organizes the chapter: 'The priority is to focus on the MVP, to
constitute corporate governance, and to validate the market with PoCs. The project's evidence
discipline is its greatest asset.' The assessment is candid about the starting point: Arkhe OS is
pre-product — no installable application, no legal entity, no license, no external audit, no revenue
model in operation, no customers, and no dedicated team [31]. The chapters of this book are, in that
light, an argument for why the gap between architecture and product is bridgeable, and the analysis is
the bridge's engineering plan.
14.2 Business model and sequencing
 The recommended model is three-tier: subscription SaaS for teams that want verification as a
service; enterprise licensing for custody, fintech, and AI infrastructure vendors whose compliance
posture requires on-premises evidence; and integration services for the regulated-custody partnerships
the analysis identifies as the highest-trust distribution channel — naming established qualified
custodians as the archetype partners [31]. The analysis explicitly counsels against a native token, on
the argument that a venture whose product is trust evidence should not import token-price volatility
into its own governance; the recommendation aligns with the architecture's separation of authority
from incentive. Sequencing follows Chapter 10: the GGUF MVP for the ML-engineer persona, then
transaction verification for crypto-native teams (Chapter 8), then evidence export for compliance
workflows (Chapter 9), with each stage gated by the metrics of Table 6 rather than by roadmap
optimism [31].

including this book, are part of that strategy: the corpus of verifiable, cited, self-auditing artifacts is

14.4 Competitive posture and the timing risk
 The competitive assessment in the senior analysis is unsentimental about the project's size:
funded security vendors in the transaction-risk space could add evidence features to their existing
distribution [36], wallet vendors could ship their own decoders [1, 38], and payments networks could
extend KYA from identity into full action governance [39, 40]. Against each, the project's stated
differentiation is depth in the layer the others treat as plumbing: an evidence graph with governance
semantics, a constitution that is itself versioned and anchored, and an open verification path that a
skeptical third party can run without trusting anyone [28]. The analysis is equally clear that
differentiation is not a moat by itself — moats here would be network effects of anchored evidence
(history cannot be forked away) and certification positions that take time to earn [31]. The timing risk
is therefore managed the way the architecture manages risk generally: by shipping the smallest
verifiable artifact quickly, publishing its evidence, and letting the market's verification instincts —
sharpened by each new incident — do the acquisition. The book's own wager, stated explicitly so that
it can be checked against later events, is that the category consolidates around evidence governance
within three years, and that open verifiability is the durable selection criterion when it does.
15. Counterarguments, Limitations, and Open Problems
15.1 The strongest objections


=== PAGINA 33 ===
ARKHE: a verifiable trust architecture for autonomous AI agents
Page 33
 Objection 1: verification does not stop social engineering. The sharpest critique of the
independent-decoding answer to Bybit is that it relocates rather than removes the human failure: a
signer under time pressure can ignore a verifier as easily as misread an interface [2, 4]. The response
is honest about scope: the architecture converts silent subversion into loud divergence — display and
decode disagree, the disagreement is itself an anchored event, and organizational policy can make
signature without agreement an invariant violation. That is defense in depth, not prevention, and the
residual risk — a human override of a correct verdict — is acknowledged throughout [7]. What
verification changes is the economics of the post-incident world: reconstruction of 'what did the
signers see' becomes a lookup, and accountability becomes attributable [4, 31].
 Objection 2: transparency logs and privacy are in tension. Append-only, public infrastructure
is an uncomfortable neighbor for data-protection law: erasure rights under the GDPR [22] and the
LGPD [23] sit uneasily with records that cannot be deleted. Chapter 9 stated the design answer —
pseudonymized ingestion, identity maps outside the graph, cryptographic deletion where personal
data must enter at all — but the critique retains force at the level of regulation-as-written: anchored
hashes of pseudonymized records may still be argued to relate to an identifiable person, and
supervisory guidance has not settled the question. The book's position is that this is a research and
policy problem the architecture surfaces rather than solves, and that pretending otherwise would be
the kind of promise the project exists to avoid [28].
 Objection 3: a 4-of-7 quorum is a liveness and centralization hazard. Raising thresholds
trades theft resistance against availability: a 4-of-7 wallet freezes with four lost or unreachable keys,
and the signer set — however large — is a club [29]. The response in ADR-011 is that thresholds are
hypotheses about simultaneous compromise, calibrated against incidents [6, 33]; the objection's
proper force is that calibration must be per-organization, and the constitution-as-code design makes
the threshold a versioned parameter precisely so it can be argued about. The deeper concern — that
any signer club is a concentration of power — is answered only partially, by witness separation and
third-party verifiability; the book does not claim it is dissolved [28].
 Objection 4: verified decoding is not verified intent. A decoder can prove what a transaction
says; it cannot prove what an approver meant. The semantic gap between syntactic correctness and
intentional consent survives the entire architecture, and no client-side artifact closes it. The book's
position is that the gap is managed rather than closed: policy constraints in the kernel (hash-pinned
counterparties, spending caps, quorum-gated anomalies) bound the damage an intention-verification
failure can cause, and the evidence trail makes every such failure a learnable event [28, 31]. Claims to
the contrary — 'clear signing solves blind signing' — are treated as marketing until they specify what
happens when the display layer is itself the adversary [1, 38].
 Objection 5: internal artifacts cannot vouch for themselves. The chapters that lean on internal
documentation — the architecture corpus, the audit reports, the senior analysis [28, 29, 30, 31, 45, 69,
70] — rest on evidence no outsider has checked, and a book that argues for third-party verifiability
must apply that standard to itself. The response is structural rather than rhetorical: every internal
artifact cited here is of a kind designed for external checking (hashes to recompute, sources to re-read,


=== PAGINA 34 ===
ARKHE: a verifiable trust architecture for autonomous AI agents
Page 34
claims dated and stamped), and the two corrections this book displays against the project's own plan
(Section 13.3) are demonstrations of the posture. Nevertheless, the honest verdict is that external audit
remains outstanding, and the project's own assessment lists it among the missing assets [31].
 Objection 6: a trust layer does not do mathematics. Against the Hodge program, the objection
is that evidence infrastructure neither proves theorems nor finds counterexamples, so its role in
Chapter 13 is decorative. The response is the one the chapter argued: verification infrastructure is
where mathematical trust is actually expending effort, from mathlib's machine-checked corpus [62,
63] to the 2026 auto-formalization results [64, 65]; the architecture contributes provenance,
anchoring, and quorum acceptance around machine verification, not instead of it. If formalization
eventually makes the infrastructure redundant for mathematics, that is a success of the trajectory, not a
failure of the book [69].
15.2 Limitations of this study
 Five limitations bound what this book can claim. First, there is no deployment telemetry: every
product claim in Chapter 10 is a design target with a falsification condition, not a measured result
[31]. Second, the crate ecosystem is described from internal documentation [28]; no external security
audit of the implementation has been performed, and implementation-level claims — absence of
unsafe code, determinism — are stated as standards the code is held to, not verified properties. Third,
the counterfactual method of Chapter 7 establishes plausibility, not causation, and the incident
reconstructions rely on published forensic reports that could themselves be incomplete [4, 33, 34].
Fourth, the mathematical chapter relies on secondary sources for much of its state-of-the-art map [58],
and while every load-bearing claim is cited, the authors are not algebraic geometers; the corrections of
Section 13.3 were checked against primary sources [52, 60], but the chapter should be read as an
engineer's verified map, not a mathematician's. Fifth, all web sources were retrieved on 15 September
2026; a fast-moving field — particularly KYA-related policy [39, 40] — may have moved since.
15.3 Open problems
 The book closes its critical account with the problems it could not solve. A formal specification
of the kernel of containment — machine-checked, not merely documented — is the architecture's
most valuable next artifact and its most demanding. Interoperation between Arkhe evidence and
emerging agent-identity frameworks such as KYA requires a credential profile neither ecosystem yet
defines [39, 40]. GDPR-compatible anchoring needs either regulatory clarification or a cryptographic
construction — key-erasable commitments with auditor-visible revocation — that does not yet exist
in deployed form. Quorum acceptance of scientific claims raises a normative question the project has
engineered around but not answered: who, legitimately, is the '4' in a 4-of-7 that counts a proof as
accepted [29, 69]? And the census vision of Chapter 13 — every Hodge class computation for a
family, hashed, signed, and anchored — awaits both the computational campaign and the community
governance to make it worth trusting [56, 57, 69]. None of these is a reason to stop; all of them are
reasons the architecture is falsifiable.

16. Conclusion and Outlook
 This book set out to examine one thesis: that trust in autonomous systems can be converted from
promise to proof, and that the conversion is an architectural act. The Arkhe design realizes the thesis
in four load-bearing elements — a kernel of containment that bounds agents regardless of their
claims, attestation that binds artifacts to identities, a WormGraph that remembers without rewriting,
and a constitution that governs its own change [28]. The incident record supplies the empirical
pressure: Bybit demonstrated the interface as the modern attack surface, StablR the arithmetic of
thresholds, SUPERFORTUNE the fragility of visual identity [2, 6, 34]. The regulatory landscape
supplies the demand side, from the AI Act's logging mandate to the LGPD's rights apparatus and
MiCA's operational expectations [17, 20, 23]. And the Hodge program supplies the limiting case: if
the evidence layer disciplines claims whose verification is as strong as mathematics can make it, the
weaker regimes of engineering practice lie within reach [46, 69].
 The honest summary of the project's position is asymmetric. On the side of assets: a coherent
architecture whose every layer answers a documented failure mode; an implemented crate ecosystem
with disciplined boundaries; a governance pattern with incident-calibrated defaults; a regulatory map
that is unusually specific for a pre-product project; and a discipline of evidence — citations, stamps,
displayed corrections — that this book has tried to model rather than merely describe. On the side of
debts: no legal entity, no external audit, no customers, no measured adoption, and a market that larger
players are now naming on their own schedules [31, 39]. The asymmetry is not accidental; it is what a
design-science project looks like at the moment its verification burden shifts from architecture to
world.
 The roadmap that follows is the one the project's own analysis prescribes, reordered by this
book's findings [31]. In the next two quarters: ship the GGUF MVP with its metrics instrumented,
constitute the legal entity with the constitution-as-code pattern applied to itself, and open the crate
ecosystem to external audit — the cheapest available purchase of credibility. In the following four:
transaction verification for crypto-native teams in production, evidence export aligned to the AI Act's
logging obligations for one design partner, and the first anchored artifacts of the Hodge program's
front F3, however small [17, 62, 69]. Thereafter: certification under ISO/IEC 42001, integration with
KYA credential frameworks as they stabilize, and the census infrastructure if — and only if — the
governance question of who counts a proof is answered in public [16, 39, 69].
 The outlook is conditional, and the condition is the project's own principle. A trust layer earns
adoption only by being verifiable at every altitude — the signer's screen, the engineer's pipeline, the
auditor's bench, the referee's desk — and by failing loudly, in public, with evidence, when it fails at
all. The claim this book leaves standing is correspondingly narrow and, by design, checkable: that the
architecture presented here is a coherent, implementable, and falsifiable answer to a failure mode the
world has now demonstrated at the scale of billions of dollars, and that the distance between that
answer and a deployed one is no longer architectural. It is organizational, financial, and — as always
in trust — personal. Prove, don't promise.
